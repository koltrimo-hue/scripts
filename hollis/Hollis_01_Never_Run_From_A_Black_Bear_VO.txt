A spring Sunday on the Little River, up past the old Elkmont cabins. The water is high and loud, the color of strong tea where it slides over the rocks. A man stands in the river with a fly rod, working a pool. Up on the trail, a woman sits a while, then walks on a little ways by herself. The woods are full of new green. Nobody is worried about anything. About an hour later, the man reels in his line and goes looking for her. He finds her pack lying in the trail. And standing in the laurel just off the path, not running, not even startled, are two black bears that will not move.

That was the twenty-first of May, in the year two thousand, in the Great Smoky Mountains National Park. And what happened on that trail had never happened before in the whole life of the park. Not once. The park had been open to the public for better than sixty years by then. Millions of people had walked those trails. Millions of people had seen black bears, photographed black bears, fed black bears when they should not have, chased black bears out of their campsites with pots and pans. And in all that time, nobody had ever been killed by one inside the park. Then, on a quiet Sunday afternoon, a woman from just over the ridge in Cosby, Tennessee, went for a walk along one of the gentlest trails in the Smokies, and she did not come back.

I am going to walk you through it the way it actually happened, as close as the records let us get. I am going to tell you what the bears were, and what they were not. I am going to tell you what the old mountain people believed about bears, which is a different thing entirely, and I will mark it plain when we cross over from one to the other. And by the end, I am going to answer the one question everybody asks when they hear this story, which is the question of what you are supposed to do. Because the old advice that most of us grew up with is wrong about half the time, and the half where it is wrong is the half that matters. Stay with me to the end and you will have it, the rule in its right order, and the reason behind it.

So let us start with the place, because the place is part of it.

Elkmont sits on the Tennessee side of the park, up the Little River from Gatlinburg and Townsend. A long time ago it was a logging town, and after the logging it was a summer place, a cluster of cabins where well-off families from Knoxville came up to sit on porches and get out of the heat. When the park came, those cabins slowly emptied out. Some of them are still standing. Some of them have been fixed up, and some of them are just shells with the windows gone and the porches sagging. If you have ever walked through there in the evening, you know it has a feeling to it. Not a bad feeling exactly. Just the feeling of a place people used to be.

The Little River Trail starts near there and follows the river upstream. It is an old railroad grade, from the logging days, which means it is wide and it is easy. There is no hard climbing on it for the first several miles. It is the kind of trail you take your grandmother on. It is the kind of trail you take a fly rod on, because the river runs right alongside it, pool after pool, with trout in it. That is why Glenda Bradley was there that day, or at least why the man she was with was there.

Her name was Glenda Ann Bradley. She was fifty years old. She lived in Cosby, which is a small place on the north edge of the park, the kind of community where people have lived alongside these mountains for generations. She was not a tourist. She was not somebody who had never seen a bear. She was a mountain woman walking in mountains she knew, and I want you to keep that in mind, because a lot of people, when they first hear this story, want to believe she must have done something foolish. The records do not show anything like that.

She had come up that Sunday with her former husband, a man named Ralph Hill. The two of them were still on friendly terms, and they had walked up the Little River Trail together. About two miles up from the trailhead, near where a side trail called the Goshen Prong comes in, they split up for a while. He went down to the river to fish. She stayed up near the trail. That is not an unusual thing to do. People do it every day of every summer up there. One person fishes, one person sits with a book or walks a little ways, and they meet back up.

He fished for something like an hour. When he came back up to find her, she was not where he had left her. He found her backpack in the trail. And then, a little way off the trail, he found her, and he found what was with her.

I am going to stop here for a moment, because I want to give you the part that is plain history before we go any further, and I want you to hear it without any decoration.

The records show that two bears were found at the body. One was an adult female. One was her yearling, which is a cub born the year before, old enough to be getting around on its own but still traveling with its mother. Rangers later weighed them. The female was a hundred and twelve pounds. The yearling was about forty pounds. Now, I want you to sit with that number for a second. A hundred and twelve pounds. That is not a big bear. That is smaller than a lot of grown men. People hear the words bear attack and they picture something rearing up seven feet tall, something out of a movie. This was a lean, ordinary, middle-sized Smoky Mountain sow, the kind of bear you might see crossing the road at Cades Cove from your car window and say, oh, look at that, isn't she pretty.

Ralph Hill tried to drive them off. Other hikers who came along tried too. The newspapers of the day reported that the bears would not leave. They did not run when people shouted. They did not run when people came at them. They stayed with the body, and they acted aggressive toward anyone who came close. That detail is the one that tells the people who study bears what kind of attack this was, and we will come back to it, because it is the key to the whole thing.

Park rangers came up the trail. They shot both bears there at the scene. The investigation that followed found that Glenda Bradley had been killed by the bears, and that the bears had been feeding on her and had tried to cover the body with leaves and dirt, which is a thing bears do with a carcass they intend to come back to. They call it caching. Some reports in the weeks after raised the possibility that a third bear may have been involved, and accounts differ on that point. What is not in dispute is the rest of it. She died on the trail. Two bears were killed afterward. And the first person ever to die from a black bear attack in the Great Smoky Mountains National Park was a fifty-year-old woman from Cosby who had come up to sit by the river on a Sunday.

Now, I said I would raise the skeptic's objection before you had to, so let me do it now.

A lot of people hear this story and they say, well, black bears do not do that. Black bears are timid. Black bears run from people. Something must have been wrong. She must have had food. She must have gotten between the mother and the cub. She must have surprised them. She must have done something.

And it is a fair thing to wonder, because most of the time, all of that is true. Black bears are timid. The great majority of the time a black bear in these mountains hears a person coming and gets out of the way, and you never even know it was there. When black bears do hurt people, it is usually a defensive thing. A person comes around a bend and surprises one at close range. A person gets between a sow and her small cubs. A person walks into a bear that is feeding and does not want to give up its food. Those attacks tend to be short. The bear knocks you down, maybe bites, maybe swats, and then it leaves, because all it wanted was for you to stop being a threat.

But that is not what the records describe on the Little River. The yearling was not a tiny spring cub that needed protecting. The people who investigated did not find that she had been carrying food that drew the bears in. And the bears did not leave. They stayed with her, they fed, they cached, and they came at people who tried to drive them off. The people who study these things have a word for that kind of behavior, and the word is predatory. It means the bear was not defending itself from a threat. It means the bear was treating a person as food.

That is very, very rare with black bears. That is what makes it so hard to hear. But it does happen, and it has happened since, and the fact that it is rare is exactly the reason most people do not know what to do when it does.

So hold that word in your mind. Predatory versus defensive. Because the whole answer to the question of what you are supposed to do comes down to which one you are looking at. And before I get to the answer, I want to tell you about the bears themselves, because the Smokies are not just any place when it comes to black bears.

The Great Smoky Mountains have one of the densest populations of black bears anywhere in the eastern United States. The park has put the number at somewhere around nineteen hundred bears in recent years, which works out to something like two bears for every square mile of park. That is a lot of bears. If you have spent any time up there, you have walked past bears you never saw. They were up in a tree, or bedded down in a laurel thicket, or turned around and walked off the other way the second they heard your boots.

It was not always like that. In the old days, before the park, bears were hunted hard in these mountains. A bear meant meat for the winter, and grease for cooking, and a hide. Bear hunting was serious business, and it was a big part of mountain life. There is a breed of hound that came out of these hills specifically for it. The Plott hound. The family that the dogs are named for brought the line over from Germany in the seventeen hundreds and settled in western North Carolina, and they bred those dogs for generations to run bears and hold them at bay. It is the state dog of North Carolina today. Every family up here that hunted bears had an opinion about the Plott hound, and most of them were good ones.

So for a long time, bears in these mountains learned to be afraid of people, because people meant dogs and guns. When the park came in, and hunting stopped inside the park, the bears slowly came back. And the bears that grew up after that, generation after generation, grew up with people who did not hunt them. People who stopped their cars to take pictures. People who, God help them, held out sandwiches.

Now here is where the old rangers' saying comes in, and I am sure you have heard it. A fed bear is a dead bear. What it means is this. A bear that learns people have food stops being afraid of people. It starts coming into campsites, tearing into coolers, walking up to picnic tables. And sooner or later, a bear like that does something that gets it killed, because the park cannot leave a bear around that has lost its fear of human beings. Nobody knows for certain what those two bears on the Little River had learned in their lives, or from whom. Some reports said the bears had been acting aggressive toward people in the days before. Accounts differ on how much the park knew. But the general lesson does not depend on the details of that one sow. The more a bear is around people without consequence, the less a person looks like something to run from.

And that brings me to the part of this story that is not history at all. Because before there was a park, and before there were rangers, and before anybody wrote a word down about Glenda Bradley, the people of these mountains had their own understanding of bears. And it was a very different understanding than ours.

The Cherokee were here first, and the Cherokee had stories about bears that go back further than anybody can count. A man named James Mooney, who worked for the Smithsonian, went among the Eastern Cherokee in the late eighteen hundreds and wrote down a great many of their stories, and one of them is about where bears come from. Now this is a story, and I am telling it to you as a story. The story goes that there was once a clan of the Cherokee people, and in that clan there was a boy who went off into the mountains every day and came back with long brown hair beginning to grow all over his body. His family asked him why. He told them that there was plenty to eat in the woods, better than at home, and that he was going to stay there. And the whole clan, the story goes, decided to go with him. They fasted, and they went into the forest, and as they went, they turned into bears.

And before they left, the story says, they told the people who stayed behind something I have always thought was strange and beautiful. They said, when you are hungry, come into the woods and call us, and we will come and give you our flesh. You need not be afraid to kill us, for we will live always. And the Cherokee had songs, the story goes, that hunters sang to call the bears to them. Mooney wrote those songs down too.

So in the oldest way of looking at it, the bear was a kind of relative. A people who had gone into the woods. You did not hate the bear and you did not fear the bear the way you fear a monster. You respected it, and you asked its pardon, and you understood that it was something close to a person.

The white settlers who came into these mountains later brought their own feelings about bears, and those were more practical. Every family I grew up hearing about had a bear story. A bear got into the hog pen. A bear came to the spring house. A bear followed somebody home from the berry patch. Folks say a bear will not cross running water when it is being chased, though I could not tell you where that came from, and nobody can prove it, and I would not stake my life on it. Folks say if you meet a bear on a narrow path, you should speak to it, low and steady, like you would speak to a cow you did not want to spook. Folks say a bear can smell a woman's perfume from a mile off and a man's fear from further than that. The story goes that bears will stand up on two legs to get a better look at you, and that is actually true, and it is not a threat. It is curiosity. A bear stands up because it wants to see you and smell you better. It is when a bear comes down onto all four feet and starts walking toward you with its head low that you ought to start paying attention.

I mention those old sayings because some of them are wise, and some of them are nonsense, and the trouble is that when you are standing on a trail with your heart going like a hammer, you cannot always remember which is which. That is why the modern rules matter. They were not written by somebody guessing. They were written by people who have studied every bad bear encounter on record and asked the same question we are asking. What worked, and what did not.

So let me give you those rules now, because this is the promise I made you at the start, and I mean to keep it.

The first rule is the one in the title of this story, and it is the oldest and the truest. Never run from a black bear. Not ever. Not from a black bear, not from a grizzly, not from any bear. And here is why. A bear can run faster than you can. A grown black bear can run something like thirty miles an hour over short ground, uphill or downhill, through brush you would trip over. You cannot outrun it. Nobody can. And when you run, you do something worse than just failing to get away. You tell the bear you are prey. Every animal that hunts has the same switch inside it. Something runs, you chase it. A dog has it. A cat has it. A bear has it. When you turn your back and run, you are flipping that switch, and you are turning an encounter that might have ended with the bear walking off into the trees into a chase.

So you do not run. You stand your ground. You face the bear. You make yourself look as big as you can, put your arms up, open your jacket if you have one on. You talk to it in a loud, firm voice. Not a scream. A voice. Hey bear. Go on now. Get out of here. And you back away slowly, never turning your back on it, giving it room to leave. If you are with other people, you bunch up together, so you look like one big animal instead of several small ones. If you have bear spray, you get it out, you take the safety off, and you hold it ready.

The great majority of the time, that is the end of it. The bear decides you are not worth the trouble and it leaves.

The second rule is the one that most people get wrong, and it is the one that matters in a story like this one. You may have heard that if a bear attacks, you should play dead. Lie down, cover your neck, go limp, and wait for it to lose interest. And that advice is not exactly wrong. But it is only right for one kind of bear and one kind of attack. It is advice for a grizzly bear that is defending itself or its cubs. Out west, a grizzly that charges you is very often trying to neutralize a threat, and if you stop being a threat by playing dead, it may leave.

But a black bear that attacks you, in these mountains, is a different matter. The guidance from the park service is plain on this. If a black bear attacks you, fight back. Do not play dead. Fight with everything you have. Use rocks, sticks, your fists, your feet, your trekking poles, your pack. Aim for the face and the nose. And this is doubly true if the bear is stalking you, following you, or if it attacks you when you have done nothing to surprise it. Because that is what a predatory bear looks like. A defensive bear wants you to go away. A predatory bear wants you to lie still. And if you play dead for a bear that is looking at you as food, you are doing exactly what it wants.

I am not going to stand here and tell you that fighting back would have saved Glenda Bradley. Nobody can say that, and it would be a cruel and foolish thing to claim. We do not know what she saw, or whether she had any warning at all. The records do not tell us that. What the records do tell us is what kind of attack it was, and that is the reason the park service, and every bear biologist I have ever heard speak on it, gives the advice the way they do. Black bear, attacks you, fight back.

The third rule is the one that keeps you from ever needing the first two. Do not let a bear get comfortable around you. Keep your distance. In the Smokies, it is against the regulations to willfully get within fifty yards of a bear, or any distance that makes it change what it is doing. That is half a football field. If you are close enough to get a good picture with your phone, you are almost certainly too close. Keep your food put away. Use the bear cables at the backcountry campsites. Never leave a pack sitting on the ground while you wander off to look at something. Never feed a bear, never leave scraps for a bear, never let a bear take your food without making a fuss about it. Every time a bear gets a free meal from a person, it gets a little closer to being the kind of bear that does not move when you holler at it.

And carry bear spray. I know a lot of people back east feel like it is something only folks out in Montana need to bother with. But it works on black bears too, and it is a whole lot easier to use than a stick.

Now, I wish I could tell you that the Little River was the only time. It was the first time, and for a long while it was the only time. But it was not the last.

In September of twenty twenty, a man from Ohio was found dead at a backcountry campsite near Hazel Creek, on the North Carolina side of the park, with a bear at his body. At first there was a question about whether the bear had killed him or only found him after he was already gone. The park later said that the evidence pointed to the bear as the cause of death. The bear was killed. And just like that, there were two. Two in all that time, in a park that gets more visitors than any other national park in the country. That is a very small number. It ought to make you feel safer, not more afraid. But it ought to make you feel safer in the right way, the way a man feels safer when he knows how to swim. Not the way a man feels safe because he has never been in deep water.

There are other cases too, outside the park and outside these mountains, scattered across the years and across the map. And in the predatory ones, when people sit down and study them afterward, the same pattern turns up. A bear that did not leave. A bear that came back. A bear that was not defending anything at all.

So let me come back, at the end, to where we started. To the Little River on a spring Sunday, with the water high and loud, and a man standing in a pool with a fly rod, and a woman walking on a little ways by herself.

I think about that trail more than I would like to. Because nothing about it was dangerous. Nothing about that day was unusual. It is the easiest trail in that part of the park. It was broad daylight. There were other people around. She was not doing anything that you and I would not do, that we have not done a hundred times, sitting by a river and letting somebody else fish. And I think that is the thing that stays with people about this story, more than the bears themselves. That the danger was not in some lonesome place at the end of the world. It was right there in the pretty part, the part you take pictures of.

The Cherokee said the bears were people who walked into the woods and did not come back. And I have always found something in that old story that I cannot quite put down. The idea that the bear and the person are closer to each other than we like to think. That we are both just animals in the same green country, looking for something to eat, and most of the time we pass each other by and never know it.

The park put up signs after that, and the rangers talked about it for years. And people still walk the Little River Trail every day of the summer, with fly rods and picnic baskets and little children, and the bears still watch them from the laurel. Most of those bears will never trouble anybody. The rule is there for the one that will. Stand your ground. Make yourself big. Back away slow. And if a black bear comes at you anyway, do not lie down. Fight.

That is the whole of the rule, in the order it goes. And now you have it.
