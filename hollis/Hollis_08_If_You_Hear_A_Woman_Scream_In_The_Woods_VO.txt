A hollow in the mountains, long after dark. A farmhouse sits at the head of it with one lamp burning in the kitchen window. The dogs are under the porch and will not come out. Somewhere up on the ridge behind the house, a sound comes down through the trees. It is a woman screaming. Long, high, and terrible, like somebody calling for help. Then it stops. The man of the house reaches for his coat and his lantern. And his wife puts her hand flat on the door and holds it shut, and tells him in a voice he has never heard her use before that he is not going out there. Not tonight. Not for that.

That scene is not one night in one place. It is a story that was told in every corner of these mountains, from north Georgia up to Pennsylvania, for two hundred years and more. Every family I ever knew up here had some version of it. A scream in the woods at night, like a woman, or like a baby crying. And the old people always said the same thing about it. Do not go out there. Because it is not a woman. It is a painter.

That is how the old mountain people said it. Painter. They meant panther. The big cat. The mountain lion, the cougar, the catamount, the puma, all names for the same animal. It once lived all through these mountains, and it was the thing that the people of the hollows feared most in the dark. And then, as far as anybody can prove, it disappeared.

That animal has more names than just about any other creature in America, and that tells you something about how widely it once roamed, and how many different people had to find a word for it. Painter is the old mountain way of saying panther, the way a mountain man might say feller for fellow or holler for hollow. Up north in New England, they called it catamount, which comes from the old words cat of the mountain, and up there it is still the name of a good many places, and sports teams, and hunting camps. Out west they say mountain lion. The scientists say cougar, or puma. Down in Florida they say panther. Some folks in the old days called it the ghost cat, because it was so seldom seen, even where it was common. You could live your whole life in its country and only ever know it by its tracks, and the deer it left behind, and the sound in the night. Every one of those names belongs to the same big, tawny, long-tailed cat. And in these mountains, for two hundred years, the name that put children to bed early was painter.

So I am going to tell you three things. I am going to tell you the old stories the way they were told, and I will mark them as stories. I am going to tell you what the records actually show about the big cats in the eastern mountains, which is a stranger tale than most people know, and which reaches right up into the last few years. And I am going to answer the question that every one of you is asking. If the painter is gone, then what is screaming in the woods at night? And what should you do when you hear it? Stay with me to the end, and I will tell you.

Let us start with the old stories, because they came first.

I grew up hearing painter tales, and so did a great many of you, if your people came out of these hills. The story goes that the painter would follow a person through the woods at night. Never close enough to see. Just behind. You would hear a stick break, and stop, and the woods would go quiet. And you would walk on, and hear it again. Folks say a painter could smell meat on a person from a long way off, and that if you were carrying fresh meat home from a hog killing, or from the store, you would have company on the road before you got home.

There is one story in particular that turns up all over the mountains, with a different name and a different hollow every time it is told. The story goes that a woman was walking home alone through the woods at dusk, carrying something, a basket of meat, or a baby, or a bundle of washing, and she heard the painter scream behind her, close. She knew she could not outrun it. So she began to drop things. First the meat, a piece at a time, and the painter would stop to eat it, and she would gain a little ground. Then, when the meat ran out, she began to drop her clothes, her shawl, her apron, her bonnet, one piece at a time. And the painter would stop at every one and sniff it and tear at it, because it smelled of her, and every time it stopped, she ran a little further. And the story goes that she burst through her own door at the very last moment, with the painter so close behind her that its claws struck the wood as she slammed it. Some tellers have the menfolk go out the next morning and find the claw marks in the door. Nobody can prove any of it. But every family seemed to know somebody whose grandmother it happened to.

And then there were the screams. Folks say the painter screams like a woman in pain, or like a woman calling for help, and that it does it on purpose, to draw a person out of the house. The story goes that the painter would scream from the edge of the woods to lure a man out with his lantern, and that the ones who went out to see what woman was in trouble did not always come back. Folks say a painter would get up on the roof of a cabin and walk across it at night, and scream down the chimney, and that the family would sit up all night with the fire built high, because the old people said a painter would not come through fire. Folks say a painter would carry off a baby from a cradle on the porch. Every one of those stories is folklore. I am telling them as folklore. Some of them are surely nonsense, and some of them may have a grain of something real buried in them, and after two hundred years of telling, nobody alive can sort out which is which.

But I want you to notice what those stories all have in common. In every one of them, the scream is a trick. It sounds like a person who needs help. And the danger is in going out to help. Hold that thought, because it is going to come back at the end.

Now let me give you the plain history.

The cougar is real, and it did once live all through the eastern mountains. When the first European settlers came into the Appalachians, the big cats were here, in the forests from the Carolinas up into New England and Canada. They hunted deer, mostly, which is what cougars eat wherever they live. And the settlers, as they cleared the land and brought in their cattle and their sheep and their hogs, hunted the cougars hard. There were bounties paid on them in a good many places. A man could collect money from the county for a painter's scalp. At the same time, the deer that the cats lived on were being hunted down to almost nothing, all across the East, and the forests were being cut. Between the guns, the traps, the bounties, and the loss of the deer and the woods, the eastern cougar was driven out of one state after another.

The records show that the last confirmed cougar in Pennsylvania was killed in eighteen seventy-four, in Berks County. Across most of the southern mountains, nobody can say for certain when the last one died, because nobody was keeping careful track. The last one that wildlife officials have accepted as a verified eastern cougar was a cat trapped in Maine, in Somerset County, in nineteen thirty-eight.

And I want you to understand how complete that emptying of the woods was, because it was not only the cats. By the early nineteen hundreds, the white-tailed deer itself had been hunted out of a great part of the eastern mountains. There were whole counties in the southern Appalachians where a man could go his entire life without seeing a deer. The elk were gone. The wolves were gone. The beaver were nearly gone. The great forests had been cut over by the timber companies, ridge after ridge, and burned behind them. The woods that the painter had lived in, the deep, dark, old woods full of deer, simply did not exist anymore. A cougar needs a great deal of room and a great many deer. When both of those were gone, the cat had nothing left to live on.

Then, over the course of the last century, things slowly turned around. The cut-over mountains grew back into forest. The states brought deer back, trapping them in places where a few were left and turning them loose in places where there were none, and protecting them with hunting seasons and game wardens. And the deer did what deer do. Today there are more white-tailed deer in a great many parts of the East than there were when the first settlers came. Which means that, for the first time in a hundred years and more, the eastern mountains have plenty of what a cougar needs to eat. Keep that in mind, because it matters for what comes next.

After that, as far as the records go, there was nothing.

In the nineteen seventies, the government listed the eastern cougar as an endangered species. It was one of the early animals put on that list. But there was a problem with protecting it. Nobody could find one. For decades, wildlife biologists looked for evidence of eastern cougars in the mountains. They looked for tracks, for kills, for hair, for scat, for road-killed animals, for clear photographs. And they did not find any that held up.

So in two thousand eleven, the United States Fish and Wildlife Service came to a conclusion. They said that the eastern cougar was extinct. That it had likely been gone since the nineteen thirties. And in January of two thousand eighteen, the government made it official, and took the eastern cougar off the endangered species list, for the simple reason that there was nothing left to protect.

Now, here is where the skeptic comes in, but from an unusual direction. Because in this story, the skeptics are the people on the other side.

Ask almost anybody who lives in the southern mountains, and especially the old people, and they will tell you flat out that the government is wrong. That the painter is still out there. People in these mountains have been reporting big cats for as long as I have been alive. Long tails crossing the road in the headlights. Tracks in the mud, too big for a bobcat. Screams in the night. A great many of those people are hunters, and farmers, and folks who have spent their whole lives in the woods and know what they are looking at. And they do not take kindly to being told by somebody in an office that they did not see what they saw.

So which is it? Well, let me give you the honest answer, which has more than one part to it.

Part of it is that a lot of what people see is something else. The wildlife people have said for years that a great many big cat sightings in the East turn out, when they can be checked, to be bobcats, or house cats seen at a distance, or dogs, or even deer seen from a strange angle in bad light. People also see black panthers. That is one of the most common reports in the southern mountains, a big, long, black cat. But here is a strange fact. There has never been a confirmed black cougar anywhere in North America. Not one. Not a hide, not a body, not a photograph that held up. Cougars are tan. So whatever the black panthers are that people see, they are not cougars, or at least nobody has ever proven one.

And the wildlife people make one more point, and it is a hard one to argue with. Today, there are trail cameras everywhere in the eastern woods. Hunters hang them on trees by the hundreds of thousands to watch their deer. Every one of those cameras takes a picture of anything that walks by, day and night, and they have photographed bears, and bobcats, and coyotes, and every other animal in these mountains, over and over again. And there are millions of cars on the mountain roads every night, and cougars, like deer, get hit by cars. If there were a breeding population of cougars living in the southern Appalachians, the biologists say, we would have them on camera all the time, and we would be finding them dead on the highways. And we do not. That is the skeptic's strongest card, and he is right to play it.

There is one place in the East where wild cougars do still live, and live for certain, and I should mention it, because it is part of this story. Way down at the bottom of the country, in the swamps and pine flats of south Florida, there is a small population of cougars that has hung on all this time. They are called the Florida panther. They nearly disappeared too, down to just a few dozen animals at one point, and they are still endangered, and a great many of them still die on the roads every year. But they are there, and they are breeding, and they are the last wild descendants of the big cats that once ranged all across the eastern half of this country. The painter of the old Appalachian stories was a cousin to those cats.

But here is the other part of the honest answer. And it is the part that would have made the old mountain people smile.

In June of two thousand eleven, the very same year the government concluded the eastern cougar was extinct, a mountain lion was hit and killed by an SUV on the Wilbur Cross Parkway, in Milford, Connecticut. Connecticut. A suburb, not far from New York City. It was a male, about a hundred and forty pounds. At first the officials thought it must be somebody's escaped pet, because there had not been a wild mountain lion seen in Connecticut in more than a hundred years. But then they ran its DNA. And the DNA told an astonishing story. That cat was a wild cougar from the Black Hills of South Dakota. And the scientists matched it to a cat that had been tracked through Minnesota and Wisconsin a year and a half before. That young male had walked something like fifteen hundred miles or more, east across half the continent, across rivers and highways and farm country and suburbs, looking for a territory of his own. Looking, maybe, for a female. And he made it almost to the Atlantic Ocean before a car on a parkway in Connecticut stopped him.

And then it happened again, closer to home. In the fall of two thousand fifteen, the Tennessee Wildlife Resources Agency confirmed a cougar in west Tennessee. A hunter turned in a sample of hair, and the DNA came back as a female cougar, and again, the genetics pointed to the Black Hills of South Dakota. Over the following year, trail cameras caught a cougar in several places across that part of Tennessee. The state's biologists said they verified around ten sightings between the fall of two thousand fifteen and the fall of two thousand sixteen, and they believed it was most likely one cat, wandering. The first confirmed cougar in Tennessee in about a hundred years.

So the honest truth is this. The old eastern cougar, the painter of the stories, is very likely gone, and has been gone a long time. But cougars from the West are on the move. The populations out in the Dakotas and the Rockies have been growing, and the young males especially roam enormous distances. Some of the scientists will tell you it is only a matter of time before more of them reach the eastern mountains for good. Some of the scientists will also tell you that the eastern cougar was never really a separate kind of animal at all, just the same cougar that lives out west, and that when the western ones come back east, they are simply coming home. Historians and biologists argue about that. But either way, the old people who said the painter would be back may yet turn out to be right.

So now we come to the question I promised you. If the painter is gone from most of these mountains, then what is it that people hear screaming in the woods at night? Because they do hear it. A great many of you have heard it. And it sounds exactly the way the old stories say. Like a woman. Like somebody in terrible trouble.

The answer is that the eastern woods are full of animals that scream. And the one that most often fools people is a fox.

The red fox, and especially the female in the wintertime, during mating season, lets out a scream that will raise every hair on your body. It is a long, high, ragged shriek, and it sounds so much like a woman screaming that people have called the police about it. If you have never heard it, I would advise you to look up a recording some evening, with the lights on. Bobcats scream and yowl too, especially when they are fighting or mating, and they are all through these mountains. The barred owl, a big gray owl of the bottomlands, has a whole range of calls, and some of them sound like a crowd of people laughing and shrieking all at once. Screech owls make a sound like a whinny or a sob. Even rabbits, when something catches them, let out a scream that sounds very much like a child.

So the painter of the old stories may often have been a fox on the ridge, or a bobcat, or an owl. And I think that is a fine thing to know. It takes some of the terror out of a dark night.

But here is why I am still telling you the old rule. The rule in the title of this story. If you hear a woman scream in the woods at night, do not go looking.

Because the old people were not wrong about the danger. They just had the wrong animal. The danger in the woods at night was never mostly the painter. The danger was going out into the dark. A man who goes running up a dark ridge at night with a lantern, into steep rough country, after a sound, is a man who can fall and break a leg, or go off a ledge, or get lost, or come face to face with whatever actually is out there, whether that is a cougar or a bear or a mother bobcat with kittens. Every one of those old stories was built the same way. A sound that sounds like somebody needs help. And the trouble comes from going out after it.

So if you are camping, or out on your porch, and you hear something scream in the woods, you stay put. You stay by the fire, the way the old families did. You keep your light handy. You make some noise of your own, so whatever is out there knows a person is here. You keep your dogs and your children close. And in the morning, in the light, if you want to go and look for tracks, then you go.

And if it ever is a real cougar, and you are one of the very few people in the eastern mountains who ever meets one face to face, the rule is the same as for any big animal. Do not run. Running is what prey does, and a cat is built to chase. Stand tall. Make yourself as big as you can. Pick up any small children so they do not run. Talk loud and firm, and back away slowly, facing it. And if it attacks, you fight back with everything you have, the same as you would with a black bear. People have fought off cougars with sticks and rocks and their bare hands.

I should say, too, so that nobody goes to bed afraid tonight, that cougar attacks on people are very, very rare, even out West, where there are many thousands of the cats. Over more than a century, the number of people killed by cougars in the United States and Canada is small enough that the researchers can list every one by name. A cougar is a shy animal by nature. It hunts by stealth, it wants nothing to do with people, and most folks who live in cougar country their whole lives never once lay eyes on one. Most of the people who have been attacked were alone, or were small children, or were running, or bent over, which can make a person look to a cat like something smaller than a person. That is why the advice is to stand up tall, keep children close, and never run.

Now, if a real person is screaming for help in the woods, that is a different matter, and you know it is. Then you call for help, you call nine one one if you can, and you go with a light and with somebody else beside you, and you go carefully. But a scream in the dark that comes once, and stops, and comes again from somewhere else on the ridge, is very likely the woods talking to itself.

I think about those old families, sometimes, sitting up all night in a cabin at the head of a hollow, with the fire built up high and the children asleep in the back room, listening to something scream on the ridge. No electric light. No phone. No way of knowing what it was. Just the old stories, and the fire, and a wife with her hand flat on the door. I do not think they were foolish people. I think they were people who had learned, the hard way, generation after generation, that the woods at night do not belong to us. That the sound that seems to call you out into the dark is not always what it seems to be. That the safest thing in the world, on a night like that, is to stay inside the circle of the light and wait for morning.

The painter is mostly gone from these mountains now. The screams are not. And every so often, a long, lean, tawny cat comes walking out of the West, across a thousand miles of farm country and highway, and reminds us that the old stories never really ended. They are just waiting for the next chapter.
