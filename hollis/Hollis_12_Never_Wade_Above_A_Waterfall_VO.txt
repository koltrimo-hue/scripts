That place is called Rainbow Falls. It is on the Horsepasture River, in the mountains of western North Carolina, in Transylvania County. And Transylvania County has a nickname, one they put on the signs and the brochures and the coffee mugs. The Land of Waterfalls. There are said to be hundreds of waterfalls in that one county, more than almost anywhere in the eastern United States. People come from all over the country to see them. And every summer, a few of those people do not go home.

There is a reason that one county has so many waterfalls, and it is worth knowing, because it is part of the danger. Running along the southern edge of those mountains, where North Carolina meets South Carolina, is a great step in the land that the geologists call the Blue Ridge Escarpment. On one side, up top, is the high mountain country. On the other side, down below, is the rolling foothill country of the Piedmont. And in between, the land drops off by a couple of thousand feet in just a few miles. Every river that comes off the high country has to get down that step. The Horsepasture, the Toxaway, the Thompson, the Whitewater, and a dozen more. They do it by falling. Over cliff after cliff, ledge after ledge, through gorges so steep and so wild that some of them were barely explored until modern times. Whitewater Falls, a few miles from Rainbow, is often called the highest waterfall east of the Rocky Mountains. The whole edge of those mountains is a staircase of falling water.

And here is the part that matters for this story. Above almost every one of those falls, the river comes across the high country in a calm, gentle stretch, with sunny rock shelves and shallow pools, before it reaches the edge. Up top, it looks like a creek. It looks like a place to cool your feet. The waterfall is just around the bend, or just over the lip, and from the water you often cannot even see how far down it goes.

Here is the thing that most people do not understand about waterfall deaths, and it is the thing I want you to carry out of this story. The danger at a waterfall is almost never at the bottom, and it is very often not on the cliff edge either. It is above the falls. In the shallow, calm, pretty water at the top, the water that looks like you could wade across it with a toddler. By the end of this story, you will understand exactly why that water is so dangerous, why a fire chief in that county said that once you go over the top, you are done, and why so many of the people who die in that water die trying to save somebody else. And I will give you the three words the rescue people want everyone to remember. They might save the life of somebody you love.

Let me start with the place, and with the numbers, because they are plain history.

Rainbow Falls is one of a string of waterfalls on the Horsepasture River, which comes tumbling down off the edge of the Blue Ridge into the gorges below. It sits partly in Gorges State Park and partly in the national forest, and it is one of the most beautiful waterfalls in the whole country. The river drops over a cliff in a single long fall, more than a hundred feet, into a deep pool, and on a sunny day the spray throws up a rainbow, which is how it got its name. A trail leads to it through the woods. People hike in, look at it from the overlook, take their pictures. And a great many of them go on up the trail to the top, where the river comes sliding across a broad shelf of rock and goes over the edge.

In two thousand sixteen, the fire chief in Lake Toxaway, a community in that county, told reporters that a death at a waterfall that summer was the seventh waterfall death in the county so far that year. Seven, in one county, in one year. Two years later, in two thousand eighteen, the same kind of story was in the news again. By that summer there had been three waterfall deaths in the county, and two of them had been at Rainbow Falls, within about two weeks of each other.

I want to tell you about three of the people who died at Rainbow Falls, because their stories, put side by side, tell you everything you need to know. I am going to tell them carefully, because these are real people with families who still miss them.

The first was a young woman named Taylor Terrell. She was twenty-four years old, a television news anchor from Georgia, smart and well liked and with her whole life ahead of her. In the summer of two thousand sixteen, she was up at the top of Rainbow Falls, wading in the creek. The reports say that she slipped on rocks covered in algae, and the current swept her over the falls. The drop at Rainbow Falls is well over a hundred feet. She did not survive.

The second, in June of two thousand eighteen, was a forty-two-year-old man. He was at the falls with his dog, and the dog got into the water, up above the falls, and got into trouble in the current. And the man did what almost any one of us who loves a dog would do. He jumped in after it. And the river carried him over the falls.

And the third, just about two weeks later, on the Fourth of July in two thousand eighteen, was a sixteen-year-old girl. An eleven-year-old child had gotten into trouble in the water above the falls, and the girl went in to help. She was swept over the edge. She was sixteen.

I am not going to say their names more than I have to. But I want you to notice the shape of those three stories. A young woman standing in shallow water at the top of a falls. A man going in after his dog. A teenager going in after a child. Not one of them was doing anything daring. Not one of them was climbing on the cliff or jumping off the rocks. They were in the calm water at the top, or they went into it to help somebody who was.

So let me explain why that water is so dangerous, because once you understand it, you will never look at the top of a waterfall the same way again.

The first reason is the rock. The rock in the bed of a mountain river, especially where the water runs thin and smooth over a shelf, is covered with a film of algae and moss. It grows there because the water is always moving over it and the sunlight is reaching it. That film is slick. Not a little bit slick. Slick like ice. People who have stepped onto it describe it as having their feet go out from under them all at once, with no warning, before they even knew they were falling. And a lot of the time, you cannot tell by looking which rocks are covered and which are not. The rule the old river people always gave was simple. If the rock is wet and dark, it is slick, and if it is in the water, it is slicker than that.

The second reason is the current. Here is something about moving water that almost nobody understands until it is too late. Moving water is much, much stronger than it looks. Water that is only up to your shins, moving at the speed of a slow walk, pushes on your legs with a surprising amount of force. If it is moving a little faster, or a little deeper, it can push a grown person right off their feet. And at the top of a waterfall, the water is speeding up, because it is about to go over. The river narrows and quickens as it comes to the edge. The water that looks lazy twenty feet back from the drop is moving a good deal faster by the time it reaches it.

And the third reason is the worst one. Once you are down, you are sliding. The shelf of rock at the top of a waterfall is smooth and tilted toward the edge, and it is covered with that slick film, and the water is pushing you toward the drop. There is nothing to grab. People in that situation claw at the rock and find nothing. The water carries them along the bottom, on their back or their belly, toward the lip, and then over it. It can happen in seconds. That is what the fire chief meant. The Forest Service has said it too, that above the falls the current can be rapid and the rocks can be slippery. When you go over the top, you're done.

And it is not only the fall itself. The bottom of a waterfall has its own danger, one that has drowned a great many strong swimmers who would never have dreamed of wading at the top. When a big volume of water pours over a ledge into a pool, it drives straight down toward the bottom, then curls back up and around toward the falls again, in a great rolling circle. The river people call it a hydraulic, or a hole, and some of the worst ones they call keepers, for the plain reason that they keep what goes into them. A person caught in one can be pushed down, brought back up toward the falling water, pushed down again, over and over, with no way to swim out, because every way they turn the water is carrying them back. The surface of the pool can look calm and foamy and inviting just a few yards from where it happens. That is why the signs at so many of the falls in those mountains say no swimming at the base too, and why the rescue divers who go into those pools after a drowning go in on ropes.

There are two more things about that water that people do not think about. The first is the cold. Mountain rivers come down out of the high country, from springs and from shaded hollows where the sun barely reaches, and even in the middle of summer that water can be startlingly cold. When a person falls into cold water all at once, the body does something it cannot help. It gasps. A sharp, deep, involuntary breath, and if your head is under the water when that gasp comes, you breathe in the river. Then the cold makes your muscles stiff and clumsy within minutes, so that even a good swimmer cannot swim the way they could in a warm lake. The second is that the river can change while you are standing in it. A thunderstorm up on the ridges, miles away, out of sight, can raise the water at the top of a falls in a matter of minutes. The shelf that was ankle deep when you stepped onto it can be knee deep and pushing hard by the time you turn around.

And there is a strange contrast in those mountains that I have always thought tells the whole story. Not far from Rainbow Falls, in the national forest, there is a famous spot called Sliding Rock, where a creek runs down a long, smooth, sloping face of rock into a pool at the bottom, and people line up all summer long to slide down it on the seat of their pants. It is one of the best loved places in western North Carolina. But it is a place that is managed. In the busy season there are people on duty watching it, and rules about where to go in and where to get out, and a pool at the bottom that people can swim out of. It is the very same kind of slick rock and moving water as the top of a waterfall. The difference is that someone is watching, and that at the bottom, there is a pool instead of a drop. Out in the wild, at the top of Rainbow Falls, nobody is watching, and at the bottom, there is a hundred feet of air.

Now, I said I would let the skeptic speak, and here is where he comes in.

The skeptic says, look, those people were careless. Don't go in the water at the top of a waterfall. Everybody knows that. There are signs. It is common sense.

And there are signs, at Rainbow Falls and at most of the waterfalls in those mountains. Big ones. Warnings about the danger above the falls. But here is the thing the skeptic misses. Common sense, when you are standing in the sunshine at the top of a beautiful waterfall with your family, says that the shallow water is safe. Common sense says that ankle-deep water cannot hurt you. Every instinct you have, from a whole lifetime of wading in creeks and standing in the shallow end of the pool, says that water you can see the bottom of is water you can stand in. The danger at the top of a waterfall is a danger that goes against common sense. That is exactly why it kills people.

And the skeptic misses something else. Two of the three people I told you about did not go in carelessly. They went in on purpose, to save a life. And there is no sign in the world that will stop a person from going in after their dog, or after a child. That is not a lack of common sense. That is love. And that is the hardest part of this whole story to talk about.

The people who study drownings have seen that pattern so many times they have a name for it. They talk about the would-be rescuer who becomes a second victim. All over this country, every year, people drown going into the water after a child, or a friend, or a dog. Very often, the one they went in to save makes it out, and the rescuer does not. Dogs in particular are much better swimmers than people give them credit for, and a dog swept downstream will very often scramble out on its own a little further down, wet and frightened and fine. The person who went in after it, in boots and jeans, with no idea of the current, may not be so lucky. I am not saying that to be hard on anybody. I am saying it because the moment it happens, there is no time to think, and the only way to do the right thing in that moment is to have decided it beforehand.

And there is one more thing that has changed at the waterfalls in recent years, and you have probably already guessed it. The pictures. Everybody wants the picture. The one from the very top, looking out over the edge, with the water sliding past your feet and the valley spread out below. The one standing on the rock in the middle of the stream with the falls behind you. People go out on those rocks for a photograph who would never go out there for any other reason. They hand somebody the phone, and they step out, and they turn around to smile, and that is exactly the moment, with their back to the water and their attention on the camera, when one slick step can go wrong. No photograph in the world is worth it. The view from the overlook is just as beautiful, and you will be around to look at the picture afterward.

So before I give you the rule, let me take you back a long way, to what the first people in these mountains believed about the rivers. Because they had a very particular way of thinking about moving water, and I have always thought it was wiser than ours.

The Cherokee, whose country this was, had a name for the river. They called it the Long Man. James Mooney, who wrote down a great many of the old Cherokee beliefs in the eighteen hundreds, recorded it. The story goes that the river is a giant, with his head up in the mountains and his feet down in the lowlands, always moving, always speaking, never resting. And the old people treated the Long Man with great respect. They went to the water to pray, and to purify themselves, and they listened to the river as though it were speaking to them, which, in their way of seeing things, it was.

And there were other stories, darker ones. The Cherokee told of beings who lived under the water, in the deep pools and below the falls. Folks say there were water cannibals, invisible people who lived at the bottom of the river and who would come up and take a person away. The story goes that a person who had been taken would seem to go on living for a while, and then waste away and die. Nobody can prove any of it, and I am telling it to you as the old story it is. But you will notice that, like so many old stories, it carries a warning inside it. The river takes people. Be careful at the water.

The settlers who came into these mountains later had their own sayings. I grew up hearing that the river is hungry. Folks say every river takes somebody every year, and that it will have its due. The story goes that some rivers take one every summer, the way a man takes a meal. I have heard old people say that about the rivers in their own county, and say it seriously. Nobody can prove that a river is hungry. But anybody who has watched the rescue squads go out on those rivers summer after summer knows why the old people talked that way.

Folks also say you should never go in the water above a falls to fetch anything, not a hat, not a shoe, not a fishing rod. The story goes that the water will let you take one step out and then take you on the second. That one, I think, is not superstition at all. A hat can be bought again at any store in the county. A shoe can be replaced. The old people who said that had watched too many folks go out after some small thing that floated off downstream, and they boiled the whole lesson down into one sentence a child could remember.

And that brings me to the rule. Here it is. It is the title of this story. Never wade above a waterfall.

Not even in shallow water. Not even at the very edge of the river. Not even for a picture. When you go to a waterfall, you enjoy it from the trail and from the overlook, and if you go to the top, you stay on dry ground well back from the water. You stay off the rocks along the edge of the river, especially the dark, wet ones. You keep your children's hands in yours, and you keep your dog on a leash. You do not let anybody in your group go out on the shelf at the top for a better look, no matter how calm the water seems.

And now the three words. The rescue people and the lifeguards and the water safety teachers have been repeating them for years, and they are the ones I promised you. Reach, throw, don't go.

If somebody is in trouble in the water, a person or a dog, your first instinct will be to go in after them. And the hard truth that the records show, over and over again, is that the person who goes in after them very often dies too. Sometimes instead. So you reach first. A long stick, a branch, a trekking pole, a rope, anything you can hold onto from the bank while they grab the other end. If you cannot reach them, you throw something. Anything that floats. A cooler, a backpack, a life jacket, a log. And you call for help, nine one one, as fast as you can. You do not go in. Not above a waterfall. Not in fast water. I know how hard that is to hear. I know that every one of us thinks, in that moment, we would go in anyway. But the person in the water needs you on the bank, alive, calling for help. They do not need two people going over the falls instead of one.

And if you are ever the one who slips and goes down in moving water, the people who teach river safety say to get on your back with your feet pointed downstream, so you can push off rocks with your feet instead of your head, and to try to work your way toward the shore at an angle. Do not try to stand up in fast water, because your foot can get wedged between rocks and the current will push you under. Those are good things to know. But above a waterfall, there may not be time for any of them. That is why the only rule that really matters there is the first one. Stay out of the water.

There is one more thing I want to say, and then I will let you go. That summer of two thousand eighteen, after the second death at Rainbow Falls in two weeks, the people in that county talked a great deal about what more could be done. More signs. Fences. Rangers. But a waterfall is a wild thing, in a wild place, and there is no fence in the world long enough to go around every river in the Land of Waterfalls. In the end, it comes down to each of us, standing at the top of a beautiful falls on a summer day, deciding whether to take that one more step.

I think about that young woman at the beginning of this story, stepping out onto the shelf to get a better look at the view. I think about the man who loved his dog, and the girl who went in after a child. Every one of them did the most natural thing in the world. The water looked gentle. The day was beautiful. And the Long Man, as the Cherokee would say, was just doing what he always does. Moving. Always moving. Toward the edge.
