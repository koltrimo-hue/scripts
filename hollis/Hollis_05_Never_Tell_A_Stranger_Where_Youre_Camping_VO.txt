A creek in the mountains of southwest Virginia, early May. Two men have set up camp by the water to fish for a couple of days. The fire is going. Supper is on. A stranger comes walking up out of the woods, friendly, talkative, and they ask him to sit and eat with them, because that is what decent people do in the mountains. He tells them his name. He tells them he went to college. He tells them he wrote papers for NASA. They do not quite believe him, but it is a pleasant evening, and he is good company. Then the light starts to go. And the stranger stands up, and walks around behind one of them.

Before I go on — I put together something that goes with these stories. It is called the Holler Field Manual, the real rules for staying alive out in these mountains, not the legend part, the part that actually keeps you breathing. Link is in the description. Now, back to it.

That creek is called Dismal Creek, and it runs through Giles County, Virginia, not far from the Appalachian Trail. And the night I just described was the sixth of May, in two thousand eight. But this story does not start in two thousand eight. It starts twenty-seven years before that, about a mile and a half away, at a little three-sided log shelter on the trail called Wapiti. And the man who walked out of the woods to that campfire had been there before.

I want to tell you about two nights, twenty-seven years apart, in the same stretch of woods, with the same man. I want to tell you about the trail itself, what it is and how it works, and the kind of trust it runs on, because that trust is the reason this story happened the way it did. I will tell you a little of what the hikers say about that place, which is folklore and not history, and I will mark it plain. And at the end of it, I am going to give you a rule. It is a simple rule, and it is in the title of this story, and I am going to tell you why it matters more than any lock or any weapon you could carry. Stay with me through both nights, and you will have it.

First, let me tell you about the trail, for those of you who have never walked it.

The Appalachian Trail runs something like two thousand two hundred miles, from Springer Mountain in Georgia all the way to Mount Katahdin in Maine. It goes through fourteen states. It was dreamed up in the nineteen twenties and finished in the nineteen thirties, and it has been walked by millions of people since. Most folks only walk a piece of it, a day or a weekend. But every year, a few thousand people set out to walk the whole thing, end to end, in one go. They call those people thru-hikers. It takes most of them five or six months.

The man who first proposed it was a forester and a planner named Benton MacKaye, back in nineteen twenty-one. His idea was a long footpath along the crest of the eastern mountains, a place where people from the crowded cities could get out and walk and be renewed. Volunteers built most of it, clubs of ordinary people with axes and grub hoes, working one section at a time, and the last piece was finished in nineteen thirty-seven. It is still kept up mostly by volunteers to this day. That is worth knowing, because it tells you something about the spirit of the thing. The trail was built by strangers, for strangers, out of good will.

All along the trail, every so often, there are shelters. Most of them are simple things, three walls and a roof, open on one side, with a wooden floor to sleep on and maybe a fire ring out front and a spring or a creek nearby. Anybody can stay in them. Nobody owns a spot. You walk in at the end of the day, tired, and you put your sleeping bag down next to people you have never met, and you eat supper together, and you talk. And in the morning, everybody goes their way.

There is a whole culture that has grown up on the trail, and a lot of it is beautiful. Hikers take trail names, nicknames that other hikers give them, and a lot of folks on the trail never learn each other's real names at all. People leave food and cold drinks at road crossings for hikers they will never meet, and the hikers call that trail magic. Folks in the little towns along the way give rides to strangers, let them sleep in their barns, feed them. Hikers write in logbooks at every shelter, notes for whoever comes along behind them. It is about the most trusting place in America. You share your supper with strangers, and you sleep beside them, and you tell them where you are headed tomorrow, because why would you not.

Hold that in your mind, because it is the heart of this story. That trust is what makes the trail what it is. And in a very small number of cases, it is the thing that gets people killed.

Now let me give you the first night, and I am going to give it to you as plain history, because it is.

In the spring of nineteen eighty-one, two young people from Maine were hiking the trail. Their names were Robert Mountford Junior and Laura Susan Ramsay. They were both twenty-seven years old. They were social workers, and they were not just hiking for themselves. They were walking the trail to raise money for a school for children with mental disabilities back home. By May, they had come up out of Georgia and through North Carolina and Tennessee and into Virginia.

On the evening of the nineteenth of May, nineteen eighty-one, they were at the Wapiti Shelter in Giles County. And another man came along and shared supper with them there. He was a local man, from Pearisburg, the town just down the road. His name was Randall Lee Smith. He was in his twenties, around their age.

The two of them never left that shelter alive.

The records show that their bodies were found nearly two weeks later, buried in shallow graves and covered over with leaves, not far from the shelter. Randall Lee Smith was arrested. And in March of nineteen eighty-two, he surprised a lot of people by standing up in court and pleading guilty to two counts of second-degree murder. He was sentenced to thirty years in prison.

I am not going to dwell on the details of how they died. The accounts are out there for anybody who wants them, and I do not think the families need to hear them told one more time by a stranger. What I want you to notice is the shape of it. Two good people, doing a good thing. A friendly local man who sat down with them in the evening. A meal shared. That is all.

And that is where the first part of the story ends. Smith went to prison, and the trail went on, and the hikers went on stopping at Wapiti Shelter, because it was there, and it was a roof.

Now let me tell you something about that place that is not history at all, and I want you to hear it in the right voice. Because when something like that happens at a shelter, the trail does not forget it.

Folks say Wapiti is a place you do not want to spend the night. Hikers coming north through Virginia hear about it from other hikers, days before they get there, the way news travels on the trail, from one supper to the next. The story goes that people who have stayed there felt something wrong. That they could not sleep. That they heard things moving around the shelter in the dark. Some hikers will walk on past it, even when they are worn out, and push on to the next campsite rather than stop there. Some say the logbook in that shelter had entries in it from people who felt watched all night. Nobody can prove any of that, and I am not going to tell you that a place can hold onto something that happened in it. But I will tell you that every shelter on that trail has a story, and Wapiti has the one people tell in a low voice.

And that is an old thing, in these mountains. Long before there was a trail, the people of these hills had a very particular way of thinking about strangers. On the one hand, the old mountain way was hospitality. You fed whoever came to your door. A traveler on the road got a meal and a place by the fire, because that is how folks survived up here, by helping each other, and because you never knew when it might be you out on the road in the cold. I grew up hearing that you never turn away a hungry man. Every family up here had some version of it.

But on the other hand, the old people had a whole set of stories that warned you about the one stranger in a hundred who was not what he seemed. The man who came to the door at dusk and asked to come in. The traveler who asked a few too many questions about who lived in the house, and how many, and where the menfolk were working that day. Folks say you were to watch a stranger's eyes while he ate. Folks say you did not tell a man you did not know where you kept your money, or when you would be away from home, or which road you would be taking in the morning. The story goes that a polite man who asks where you are going is either making conversation or making plans, and you cannot always tell which until it is too late. I have heard old people say that if you have to tell a stranger something, tell him you are expecting company. Those are not laws written anywhere. They are just the kind of thing that families passed down, generation to generation, in a country where there was a lot of road and not a lot of help.

So the old mountain way was to feed the stranger, and to keep your plans to yourself. Both at once. I want you to hold onto that, because it is going to come back.

Now we go to the second night.

Randall Lee Smith did not serve thirty years. He was released from prison in nineteen ninety-six, on what they call mandatory parole, after serving about fifteen. He came home to the same part of Virginia. He came off probation in two thousand six. People around Pearisburg knew who he was. The local paper later wrote that he never shook the reputation of being the man who killed those two hikers. He kept to himself, by most accounts.

Now I want to pause here, because this is the place where a lot of people get angry when they hear this story, and they have a right to. A man pleads guilty to killing two hikers on a trail. He gets thirty years. He serves fifteen. And then he goes home to the same county, a few miles from the same trail, and there is nothing in the world to keep him away from it. Historians of the trail, and the people who keep it, have argued about that for years. I am not going to settle that argument for you. But I will tell you that the hikers coming through that section of Virginia in the years after nineteen ninety-six had no way of knowing who the quiet man from Pearisburg was, or that he lived just down the road. To a hiker coming up from Georgia, every local is just a local. Every friendly face at a road crossing is just a friendly face. That is how the trail works.

And on the sixth of May, two thousand eight, he was out along Dismal Creek, about a mile and a half from the Wapiti Shelter.

Two men were camping there by the creek, fishing. Their names were Scott Johnston and Sean Farmer. They were not thru-hikers. They had come up for a few days of trout fishing. And in the evening, a man came walking up out of the woods to their camp, and he was friendly, and they invited him to eat with them.

He told them his name was Ricky Williams. That was not his name. He talked a good deal. He told them he had graduated from Virginia Tech. He told them he wrote papers for NASA. The two men said afterward that they did not believe a word of it, but he was harmless enough, they figured, just a lonely fellow who wanted somebody to talk to. They shared their supper with him. Now you see why I told you about the first night first. Because it was the same shape exactly.

When it got dark, the man stood up, walked around behind Sean Farmer, and shot him in the head. Then he turned and shot Scott Johnston in the neck. And while he was shooting Farmer again, Johnston ran. The man fired after him and hit him in the back of the head. And Scott Johnston kept going.

Both of those men, shot in the head and the neck, got away. The accounts of how they did it are about the hardest thing to believe in this whole story, and the two of them later told it themselves, on national television. They got themselves out of those woods and to help. Both of them lived.

Randall Lee Smith tried to get away in a truck that belonged to one of the men. He wrecked it. He was arrested that same day. And four days later, while he was in jail, he died from the injuries he got in that crash. He never stood trial for the second night.

That is the documented history, and every part of it is in the newspapers of the day. Two nights, twenty-seven years apart, a mile and a half from each other. The same man. The same friendly approach. The same shared supper. The same darkness falling.

And I want you to notice something about that second night, because it is the most useful thing in this whole story, if you ever find yourself in a camp with somebody who is not right. The two fishermen said afterward that they did not believe him. They did not believe he went to Virginia Tech. They did not believe the business about NASA. Something in them noticed that the stories did not add up. They just did not take the next step, which is to ask why a man would walk out of the woods and tell two strangers a pack of things that were not true. And I am not faulting them. Not one person in a thousand would have taken that step. We are raised to be polite. We are raised to let a man tell his tall tales and nod along and pass him the beans.

But the people who teach wilderness safety will tell you that is exactly the kind of thing worth paying attention to. A stranger who gives you a name that does not seem to fit him. A stranger whose story keeps changing. A stranger who does not seem to be equipped for where he is, who has no pack, or no fishing gear, or no reason that you can see for being there. A stranger who asks a lot of questions about you, and who you are with, and how long you are staying, and when your friends are coming, and answers none about himself. A stranger who lingers after the meal is done, after the light is going, when a normal person would be heading back to his own camp. None of those things by themselves mean anything. Most lonely people who talk too much are just lonely people who talk too much. But when they start piling up, that is the time to trust the feeling in your gut, and not the manners your mother taught you.

Now I promised I would raise the skeptic's objection first, so let me do it here.

The skeptic says, look, this is a terrible story, but it is a very rare one. And the skeptic is right. The Appalachian Trail is, by any honest measure, one of the safest places you can spend time in this country. Millions of people walk some part of it every year. The number of people who have been murdered on or near it, in all the decades since it was built, is small enough that you can list them, and people have. You are a great deal more likely to be hurt in your car on the way to the trailhead than on the trail itself. You are more likely to be hurt by a fall, or by the weather, or by bad water, or by your own heart, than by another human being out there. I want you to know that, and believe it, because it is true.

But the list exists. And when you look at the list, there are some patterns in it that are worth knowing.

In the nineteen seventies, there were killings at shelters in Georgia and elsewhere along the trail. In May of nineteen eighty-eight, in Pennsylvania, two women were camping near the trail when a man who had been watching them from the woods opened fire with a rifle. One of them, Rebecca Wight, was killed. The other, Claudia Brenner, was shot several times and still walked out for help, a long way, on her own. The man who did it was caught and sent to prison, and the court heard that he attacked them because they were two women together. In September of nineteen ninety, again in Pennsylvania, near Duncannon, two young thru-hikers named Geoffrey Hood and Molly LaRue were killed at a shelter by a drifter named Paul David Crews, who had come onto the trail and was caught later, far away. And in nineteen ninety-six, in Shenandoah National Park in Virginia, two young women named Julianne Williams and Lollie Winans were killed at their campsite, not on the trail itself but not far from it. That one has never been solved.

And more recently, in the spring of two thousand nineteen, also in southwest Virginia, a hiker from Oklahoma was stabbed to death on the trail by a man that other hikers had been warning each other about for days. People had been posting about him online, describing his behavior, telling each other to steer clear. The warnings went up and down the trail by word of mouth. But not everybody heard them in time.

So what do you see when you put all those together? You see that very often, the person who did it was somebody who came along, who seemed to be part of the trail, who sat down at the fire, or walked alongside for a while, or just watched. You see that very often, the people who died had been kind to that person, or at least had not been unkind. And you see that very often, the person who did it knew where the people he hurt were going to be. He knew where they were sleeping. He had been told, or he had watched.

Which brings me to the rule. The one in the title. The one I promised.

Never tell a stranger where you are camping.

It sounds so small. It sounds almost rude. On the trail, the first thing people ask each other is where are you headed tonight. It is the most natural question in the world. And most of the time the person asking is just another hiker who wants to know if they will see you at the next shelter. But you do not have to answer it straight. Not to somebody you just met. You can say you have not decided. You can say you will see how your legs feel. You can say you are meeting friends up the trail. Nobody will think twice about it. And if the person asking is that one in a hundred thousand, then you have just taken away the one thing he needed most.

And there are other rules that go with it, and the hikers who have been out there a long time will tell you every one. If somebody makes the hair stand up on the back of your neck, you listen to that feeling, and you move on. You do not stay at a shelter with somebody who bothers you, just because you are tired and it is getting late. You pack up and walk another mile, or two, or three. You camp somewhere that person does not know about. If you are hiking alone, you tell somebody back home where you will be, and when, and you check in, but you do not post where you are sleeping tonight on the internet for the whole world to see. Post it after you have left. You pay attention to what other hikers are saying, about a person on the trail who is acting strange, and you pass it on. And if you are camped somewhere and a friendly stranger walks up out of the woods at suppertime, you can be kind to him, you can even feed him, the way the old mountain people did. But you keep your plans to yourself.

Feed the stranger. Keep your plans. That is the old mountain way, both halves of it. The trail kept the first half and forgot the second.

People on the trail will tell you that the old Wapiti Shelter was torn down and moved on account of what happened there. Accounts differ on that. Some folks who know that section of trail well say it was replaced years later simply because it had gotten old, and that the shelter standing today sits a little way off from the old spot. What the accounts do agree on is that after nineteen eighty-one, the road that used to run up close to the shelter was closed off, so that today you have to walk in a good way to reach it. Fewer people who are not hikers wander up there now. That was the point. The creek is still there. The trout are still in it. People still go up there to fish in May.

I think about those two men by the creek in two thousand eight. They did everything a decent person is supposed to do. A lonely stranger came to their fire, and they fed him. They did not believe his stories about NASA, but they did not run him off either, because who runs a man off for telling tall tales at supper. And I think about Robert Mountford and Laura Ramsay, twenty-seven years before, doing the very same thing at that shelter a mile and a half away. Walking the trail to raise money for children. Sharing what they had.

It is not their kindness I would change. Not one bit of it. The trail is a better place for people like them. All I would change is one sentence. The answer to the question, where are you sleeping tonight. Because nearly every time the worst has happened out there, it started with somebody knowing.
