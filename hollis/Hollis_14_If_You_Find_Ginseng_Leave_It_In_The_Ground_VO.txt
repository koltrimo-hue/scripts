This fall, two people walked out of the Great Smoky Mountains with nearly seven hundred roots hidden in their bags. A steep slope deep in the park, in late September, green and dim. The ground is rich and black and covered with ferns. And here and there among them stands a low plant with five-fingered leaves and a little cluster of bright red berries at its center. The plant on that slope is ginseng. Up in these mountains, a lot of folks just call it sang. And the two people on that slope were caught by rangers in the Great Smoky Mountains National Park this past September, in two thousand twenty-six, with six hundred and eighty-eight ginseng roots between them. One of them had a hundred and sixty-seven. The other had five hundred and twenty-one. Altogether, about eight pounds of root. It is against federal law to dig ginseng in the park, and it can bring a fine of up to five thousand dollars and up to six months in jail.

Now, you may be thinking, all that trouble over a plant? And that is exactly the right question to ask, because the answer is one of the strangest stories in the whole history of these mountains. It goes back three hundred years. It runs from the hollows of Kentucky and Carolina all the way to the Emperor of China. It involves Daniel Boone, and a ship that sailed out of New York the year after the Revolution, and a root that some people believed could add years to a man's life. By the end of this story, you will know why a little plant on a shady hillside has been worth more than its weight in silver, why so many families in these mountains have a secret patch they will never tell you about, and why, if you ever find one in a national park, the right thing to do is to walk away and tell nobody. Not even me.

So let us start with the plant.

American ginseng is a small woodland plant that grows wild in the shady hardwood forests of the eastern United States, and nowhere does it grow better than in the Appalachian Mountains. It likes the cool, moist, north-facing and east-facing slopes, in deep, rich soil, under tall trees, in the kind of cove forest that the southern mountains are famous for. Above ground, it is not much to look at. A single stem, with leaves that spread out like fingers on a hand, five leaflets to a leaf. In the late summer, a little cluster of berries forms at the center, and in the fall those berries turn a bright, glossy red. That red is often how people spot it.

And the Smoky Mountains are about as good a home as ginseng could ask for. The Smokies are famous among scientists as one of the richest forests in the whole temperate world, with more kinds of trees than all of northern Europe, and some of the finest of that forest is in the coves. A cove, in mountain talk, is a sheltered bowl of land at the head of a hollow, where the soil is deep and dark and always a little damp, and the trees grow enormous, and the ground underneath them is carpeted with wildflowers in the spring. Tulip poplars, sugar maples, basswoods, buckeyes. That is ginseng country. Before the trade, there were cove forests in these mountains where ginseng grew as thick as clover.

But the part that matters is underground. Ginseng has a thick, pale, forked root, and the older the plant is, the bigger and more valuable that root gets. And ginseng grows slowly. It may take five years or more before a plant even makes seed, and a really good root can be decades old. The diggers can tell the age of a root by counting the little scars on its neck, one for every year it has put up a stem.

Even the seed is slow. Those bright red berries each hold a seed or two, and when they fall to the ground, they do not sprout the next spring the way most seeds would. A ginseng seed usually lies in the leaf litter through a whole year and more, through two winters, before it finally comes up, a tiny plant with a single little leaf. And then it begins the long, slow business of becoming a root worth digging. Deer browse it. Mice and birds eat the berries. A dry summer can set it back for years. A wild ginseng plant that makes it to old age, with a big, heavy, forked root, has survived a great deal. That is part of why the old ones are so valuable. You cannot hurry them. A plant with three of those finger-spreading leaves instead of one or two is usually an older plant, and the old diggers called it a three-prong.

And here is the strangest thing about the root. Very often, it is shaped like a person. A thick body, with two forked legs, and sometimes arms. That shape is at the heart of why people have valued it for thousands of years. In China, where people have used ginseng as a medicine since ancient times, the name for it means, more or less, the man root. The root that looks like a person, and that was believed to strengthen the whole person. To bring back vigor, and ease weakness, and some believed even to lengthen life. Chinese ginseng had been dug so hard over the centuries that wild plants became rare there, and its price climbed higher and higher.

So now let me give you the plain history of how a root from these mountains ended up on the other side of the world.

In the early seventeen hundreds, a French Jesuit missionary in Canada, near Montreal, learned about Chinese ginseng from reports sent back by other missionaries in China. He went looking in the Canadian woods for something like it, and he found it. The American plant was a close cousin of the Chinese one. Before long, ginseng dug in the forests of North America was being shipped to China, where people would pay very well for it. Canada's ginseng was dug out within a few decades, and the traders turned south, into the American colonies, and into the mountains.

Then, in seventeen eighty-four, the year after the Revolutionary War ended, the first American ship to sail directly to China left the harbor in New York. It was called the Empress of China. And along with silver, the main thing it carried to trade was ginseng. Accounts differ on just how much. Some say a few thousand pounds, some say tons. Either way, one of the very first things this brand new country had to sell to the rest of the world was a root dug out of its own woods.

And the man who dug a good deal of it was Daniel Boone. That is the part a lot of people do not know about him. In the seventeen eighties, Boone made a good share of his living in the ginseng trade, hiring men to dig it and buying it up from the people in the backcountry, and shipping it east. There is a story about it that every ginseng digger in these mountains seems to know. The story goes that Boone loaded a great cargo of ginseng onto a boat and started up the Ohio River to take it east to sell, and near the mouth of the Kanawha, the boat swamped and turned over, and the whole load was ruined. And the story goes that Boone and his men simply went back into the mountains and dug another load. Historians argue about the details of that one, and some of the people who have studied it think it has grown in the telling. But it is told by diggers to this day, and it says something about the kind of man Boone was, and the kind of money there was in that root.

By the late eighteen hundreds, the mountains were the heart of the whole American trade. One account says that in the year eighteen seventy-six, four hundred thousand pounds of ginseng were sold out of western North Carolina alone. In the eighteen nineties, the southern Appalachians supplied more than three-fourths of all the medicinal plants and herbs sold in the United States, and ginseng was by far the most valuable of them. Every little country store in the mountains bought sang. A family that went out in the fall and dug a few pounds could pay its taxes, buy shoes for the children, get through the winter. For a lot of mountain families, the sang money was the only cash money they saw all year.

And it is still bought and sold that way in a good many places. In the fall, when the digging season opens in the states that allow it, buyers set up in country stores and gas stations and back rooms all through the mountains, licensed by the state, with scales and cash. Diggers come in with their roots wrapped in newspaper or in a paper sack, dried or fresh, and the buyer looks them over, judges the age and the shape and the quality, and makes an offer. Shapes that look most like a person, and roots that are old and wrinkled and wild, bring the best money. The buyers sell up the line to dealers, and the dealers ship it overseas, mostly to Asia, the same route that root has been traveling since before this country was a country.

There is an important difference you should know about, too. Not all ginseng is wild. In the late eighteen hundreds, as the wild plants got scarce and the prices climbed, farmers in Wisconsin began growing ginseng in fields, under wooden shades that imitate the forest canopy. Wisconsin still grows a great deal of it today. But field-grown ginseng is worth only a small fraction of what wild ginseng brings. The buyers in Asia can tell the difference, and they pay for the wild root, the one that took decades to grow in the shade of an old mountain forest. Which means that all the farming in the world has never taken the pressure off the wild plants. It has only made the wild ones more valuable.

And that brings me to the folklore, because a plant that important to a people's survival is going to gather a great many stories.

I grew up hearing that ginseng hides from you. Folks say that a sang patch you found one year will be gone the next, and then show up again somewhere else on the same hillside, as though the plants had walked off in the night. The story goes that the plant knows when it is being hunted. Nobody can prove that, and the honest explanation is that ginseng sometimes stays dormant underground for a year or two without putting up a stem. But you could not convince some of the old diggers of that.

Folks say you can find ginseng by the company it keeps. The old diggers talk about sang sign. The plants that like the same ground ginseng likes. Black cohosh, with its tall white spikes. Maidenhair fern. And especially the rattlesnake fern, which the old people said points the way to ginseng, so that if you find one, you look in the direction its frond is pointing. That one has something to it, at least the part about the company. Those plants really do grow in the same kind of rich, shady ground. Whether the fern is pointing anywhere in particular, nobody can prove.

The old accounts say the Cherokee had their own way with the plant. Mooney wrote that they spoke of it with great respect, and that a Cherokee digger, when he found ginseng, would pass by the first plants he saw, and only take a later one, and say a few words to the plant, and leave a bead or something in the hole as a gift. Some say they called it the little man. Nobody can say for certain how old those practices are, or how widely they were kept. But you will notice that the old way of digging had something built into it. You did not take every plant you saw.

And the settler diggers had their own version of that. I grew up hearing that a good sang digger always plants the berries. When you dig a plant with ripe berries, you take those red berries and you push them down into the soil, right there, so that there will be sang on that hillside for your grandchildren. Folks say you never dig the little ones, only the big old three-prongs and four-prongs. And folks say you never, ever tell anybody where your patch is. Not your neighbor. Not your preacher. Sometimes not even your own children, until you are old. There are family sang patches in these mountains that have been kept secret for a hundred years and more, passed down from grandfather to grandson on a walk in the woods, with a warning not to tell.

And sanging, as folks called it, was a family thing. In the fall, after the crops were in, whole families would go out into the woods together, the grown folks and the children, and spend the day working the slopes. Children learned early to spot the red berries and the spread of the leaves, and they learned the rules right along with it. Dig carefully, so you do not break the root. Plant the berries. Leave the little ones. Mind where you are. A great many people who grew up in these mountains will tell you that some of the best days of their childhood were spent sanging with a grandparent, and that they learned more about the woods on those days than they ever learned anywhere else. It was how the knowledge of the forest got passed down, one hillside at a time.

And folks say, and this one I would take seriously, that you do not go digging on another man's mountain. The story goes that sang patches have been guarded with shotguns, and that more than one fight in these hollows, and worse than a fight, started over who dug whose ginseng. I will not tell you particular stories, because I cannot prove them. But anybody who grew up in these mountains will tell you that sang is not a thing to joke about with a man on his own land.

Now, here is where the skeptic speaks up, and he has a real point to make.

The skeptic says, people have been digging ginseng in these mountains for three hundred years. It is part of the culture. It is part of the heritage. Mountain families have depended on it for generations, and a lot of them are poor, and still need the money. Who is the government to tell them they cannot dig a plant that grows wild in the woods? And what harm is there in a few roots?

And I understand that, I truly do. Sang digging is as much a part of these mountains as fiddle music. And on private land, in season, following the rules, it is still a legal and honorable thing that a great many people do. But here is the hard answer to the skeptic. The ginseng is running out.

Three hundred years of digging, with prices climbing higher and higher, have done to the wild American ginseng what they did to the Chinese ginseng long before. In a great many places where it once grew thick, it is now rare, or gone. Today wild ginseng can bring five hundred dollars or more for a pound of dried root, and when a plant is worth that much, people dig every one they can find, big or small, berries or no berries. That is why the trade in wild American ginseng has been regulated by international agreement for decades, and why the states that allow digging have seasons, and rules about how old a plant must be before it can be taken, and require the berries to be planted. And that is why, in the Great Smoky Mountains National Park, digging it is not allowed at all.

And the trouble got worse, not better, in our own time. About a dozen years ago, a television show about ginseng diggers in the mountains made sang hunting famous all over the country. Overnight, people who had never heard the word sang knew what it was worth. Some of the old diggers and the people who protect the plants will tell you the poaching pressure went up after that, and that strangers began showing up in the woods with digging tools. Historians of the trade argue about how much one show really changed things. But nobody argues that the wild plants needed more people out looking for them.

And ginseng is not the only plant those mountains have had to protect. The park ended the gathering of ramps, the wild onions that mountain people have dug every spring for generations, because the ramp patches were being dug out faster than they could grow back. Goldenseal, another old medicine root of the mountain woods, has gotten scarce in a great many places for the same reason as ginseng. Even galax, a little evergreen plant with shiny round leaves that florists buy by the thousands, has been taken from the woods so heavily in some years that the forest service had to put rules on it. Every one of those plants has a long history in mountain families, and every one of them has a limit. The woods can give a great deal. They cannot give everything to everybody forever.

The park is one of the last places where wild ginseng can grow the way it did before the trade, in old forest, undisturbed, from generation to generation. It is a kind of seed bank for the whole mountain chain. And poaching there has been serious for a long time. Back in two thousand nine, one person found guilty of digging ginseng in the park was sentenced to thirty days in jail and fined five hundred dollars. The accounts say that the park has worked for years to protect the plants in ways it does not advertise, including marking roots so that poached ones can be identified. When rangers do catch poachers, as they did this fall, they try to put the roots back in the ground. But the park has said that the long-term record shows only about one in three of those replanted roots survives. The rest are gone. A plant that took twenty years to grow, gone in a minute with a digging tool.

So let me give you the rule. It is the title of this story. If you find ginseng in the Smokies, leave it in the ground.

If you are hiking in a national park, or any place where digging is not allowed, and you come upon those five-fingered leaves and those red berries on a shady slope, you have found something rare and old and precious. Look at it. Admire it. And leave it exactly where it is. Do not dig it. Do not pick the berries to take home. And here is the part that the people who protect it would most want you to hear. Do not tell anybody where it is. Do not post a picture of it online with the location. Do not mention to the fellow at the gas station that you saw a big patch of sang up such-and-such a trail. That is how poachers find it. The kindest thing you can do for a wild ginseng plant is to forget where you saw it.

And if you ever come across somebody digging in the park, bent over on a slope far off the trail with a tool and a bag, you do not walk up and confront them. You do not know who they are, or what they are carrying, or how they will take it, and a lonely hillside a long way from anybody is no place to find out. You keep walking. You remember what you can, the trail, the place, the time, what they looked like, what they were driving at the trailhead. And when you get back to where you have a signal, or to a ranger station, you tell the rangers. That is their job, and they are good at it. The rangers cannot be on every slope in half a million acres of mountains. They depend on the eyes of the people who love the place. An ordinary hiker who saw something that did not look right, and said so afterward, may be the only witness that hillside ever has, and that may be what keeps a patch of old sang in the ground for another fifty years.

And if you dig on your own land, or with the landowner's permission, in the legal season, then dig the old way. Learn your state's rules and follow them. Take only the old, big plants. Plant every red berry you find, right back in the ground where it grew. Leave the little ones for the years to come. Never dig on somebody else's land without asking. And remember that the old diggers who passed by the first plants they found, and planted the berries for their grandchildren, were not being sentimental. They were making sure there would be sang on that mountain when they were gone.

And one more thing, from the Holler Field Manual side of me. Sang country is rough country. Ginseng grows on the steepest, shadiest, most out-of-the-way slopes in these mountains, and the people who go looking for it spend all day bent over, staring at the ground, working across slopes far from any trail. That is how people get lost, and fall, and turn an ankle a long way from help, and step on a copperhead they never saw. If you go out in the woods looking for anything, ginseng or mushrooms or old homesites, you tell somebody where you are going, you carry a map and a light, and every so often, you stand up straight and look around you, and remember where you are.

I think about those two people on that slope this September, sometimes. Bent over in the green light, with their little tools, not looking up at anything. And I think about Daniel Boone, if the story is true, standing on the bank of the Ohio watching his whole cargo of sang go floating off down the river, and then turning around and going back up into the mountains for more. And I think about the old diggers who pushed the red berries back into the black soil with their thumbs. For three hundred years, people have gone up into these mountains after a little root shaped like a man. The question now is whether there will be any left for the next three hundred.
