That man in the woodpile is not one person. He is thousands of people, every year. The copperhead is the snake that bites more people in the eastern United States than any other venomous snake, and it is not close. It lives in the woodpiles, and the rock walls, and the leaf litter, and the old barns, and the creek banks, all through these mountains and well beyond them. And the people it bites, an awful lot of them, have grown up with the same set of things they were told to do about it. Things their parents were told, and their parents before them. Cut it. Suck it. Tie it off. Pour whiskey on it. Pour whiskey in you.

Here is the strange part. In thirty years of records, from every poison control center in this country, copperheads killed five people. Five. And yet people still come into emergency rooms every year with hands and legs that were made worse by what was done to them after the bite. By the end of this story, you will know the exact things to do in the first ten minutes, and the old remedies, every one of which you have probably heard from somebody you love, that the doctors are begging people to stop.

Let us start with the snake.

The copperhead is a pit viper. That puts it in the same family as the rattlesnake and the cottonmouth. Pit vipers are named for a little pit on each side of the face, between the eye and the nostril, that can sense heat, so that the snake can find a warm-blooded mouse in the pitch dark. Copperheads are not big snakes. Most grown ones run two or three feet long. They are thick-bodied, with a broad, triangular head, and a coppery, reddish-tan color, and down the length of the body they have a pattern of darker bands that people describe as looking like hourglasses, or like a row of Hershey's Kisses seen from the side. Once you know that pattern, you will never forget it. But here is the trouble. That pattern was made for exactly one purpose. To disappear into a bed of dead leaves. A copperhead lying in leaf litter on the forest floor, or at the bottom of a woodpile, or along a stone wall in the fall, is about as close to invisible as an animal can get.

And the copperhead knows it. Its whole plan for staying alive is to lie still and let you walk by. Unlike a rattlesnake, it has no rattle to warn you. It does not usually flee when you come close, the way a lot of snakes do. It freezes. It trusts its camouflage. And most of the time, the camouflage works, and you walk right past, and you never know it was there. It is only when you step on it, or put your hand right down on it, or pick it up, that it strikes.

That is why so many copperhead bites are on the hands and the feet and the ankles. Picking up firewood. Pulling weeds in the garden. Reaching into a flower bed. Walking barefoot across the yard at dusk, on a warm summer evening, which is exactly when copperheads like to be out hunting. Turning over a board or a sheet of tin. Climbing up a rock ledge and reaching over the top without looking.

And the late summer and the early fall are the worst of it, for a reason most people never think about. That is when copperheads have their young. Unlike a lot of snakes, copperheads do not lay eggs. The mother carries the young inside her and gives birth to live babies, usually somewhere around August or September, a handful at a time. And those babies are born with fangs and venom, ready to go, from the very first day. They are small, about the length of a pencil, and they look just like the grown ones, except for one thing. The very tip of a baby copperhead's tail is a bright yellow, or a yellowish green. And the little snake will lie still in the leaves and twitch that bright tail tip back and forth, slowly, so that it looks like a caterpillar or a worm. A frog or a lizard comes over to investigate, and the little copperhead gets its supper. The people who study snakes call that luring. So in the fall, the woods and the yards are full of brand new copperheads, small enough to miss entirely, out exploring the world for the first time.

Now, how many people does it bite? The most quoted number comes from a medical study done back in the nineteen sixties, published in the Journal of the American Medical Association. That study estimated something like two thousand nine hundred copperhead bites a year in the United States. That is an old number, more than fifty years old now, and the doctors who study this will tell you nobody has done a better count since. But nobody doubts that copperheads still bite a great many people every year, more than any other venomous snake in this country.

And now here is the part that surprises people. How many of those people die?

Very, very few. That same old study put the death rate from copperhead bites at something like one in ten thousand. More recently, researchers went through thirty years of records from the poison control centers, from nineteen eighty-nine up through two thousand eighteen, and looked at every death from a native snakebite they could find in this country. In thirty years, in the whole United States, they counted about a hundred deaths from native snakes. And of the deaths where they knew which snake did it, the great majority, about nine out of ten, were rattlesnakes. Copperheads accounted for five. Five deaths in thirty years, out of something like tens of thousands of bites.

The rattlesnake is the other venomous snake of these mountains, and it is a different animal altogether. The timber rattlesnake lives up in the rocky ridges and the old-growth slopes all through the Appalachians, and it is a big, heavy snake, much bigger than a copperhead, sometimes black, sometimes yellow, with dark bands across its back and a rattle on the end of its tail. Timber rattlers spend the winter together in dens, deep in cracks in the rocks on south-facing slopes, and come out in the spring to spread out through the woods. They are slow to anger, and they will usually rattle long before they ever strike. But when one does bite, it can deliver a great deal more venom than a copperhead, and a far more dangerous kind. The mountain people respected the rattlesnake. There were men up here who hunted them, and men who handled them, and a good many graves from the old days with a snakebite somewhere in the story. If the copperhead is the snake that bites the most people, the rattlesnake is the one that kills them. Everything I am about to tell you about what to do for a copperhead bite goes double for a rattlesnake.

And while I am at it, a word about the cottonmouth, the water moccasin, since so many folks in the mountains are sure they have seen one. Cottonmouths are mostly snakes of the lowlands, the swamps and the slow rivers of the coastal plain and the deep South. Up in the high mountains, the big dark snake you see swimming in the creek or lying on a rock by the river is almost always a northern water snake, which is not venomous at all, though it is ornery and will bite if you grab it. A great many harmless water snakes have been killed over the years on the belief that they were cottonmouths.

So the skeptic says, well, there you go. Copperheads are nothing. Their venom is weak. Most of the time they do not even inject much of it. Why make a fuss? Why tell a story about a snake that hardly ever kills anybody?

And the skeptic has a little more evidence on his side, too. A good share of copperhead bites are what the doctors call dry bites. The snake strikes, the fangs go in, but little or no venom comes with them. The estimates run anywhere from one bite in ten to one bite in four. Nobody is entirely sure why. Some folks think the snake is saving its venom for something it can actually eat, since a grown person is a long way from being a meal for a three-foot snake. And even when a copperhead does inject venom, it usually injects only a small part of what it is carrying. All of that is true. And it is one more reason the old remedies seemed to work so well for so long. A man would get bitten, and cut himself, and tie himself off, and drink his whiskey, and when he lived, everybody would say it was the remedy that saved him, when very often it was simply a dry bite.

And here is my answer to the skeptic. Two things.

The first is that hardly ever kills is not the same thing as harmless. A copperhead bite hurts like fire, and it swells, sometimes enormously, up the hand and the arm, or up the foot and the leg. The skin can bruise and blister. People are often in the hospital for a day or two, and some of them need antivenom, and the swelling and the pain and the stiffness can last for weeks. In children and in older folks, and in people bitten on the hand, it can be very serious. One study found that around two out of every hundred copperhead bites led to death or to what the doctors call major effects. That is a small number. But if you are the one in fifty, it is not small to you.

And the second thing, the more important thing, is this. A lot of the harm that comes from a copperhead bite does not come from the snake at all. It comes from what people do after they are bitten. From the old remedies. And that is where this story really lives.

So let me take you into the folklore now, and I want you to hear it in the right voice, because some of these old beliefs are as much a part of these mountains as the ridges themselves.

I grew up hearing more snake lore than I could ever tell you in one sitting. Every family up here had its own. Folks say a snake will not die until the sun goes down. You can chop its head clean off, the story goes, and the body will go on twisting and writhing until sundown, and only then will it be still. That belief is old, and it shows up in tall tales all over the country. Folks say that if you kill a snake and hang it belly up over a fence, it will bring rain. I have seen dead blacksnakes hung on fences in a dry summer by people who would swear they did not believe it. Folks say a copperhead smells like cucumbers, and that if you walk into a place in the woods and smell fresh cut cucumbers, there is a copperhead near. Some people swear by that one. The scientists say snakes do give off a musky smell when they are disturbed, and some folks describe it that way, but nobody can prove you could find a copperhead by sniffing for it, and I would not try.

And then there is the hoop snake. This is the great tall tale of the American woods, and it is older than most people realize. The story goes that there is a snake that takes its tail in its mouth and rolls down the hill after you like a wagon hoop, faster than a man can run. And when it gets close, it lets go and straightens out like a spear and strikes at you with a poison stinger on the end of its tail. The only way to save yourself, the story goes, is to jump behind a tree, so that the snake hits the tree instead. And then the tree swells up and dies from the poison, and its leaves turn brown, and in some versions of the story it swells up so big that the men cut it down and got enough lumber out of it to build a barn. A man named John Lawson wrote about a rolling snake like that in his book about Carolina, published way back in seventeen hundred and nine. So the hoop snake has been rolling around these woods for more than three hundred years, and nobody has ever caught one.

There is a whole family of old beliefs about milk snakes that crawl into barns and drink milk straight from the cows, and about blacksnakes that will chase a man, and about snakes that can charm a bird out of a tree with their eyes. All folklore. Some of it harmless, some of it just good fun on a porch on a summer evening.

But the old snakebite cures are a different matter. And this is the part of the folklore I want you to really hear, because these are the things Grandpa told you to do.

The oldest one, the one in every Western movie and every old Boy Scout handbook, is cut and suck. You take a knife and cut an X across the fang marks, and then you suck the venom out with your mouth and spit it on the ground. Then there was the tourniquet. You tie a belt or a strip of cloth or a bootlace tight around the arm or the leg, above the bite, to keep the poison from getting to the heart. Then there was the whiskey. Drink a good deal of it, folks said, to fight the poison, and pour some on the bite for good measure. Some people packed the bite in ice. Some people put kerosene on it, or turpentine, or tobacco juice, or a poultice of one kind or another. Folks say the old timers would split a live chicken open and press it against the bite to draw the poison out. I am telling you these as things people did. People really did them, and some people still do.

And here is what the doctors will tell you about every one of them.

Cut and suck does not work. It was discredited decades ago. The venom does not sit in a little pocket under the fang marks, waiting for you to suck it out. It goes into the tissue right away, and you cannot get any meaningful amount of it out with your mouth. What you can do, with a knife, is cut through nerves and tendons and blood vessels in a hand or a foot that is already swelling and already in trouble, and open it up to infection. People have hurt themselves far worse with the knife than the snake hurt them.

The tourniquet is worse. The idea sounds sensible. Keep the poison from spreading. But what a tight tourniquet actually does is cut off the blood to the whole limb below it. The venom of a pit viper does its harm right there in the tissue around the bite, and when you trap it there and starve the limb of blood at the same time, you can cause exactly the kind of damage that ends with a person losing fingers, or a hand, or worse. The medical people who study snakebite have a phrase for the tourniquet. They call it a harmful tradition.

Ice is the same kind of mistake. Freezing a limb that is already swelling and already losing blood flow only adds more damage to the tissue. And whiskey, for all its comforts, thins the blood and speeds up the heart and makes it harder for the doctors to tell what is going on with you when you get there. There is a doctor quoted in an old news story, a man who had learned all of those remedies as a Boy Scout. He said none of it is important, and in fact, those treatments can be harmful. That is about as plain as it can be said.

So I promised you the rule. Here it is. If a copperhead bites you, do not do what Grandpa told you. Here is what to do instead.

First, get away from the snake. Step back out of striking range, so it cannot bite you again, and so nobody else gets bitten trying to kill it. Do not try to catch it or kill it to bring it to the hospital. The doctors do not need the snake. They treat the bite. People get bitten a second time all the time trying to kill the snake that bit them.

Second, stay as calm as you can. I know that is easy to say. But a copperhead bite, as we have seen, is very rarely deadly, and especially not in the first hours. You have time. Panic is not going to help you.

Third, call for help. Call nine one one, or have somebody drive you to the nearest emergency room. If you are in the backcountry, start making your way out, calmly, and send for help if you can. You can also call poison control, and they will talk you through it.

Fourth, take off anything tight on the bitten arm or leg. Rings, watches, bracelets, tight boots. The swelling is going to come, sometimes fast, and a ring on a swollen finger can cut off the blood like a tourniquet. Get it off before the swelling gets there.

Fifth, keep the bitten limb still, and keep it at about the level of your heart. Do not hold it way up over your head and do not let it hang way down. Just keep it still and comfortable.

And then there are the things you do not do. You do not cut it. You do not suck it. You do not put a tourniquet on it. You do not put ice on it. You do not drink alcohol. You do not take a handful of aspirin or other pain pills, which can thin the blood, unless a doctor tells you to. You just get to the hospital.

At the hospital, the doctors will watch the swelling, and they will check your blood, and if the bite is bad enough, they will give you antivenom. The modern antivenom for pit vipers like the copperhead came into use in this country around two thousand, and it has made a great difference. In the old days, before any of that, a bad bite could take a hand. Now, most people bitten by a copperhead who get to a hospital and leave the knife and the whiskey alone come out of it with a sore, swollen limb for a while, and a story to tell.

And one last thing, and it is the strangest piece of plain history in this whole story. Do not pick up a dead snake. Not even one you are sure is dead. Not even one with its head cut off. A pit viper's nerves and reflexes go on working for a while after it dies, and a severed snake head can still bite, and still inject venom, for some time afterward. There was a man in Texas, a few years ago, who cut the head off a rattlesnake in his yard and reached down to pick it up, and the head bit him. He very nearly died from it. So perhaps the old saying about snakes not dying until sundown was not entirely wrong. It was just a little off on the details.

But the best rule of all, the one that would keep almost everybody from ever needing the rest, is the oldest one in the mountains. Watch where you put your hands and your feet. Do not reach where you cannot see. Wear boots in the woods and shoes in the yard at night. Use a flashlight when you walk out in the evening in the summer. Look before you pick up firewood, or move a board, or reach up onto a ledge. Kick the leaves before you sit down. The copperhead is not looking for you. It is trying very hard not to be noticed. Give it the chance, and it will let you go right on by.

And I will add one more thing, which the old folks did not always want to hear. Leave the snake be. A great many snakebites, the doctors and the wildlife people will tell you, happen to people who saw the snake first, and decided to kill it, or catch it, or move it, or poke at it with a stick to see what it would do. A snake that has been left alone very rarely bites anybody. A snake that has been cornered, pinned, or picked up will do what any animal would do. The copperhead also does a good deal of work for us that we never see. It eats mice and rats and voles, the same animals that get into the corn crib and the feed room and the house. A woodpile with a copperhead in it is a woodpile with fewer mice. That does not mean you have to love the snake. It just means you can give it room, and wear your gloves, and look before you reach, and let it go on about its business.

I think about that man in the woodpile, at the beginning of this story, and about his granddaddy's advice. And I do not hold it against the granddaddy. He was doing the best he knew, with what he had been taught, in a time when the nearest doctor might be a day's ride away and there was no such thing as antivenom. People in those days had to do something. And so they cut, and they sucked, and they tied off, and they prayed, and most of the time the patient lived anyway, because most copperhead bites were never going to kill anybody. And so the remedies got the credit, and got passed down, generation after generation.

That is how a lot of folklore gets made. Not out of foolishness, but out of people doing their best in hard places, and remembering what seemed to work. The trouble is that the mountains have changed, and the medicine has changed, and some of what Grandpa knew is no longer the help it once seemed to be. The love behind it has not changed one bit. Just the knife.
