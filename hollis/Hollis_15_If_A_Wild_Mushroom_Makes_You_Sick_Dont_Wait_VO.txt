That woman was fifty-three years old. Doctors in New York wrote up her case in a medical journal, because it shows so clearly how this particular poison works. She had picked the mushrooms herself. Nine hours after she ate them, she came to the emergency room with severe nausea, vomiting, cramps and diarrhea. And the doctors identified what she had eaten as a mushroom that grows all through the eastern woods, from the Appalachian Mountains to the Great Lakes, a mushroom so beautiful and so deadly that the people who know it best call it the destroying angel. She did not survive.

Here is the strangest and cruelest thing about that mushroom, and it is the reason I am telling you this story. The destroying angel does not hurt you right away. It waits. And then, after the worst of the sickness, a great many people who have eaten it start to feel better. They think they are over it. They go home. And that is very often the moment it kills them. By the end of this story, you will understand exactly why that happens, why the old tricks your grandparents may have used to tell a good mushroom from a bad one are worthless, and the one thing to do that turns this from a poisoning that kills about half the people who wait it out into one that nine out of ten people survive. Every person who walks in the woods should know it. So should everybody who has ever been handed a basket of wild mushrooms by a neighbor.

So let us start with the mushroom, because you should know what it looks like.

The destroying angel is not one single mushroom. It is a small group of very close relatives, all in the family the scientists call Amanita. The one most common in the eastern United States has a long Latin name, Amanita bisporigera, and the doctors in that New York case named it. It grows in the summer and early fall, in the hardwood forests and mixed woods of the East and the Midwest, especially under oaks, and especially after a good rain. And it is, I will be honest with you, one of the most beautiful things you will ever see on the forest floor. It is pure white, all over. A smooth white cap, white gills underneath, a tall white stem, with a thin white skirt, a ring, hanging around the upper part of the stem. And at the very bottom, down in the dirt, the base of the stem sits in a kind of cup, a white sac, like the remains of an eggshell. That cup is one of the surest signs of the deadly Amanitas. But here is the trouble. It is usually down under the soil and the leaves. If you snap a mushroom off at ground level instead of digging it up, you never see it.

It helps to understand what a mushroom actually is, because it explains a good deal about where the destroying angel shows up. The mushroom you see standing on the forest floor is not the whole living thing. It is only the fruit. The real body of the fungus is underground, a vast, thin web of threads spread all through the soil, sometimes for yards and yards in every direction. The mushroom pops up out of that web when the conditions are right, mostly after rain, to drop its spores, the way an apple tree puts out apples. And the destroying angel's web underground lives in partnership with the roots of trees, especially oaks. The fungus wraps itself around the fine tips of the tree's roots and helps the tree draw water and minerals out of the soil, and in return the tree feeds the fungus sugar. That is why the destroying angel so often comes up under oaks, and in yards and parks and cemeteries with old oak trees, not only out in the deep woods. A great many people who have been poisoned found it within sight of a house.

The destroying angel has a cousin, the death cap, which came over from Europe and has spread in some parts of this country, and between the two of them, the death cap and the destroying angel, they cause the great majority of deaths from mushroom poisoning in the United States. Some of the references put it at more than nine out of every ten mushroom deaths.

Now, the poison. And this is plain science, so let me give it to you plain.

The destroying angel carries a group of toxins called amatoxins, and the worst of them is one called alpha-amanitin. It is a very strange poison. It does not burn your mouth or upset your stomach right away. It gets into your body quietly, and it goes looking for one organ in particular. Your liver. When it gets inside the cells of the liver, it shuts down the machinery those cells use to make the proteins they need to live. Without that machinery, the liver cells simply die. Not all at once. Over a matter of days. Millions upon millions of them. And the kidneys can be damaged right along with it.

And here is the part that matters most. Cooking does not destroy it. Boiling does not destroy it. Drying and freezing do not destroy it. A destroying angel fried in butter is exactly as deadly as one eaten raw. And it does not take much. A single mushroom can be enough to kill a grown person.

And there is something worth knowing about why we are hearing more about mushroom poisonings these days, not less. In recent years, foraging for wild food has become popular again, in a big way. People who grew up in towns and suburbs, who never once went out in the woods with a grandparent to learn the plants, are going out with a phone app or a book, picking mushrooms, and posting pictures of them. A lot of that is a fine thing, and a lot of those folks are careful. But the doctors and the poison centers have noticed that a phone app that guesses what a mushroom is from a photograph is no substitute for a person who knows, and that the people most likely to get hurt are the ones who are new at it and confident about it. The old mountain foragers learned one or two mushrooms at a time, over years, at somebody's elbow.

So now let me walk you through what happens to a person who eats one, because once you understand the timing, you will understand the rule.

For the first several hours, nothing happens. Six hours, ten hours, sometimes more. You feel fine. You go about your evening. You go to sleep. That delay is the poison's great advantage, because by the time you feel anything at all, the toxin has long since left your stomach and gotten into your blood and your liver. There is nothing left to throw up.

Then the first stage hits, and it hits hard. Violent vomiting. Terrible cramps. Diarrhea. It can last a day or so. A great many people, when this starts, think they have food poisoning, or a stomach virus, and they try to ride it out at home. The woman in New York came in nine hours after her meal.

And then comes the second stage, and this is the one that kills people. After the first violent day, the vomiting and the cramps ease off. The person starts to feel better. Sometimes a good deal better. They can keep down a little water. They think the worst is over. Doctors have a name for this stage. Some of them call it the honeymoon. And all the while, underneath that feeling of getting better, the liver is dying.

Two or three or four days after the meal, the third stage arrives. The liver begins to fail. The skin and the eyes turn yellow. The blood stops clotting the way it should. The kidneys may fail. The person can become confused, and fall into a coma. At that point, the only thing that can save a great many of them is a new liver.

The North American Mycological Association, the folks who keep track of mushroom poisonings in this country, have said that with prompt medical care, the survival rate from this kind of poisoning is ninety percent or better. With little or no medical care, it is fifty percent or less. That is the whole story in two numbers. Get to the hospital early, and you will very likely live. Wait it out at home, and it is a coin toss.

And early really does matter, because the doctors have things they can do in those first days that they cannot do later. They can pour fluids into a person to keep the kidneys flushing and to replace everything lost in that first violent stage. They can use medicines meant to protect the liver and help it hold on. One of them, studied in this country in a clinical trial for exactly this kind of poisoning, is made from milk thistle, an old herbal remedy of the very kind the mountain people would have recognized. They can watch the liver with blood tests every few hours and see the trouble coming before the patient feels it. And if the liver begins to fail anyway, they can get the person onto a transplant list while there is still time. None of that is possible for somebody lying on the couch at home, feeling a little better, waiting for the flu to pass.

I should tell you, too, that most mushroom poisonings in this country are not the deadly kind. Thousands of them are reported every year, and the great majority are people, very often small children, who ate something that gave them a miserable few hours of stomach trouble and nothing worse. That is a good thing. But it is also part of the danger, because it teaches people that a bad mushroom means a bad night and then you get over it. With most mushrooms, that is true. With the destroying angel, it is exactly backward.

And keep an eye on the little ones, and on the dogs. Children will put anything in their mouths, and a white mushroom popping up in the yard after a summer rain is exactly the kind of thing a toddler will pick up and taste. The same goes for dogs, and the veterinarians will tell you that dogs die from the deadly Amanitas too, sometimes after eating just one. If a child or a pet has eaten a wild mushroom and you do not know what it was, you treat it as an emergency, and you bring the mushroom, or one just like it from the same spot, along with you.

There was a woman in Canada, a few years ago, fifty-two years old, who ate mushrooms she found in a park. They were a destroying angel. She went into liver failure, and she needed a liver transplant to live, and she was lucky enough to get one quickly. Her doctor said afterward that if she had not, it could have been much worse. In Mexico, a man who ate destroying angels went into liver failure and was put on machines to keep him alive until he could get a transplant. He died before it could happen.

Now, I said I would let the skeptic speak, and here he is.

The skeptic says, people in these mountains have been picking wild mushrooms for generations. My grandmother picked them every spring of her life and never once got sick. Mushroom deaths are rare. You are scaring people over nothing.

And the skeptic is right that deaths are rare. The mycological association has said that over a period of thirty years, only a handful of deaths from the eastern destroying angel were ever reported to them. Most people who forage for mushrooms do it carefully, and pick the same few kinds they know well, and never have a problem. I am not here to tell you not to love the woods, or not to enjoy a mess of morels in the spring.

But here is my answer to the skeptic. The reason deaths are rare is the hospital. The people who survive are, overwhelmingly, the ones who got to a doctor in time. And the reason I am telling this story is that the destroying angel is built, almost as if on purpose, to keep you from getting to the doctor in time. It waits until it is too late to throw up. It makes you think you have the stomach flu. And then it makes you think you are getting better. A rare poison that kills by fooling you is exactly the kind of thing people need to hear about before it happens, not after.

And there is a second answer to the skeptic, which is about Grandma's rules. Because a great many mountain families had rules for telling a good mushroom from a bad one, and a lot of those rules are still passed around today. And this is where the folklore comes in.

I grew up hearing a good many of them. Folks say that if you cook a mushroom with a silver spoon or a silver coin in the pot, and the silver turns black, the mushroom is poison. Folks say that if an animal has been eating on a mushroom, a squirrel or a deer or a slug, it must be safe for people. Folks say that a poison mushroom will taste bitter or smell bad, so you would know. Folks say that if you can peel the skin off the cap easily, it is safe to eat. Folks say that boiling a mushroom with salt, or vinegar, or an onion, will draw the poison out. Folks say that all white mushrooms are safe, or that all mushrooms growing on wood are safe, or that toadstools are poison and mushrooms are not, as though there were a difference anybody could see.

Every single one of those is false. Not partly false. False. The destroying angel does not turn silver black. Squirrels and slugs eat destroying angels without harm, because their bodies are not built like ours. People who have survived eating the deadly Amanitas have said they did not taste bad at all. You can peel the cap of a destroying angel as easily as any mushroom. No amount of boiling, salting, or vinegar does anything to the poison. And as for white mushrooms being safe, the destroying angel is the whitest mushroom in the woods. So why did those rules last so long? For the same reason the old snakebite cures lasted. Most of the mushrooms people tested with a silver spoon were never dangerous to begin with, and so the spoon got the credit. The few times somebody did get the deadly kind, the sickness came a day later, and nobody connected it to the supper. A rule that seems to work ninety-nine times out of a hundred gets passed down with great confidence. It is the hundredth time that the rule was never built for.

The old country had other mushroom beliefs too, that came over with the settlers, and some of them are wonderful. Folks say a ring of mushrooms in the grass, a fairy ring, is where the fairies danced the night before, and that if you step inside one, you may be taken off to fairyland and never come back, or come back a hundred years later. The scientists will tell you a fairy ring is just a fungus growing outward underground in a circle from where it started, year after year. Folks gave the strange fungi of the woods names that tell you what they thought of them. Witches' butter, for a yellow jelly that grows on dead branches. Dead man's fingers, for a black fungus that pokes up out of rotting wood like the fingertips of something buried. And there is a big orange mushroom that grows in clusters on the stumps of these mountains in the fall, called the jack-o'-lantern, that has a secret. If you take it into a dark room and let your eyes adjust, the gills glow a faint, ghostly green. The old people called glowing wood in the forest foxfire, and they had a great many stories about it. And the jack-o'-lantern mushroom, for all its beauty, is poisonous. It is one of the mushrooms people most often eat by mistake, because it looks a good deal like the chanterelle, a golden mushroom that is one of the finest eating mushrooms in the woods. It will not kill you the way the destroying angel does. But it will make you very sick.

And the mountain people did have mushrooms they knew and loved and ate safely for generations, and I do not want to leave that out. In the spring, all through these mountains, people go out after morels. The honeycomb-capped mushroom that comes up under the poplars and the old apple trees in April and May. In some parts of these mountains, folks call them molly moochers. In parts of Kentucky, they call them dryland fish, because when you slice them and roll them in cornmeal and fry them in a skillet, they taste, the old folks say, about as good as fresh fish. Morel season is a ritual in a great many mountain families, and the patches are guarded as closely as any sang patch. But even morels have a dangerous look-alike, the false morel, with a brainy, wrinkled, lumpy cap instead of the honeycomb pits, and it carries a poison of its own. The old morel hunters always cut every one in half, lengthwise. A true morel is hollow all the way through. A false one is not.

And I want to say a word for the old foragers, because they deserve it. In the hard years in these mountains, during the Depression and before it, the woods were the grocery store for a great many families. Poke salad and ramps and creasy greens in the spring. Morels and berries and wild grapes. Hickory nuts and black walnuts and chestnuts in the fall. Folks knew the woods because they had to, and they learned them from their parents, one plant at a time, by being shown, and by being told which ones never to touch. That kind of knowledge, passed hand to hand over a lifetime, is a very different thing from a picture in a book. The old foragers who lived to be old did it by being humble about what they did not know. They ate the few mushrooms they were sure of, and they left the rest of the forest alone.

So let me keep my promise. Here is the rule. It is the title of this story. If a wild mushroom makes you sick, even the next day, do not wait it out.

What that means, in plain terms, is this. If you or anybody in your family gets sick after eating wild mushrooms, at any point, you go to the emergency room. Right then. Not in the morning. Not after you see if it passes. And especially if the sickness starts late, six hours or more after the meal, because that late start is the warning sign of the deadly kind. You do not let anybody tell you it is just a stomach bug. And if you start to feel better after the first bad day, you do not go home and forget about it. Feeling better is not the same as getting better. The doctors need to check your liver, with blood tests, over several days, and they need to start treatment as early as possible. That is what turns a coin toss into nine chances out of ten.

Bring the mushrooms with you, if there are any left, raw or cooked. Bring the peelings, the scraps, the ones still in the basket. The doctors and the experts can often identify what you ate, and that tells them how to treat you. A good many hospitals and poison centers in this country can call on volunteer mushroom experts, people from the mycological clubs who know their fungi the way a birdwatcher knows birds, and who will look at a photograph or a scrap of cap in the middle of the night to help a doctor figure out what a patient ate. Those folks have saved a great many lives without anybody ever hearing their names. But they can only help if there is something to look at. And call poison control on the way. The number is on the internet, and it is a good number to have written down on the refrigerator in any house.

And the rule underneath that rule, the one that keeps you from ever needing it. Never eat a wild mushroom unless you are absolutely, completely sure what it is. Not fairly sure. Not pretty sure because it looks like the picture in the book. Not sure because your neighbor said so. If you want to learn mushrooms, learn them from somebody who really knows, in person, out in the woods, with the mushroom in your hand, dug up whole so you can see the base. Many states have mushroom clubs, full of folks who would love nothing better than to teach you. Start with the few that have no deadly look-alikes. And if there is ever any doubt at all, throw it out. As the old saying goes, there are old mushroom hunters, and there are bold mushroom hunters, but there are no old, bold mushroom hunters.

I think about that woman at the beginning of this story, sometimes, coming home from the woods with her basket of beautiful white mushrooms. And I think about her going to bed that night feeling perfectly fine, with the clock already running. It is about the quietest danger in the whole forest. No teeth, no fangs, no cliff edge, no rising water. Just something pale and lovely standing up out of the leaves after a summer rain, waiting to be picked.

The destroying angel is out there right now, in the woods behind a great many of your houses, from late spring into the fall. Most of us will walk past it all our lives and never give it a thought. But now you know its name. And now you know the one thing that matters most. When in doubt, throw it out. And if anybody ever gets sick, do not wait.
