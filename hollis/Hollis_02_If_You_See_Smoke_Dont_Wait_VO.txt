The evening before Thanksgiving, high on the Chimney Tops. Two bare stone peaks stick up out of the forest like the knuckles of a fist, and the last hikers of the day are coming down off them in the cold. The leaves underfoot are dry as paper. It has not rained properly in weeks. Somewhere near the top, a thin line of smoke goes straight up into still air, and nobody pays it much mind, because it is small, and because it is a long way from anywhere. Five days later, that same smoke will be standing over a town of four thousand people, and the wind behind it will be blowing harder than a hurricane.

That fire has a name now. They call it Chimney Tops Two. It was reported in the Great Smoky Mountains National Park at about half past five in the evening on Wednesday, the twenty-third of November, two thousand sixteen. By the time it was done, fourteen people were dead, fourteen thousand had fled their homes, around two thousand four hundred buildings were damaged or destroyed, and something like seventeen thousand acres of mountain had burned black. Most of the dying happened in a single night, on Monday the twenty-eighth, in and around the town of Gatlinburg, Tennessee.

I want to tell you how a fire that small, in a place that remote, ended up in the middle of a town full of tourists and pancake houses and wedding chapels. I want to tell you what the people who lived through it said about the moment they knew. I am going to give you a little of what the old mountain people believed about fire, which is its own kind of wisdom, and I will mark it as folklore when I do. And at the end, I am going to give you the one rule that this story teaches more plainly than any story I know. It is a short rule. But almost everybody who died that night died because, for one reason or another, they did not have time to follow it. Stay with me and you will understand why.

So start with the autumn of that year, because the fire did not start on a match. It started on a drought.

The fall of two thousand sixteen was one of the driest anybody in the southern mountains could remember. Rain just stopped coming. Week after week, the forecasts said maybe, and then nothing fell. The springs got low. The creeks went down to trickles and then to dry rocks. By November, the whole southern Appalachian chain was in what the weather people called exceptional drought, which is the worst category they have. There were wildfires burning all over the region that fall, in Georgia, in North Carolina, in Tennessee, dozens of them. Some days the smoke lay so thick over the valleys that you could look straight at the sun at noon and it was just a dull orange coin. Burn bans were up everywhere. People were told not to so much as light a cigarette outside.

Now, in a normal year, the leaves come down in October and November and lie there soaked and matted on the forest floor, and they do not burn worth anything. That year, they came down onto dry ground and they stayed dry. Every hollow in the mountains filled up knee-deep with what amounted to kindling. That is the stage. Hold it in your mind.

And it was not only the Smokies. That same fall there were big fires burning in places a lot of you will know. There was one in the Cohutta Wilderness down in north Georgia, the Rough Ridge fire, that burned for weeks across the ridges. There was one up on Party Rock, above Lake Lure in North Carolina, where folks had to clear out of homes along the lake. The firefighters that season were stretched from one end of the mountains to the other, crews brought in from out west, from all over the country, sleeping in their trucks and moving from one fire to the next. When people tell this story now, they sometimes talk as if Chimney Tops was the only fire burning. It was not. It was one of dozens, in a season that had already worn everybody down to the bone before the worst night ever came.

The Chimney Tops Trail is one of the most popular trails in the park. It goes up from the road over the mountain, the Newfound Gap Road, and climbs steeply to those two rock peaks. In those years people used to scramble right up onto the bare rock at the top, though the park has since closed off the very summit. On the afternoon of the twenty-third of November, according to what came out later, a hiker on that trail took a photograph of two teenage boys near the top. The accounts that were reported say the boys were seen dropping lit matches. That evening, a fire was reported near the summit.

That part is plain history. What happened over the next four days is where people still argue, and I want to give it to you straight, because this is where the skeptic speaks up, and he has a point.

The skeptic says, well, why on earth did they not just put it out? It was a small fire. It was on a mountaintop in a national park, with rangers and firefighters and helicopters available. Why let it burn for days?

And here is what the records show. The fire was on a very steep, very rugged spot, a knob of rock surrounded by dense, thick growth, the kind of slope where you cannot safely put a crew of firefighters on the ground, especially at night, especially in the dark. The park's fire managers looked at it and made a decision that fire managers make all the time in the mountains. Rather than send people onto that terrain, they would try to contain it. They would draw a box around it, using trails and roads and creeks and natural barriers, and let it burn down inside that box, where they expected the landscape itself to hold it. Between Thursday and Saturday, the twenty-fourth to the twenty-sixth, crews worked on that containment area lower down the mountain, where they believed they had a better chance of stopping it.

The fire grew slowly in those days. It was still a small fire by most measures. People in Gatlinburg could see the smoke, but they had been seeing smoke all month. There were fires burning in every direction that fall. A little more smoke over the Chimneys did not stand out.

Then on Sunday the weather began to turn. The humidity dropped. An inversion that had been holding the air still over the mountains lifted. The fire began to move more. And on Sunday evening and into Monday, a powerful storm system was coming in from the west, the kind of system that, on the way over the mountains, can push wind down the slopes on the other side at tremendous speeds.

Historians and fire people still argue about whether the park should have been more aggressive on that first day or the second. A review was done afterward. Lawsuits were filed later by families who lost people, and in the end, a federal judge threw those suits out, on the grounds that the government's choices in fighting the fire were protected by the law. People in Sevier County have feelings about all of that, and they are entitled to them. I am not going to tell you who was right. I am going to tell you what happened when the wind came.

Monday, the twenty-eighth of November, two thousand sixteen.

The morning was smoky and strange. The wind came up during the day and kept on coming. By the afternoon, the fire on the Chimney Tops had jumped its boundaries. The wind took the burning leaves and the bits of bark and the glowing embers off that mountain and simply carried them, a mile or more, and dropped them down onto other mountainsides, where every one of them landed on that dry kindling and started a brand new fire. Firefighters call those spot fires. That evening there were spot fires starting everywhere, ahead of the main fire, in places nobody could have predicted.

By the evening, the wind was blowing a sustained forty to fifty miles an hour, and the records show gusts measured as high as eighty-seven miles an hour. Some accounts put them higher, over ninety. That wind took down trees all over the hills above Gatlinburg. And when the trees came down, they took down power lines. And when the power lines came down, onto dry leaves, in that wind, they started fires of their own.

So now you had fire coming in on the wind from the mountain, and fire starting up out of the ground from the power lines, in a dozen different places at once, and darkness coming on, and a town full of people who had driven up for the holiday week.

Around six o'clock that evening, according to the timelines put together afterward, the fire came down on Gatlinburg from more than one side.

I want to tell you what that looked like to the people who were there, as best the accounts let me.

The town of Gatlinburg sits in a narrow valley, a slot in the mountains, with the main road running up the middle and the hillsides on both sides covered with cabins and rental chalets and motels and houses. A great many of those cabins are up on little roads that wind back and forth up the slopes, narrow roads, one lane in places, the kind of roads where you meet a car coming the other way and somebody has to back up. That is a lovely way to build a vacation town. It is a terrible way to build a town you have to evacuate in the dark.

People up on those hillsides that night have said it went from smoky to burning in what felt like minutes. One minute you could see a glow on the ridge. The next minute the trees across the road were on fire, and burning leaves were blowing against the windows like snow. People got into their cars and found the road ahead blocked by fallen trees. People turned around and found the road behind blocked by fire. The wind was so loud you could not hear yourself think. The power was out. The cell towers were going down. People called nine one one and could not get through, or got through and were told that help was coming, and help could not get to them.

There is a story that came out in the days after, of an elderly couple, both eighty-two years old, married for fifty-seven years, who got out of their house and walked down the side of the mountain in the dark, through the smoke, holding onto each other, with the fire burning around them. They made it. There were a lot of stories like that. People who ran. People who drove through smoke so thick they could not see the hood of the car, with fire on both sides of the road. People who got out with nothing but the clothes on their backs.

And there were the fourteen who did not make it.

I am not going to read you their names and ages one by one. Their families have heard this story told enough times. But I will tell you that among them were older people who could not move fast. Among them was a mother and her two daughters, whose husband and father was not with them when the fire came, and who lost all three of them that night. He spoke to reporters afterward, and he later went to court over it, and those of you who heard him speak will not have forgotten it. The accounts say some of the dead were found in their homes, and some out on the roads, trying to get away. Most of them died in that same handful of hours on Monday evening, when the fire came in faster than anybody thought a fire could.

And here is the part that still makes people in Sevier County angry, and it is plain history. The evacuation alert never went out to people's phones. The records show that the plan was for an emergency alert to be sent through the state system to every cell phone in the area. But the command post did not get the request through to the state until about half past eight that night, and by then the phone and internet and power lines that the agencies needed to communicate were down. Park officials called it a communications failure. A citywide evacuation order did go out over the radio and the television, but not until about nine o'clock that night. Hours after the fire was already burning buildings.

So a lot of people sat in their cabins that evening looking at the smoke and the glow and waiting to be told. Waiting for somebody to say, now. And the word did not come in time.

That is the heart of this story. I will come back to it at the end. But first I want to take you somewhere older, because these mountains have been burning for a very long time, and the people who lived here before us had a lot to say about fire.

The Cherokee had a story about where fire came from, and it is one of my favorite of all the old stories. James Mooney wrote it down in the eighteen hundreds, among a great many others, and here is how the story goes. In the beginning, there was no fire, and the world was cold. Then the Thunders, who lived up beyond the sky, sent their lightning down and set fire to the bottom of a hollow sycamore tree that grew on an island. The animals could see the smoke coming out of the top of the tree, and they wanted that fire, but the tree was on an island, and there was water all around it.

So the animals held a council. And the Raven offered to go, because he was large and strong. He flew across the water and lit on the sycamore tree, and while he was wondering what to do, the heat scorched all his feathers black, and he was frightened and came back without the fire. And that, the story goes, is why the raven is black. Then the little Screech Owl volunteered, and when he looked down into the hollow tree, a blast of hot air came up and nearly burned out his eyes, and that, the story goes, is why his eyes are red to this day. Then the Hooting Owl and the Horned Owl went, and the smoke and the ashes left white rings around their eyes. And then the black racer snake swam over, and got into the tree through a little hole at the bottom, and the heat and the smoke drove him crazy, so that he darted around in circles, and came out burnt black, and the story goes that is why the black racer still twists and doubles back when he moves. One by one, the animals tried, and one by one the fire marked them.

Finally the little Water Spider said she would go. She ran across the top of the water to the island, and she spun a thread out of her own body and wove it into a little bowl, which she fastened on her back. Then she got into the tree and put one tiny coal of fire into her bowl and carried it back across the water. And that is how the world got fire.

I have always loved that story because every creature who goes after the fire comes back marked by it. Nobody gets near fire without it leaving something on them. The old people understood that.

And then the settlers came, and they had their own relationship with fire in these hills, and it was a working relationship. For generations, a lot of mountain families burned the woods on purpose. In the late winter or early spring, folks would set fire to the leaf litter on the slopes around their places and let it creep along the ground. They said it cleaned the woods out. They said it killed the ticks and the chiggers and ran off the snakes. They said it brought up green grass for the cattle that ranged loose on the mountain, and opened the woods so you could see game. That is documented, and it is plain history, and for a long time the government fought hard against it, and called it woods arson. Folks say a mountain man would burn his own ridge every spring and then swear up and down to the forest ranger that he never saw a thing. I grew up hearing that kind of story told with a grin.

And here is the strange part. Some of the people who study these forests today will tell you the old woods burners were not entirely wrong. Some of the trees up here actually need fire. There is a pine that grows on the dry ridges in the southern mountains, the Table Mountain pine, that keeps a lot of its cones closed up tight, and many of them only open properly when a fire comes through and heats them. The oaks that make up so much of these mountains came up for thousands of years in woods that burned now and again, low and slow. When fire was taken out of these mountains almost entirely, the leaf litter piled up, and the thick young growth crowded in, and the forest changed. The park and the forest service do their own planned burning now, in the right season and the right weather, because they have come around to understanding that fire belongs in these woods.

But that kind of fire, the low kind that creeps along the ground on a damp spring morning, is a different animal from what came off the Chimney Tops. A fire in drought, in a high wind, is not a thing you manage. It is a thing you get out of the way of.

I want to say a word about why, because it is the part most of us who live in flat country, or who only come up to the mountains on vacation, never think about.

Fire on a mountain behaves differently than fire on flat ground. Heat rises. So when a fire is burning at the bottom of a slope, all the heat coming off it goes up the hill ahead of it, drying out the leaves and the brush above, warming them up, getting them ready. The fire climbs a slope much faster than it can creep across level ground, and the steeper the slope, the faster it goes. And the hollows up here, those narrow V-shaped valleys running up the side of every mountain, act like the flue of a chimney. Air gets pulled up through them, and the fire gets pulled right along with it. Firefighters have a healthy fear of what they call chimneys and saddles in the mountains, for exactly that reason.

Now add the wind. In a high wind, a fire stops being one fire. Every piece of burning bark and every curled leaf that gets lifted up off the ground becomes a little torch flying through the air. Most of them burn out before they land. But on a night like that one, with the woods that dry, it only takes a few. They land a quarter mile ahead, a half mile, sometimes further, and every one that catches is a new fire, starting up behind you, or beside you, or across the only road out. That is why people that night felt like the fire came out of nowhere. In a sense, it did. It came out of the air.

So when you are in the mountains and the woods are dry and the wind is up, you have to stop thinking about where the fire is. You have to start thinking about where the fire could be in the next ten minutes. Because that is a much bigger place.

And that brings me back to the promise I made you at the start.

When people went back afterward and talked to the survivors of that night, one thing came up again and again. The ones who made it out with time to spare were very often the ones who left before they were told. They looked at the smoke and the glow on the ridge, and they felt that wind, and something in them said, this is wrong, and they did not wait around to find out how wrong. They got in the car and went down the mountain while the roads were still open. I would bet a good many of them felt foolish doing it, the way anybody feels foolish leaving a warm cabin over a little smoke. That feeling did not last the night.

The ones who had the closest calls, and some of the ones who did not make it, were very often the ones who waited. Waited for the alert. Waited for the knock on the door. Waited for somebody official to say the word. And the hard lesson of that night is that in a fast fire, in a high wind, in the mountains, the word may never come. Not because nobody cares. Because the phones are down, and the trees are across the road, and the people whose job it is to tell you are fighting for their own lives in the dark.

So here is the rule, and it is the title of this story. If you see smoke in the mountains, do not wait. If you are in a cabin, or a campground, or a trailhead parking lot, and you see smoke building, and the wind is up, and the woods are dry, then you go. You go early. You go while it still feels like too early. You know two ways out of wherever you are staying, before you ever unpack. You keep your gas tank above half. You keep your shoes by the door and your keys where you can find them in the dark. If you are hiking and you smell smoke you cannot account for, you turn around and head back toward the trailhead and the road, not up toward the view. And you remember that a fire on a mountain runs uphill faster than you ever could, and that a wind can carry it a mile in a moment.

You will feel foolish sometimes. You will leave a cabin you paid good money for, and sit in a diner down the road, and the fire will never come. Feel foolish. Foolish is fine. Foolish is the best thing that can happen to you.

The charges against the two teenagers were dropped in June of two thousand seventeen. The district attorney said that the investigation had found too many separate fires, from too many separate causes, including the power lines that came down in the wind, and that it would be impossible to prove which fire caused which death or which building lost. So legally, nobody was ever held to answer for it. That is how it ended in the courts, and I will not tell you how to feel about it.

Gatlinburg came back. A town like that always does. The cabins were rebuilt, many of them. The tourists came back. Dolly Parton, who grew up in that same county, set up a fund that sent money every month to families who lost their homes, and folks there have not forgotten it. And the Chimney Tops are still there, two stone knuckles sticking up out of the forest. If you go up the trail today, you can still see the burn scars in places, the black trunks standing among the new growth, the gaps on the slopes where the trees did not come back.

I think about the Water Spider in that old story, sometimes, carrying one little coal in a bowl on her back. One little coal. That is all fire ever is, at the beginning. The Raven did not know what he was flying toward. Neither did the owls. And every one of them came back marked. The mountains have always known what fire is. It is only us, who come and go, who forget.
