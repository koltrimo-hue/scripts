A gravel pull-off on a mountain highway in western North Carolina, long after dark. A dozen people stand along a low stone wall, bundled up against the cold, talking in whispers like they are in church. Out across a deep black valley lies a long, low ridge, flat on top, with nothing on it. No houses. No roads. No lights. They have been watching it for two hours. Somebody yawns. Somebody says it is time to go. And then, just above the dark line of the ridge, a light comes up. Round. Orange. Silent. It hangs there for a few seconds, steady as a lamp. Then it simply goes out.

That ridge is Brown Mountain. It runs along the line between Burke County and Caldwell County, in the foothills of the Blue Ridge, at the edge of the Pisgah National Forest, not far from the deep cut in the earth they call the Linville Gorge. And for more than a hundred years, people have been standing on overlooks around it at night, watching for the lights. The Brown Mountain Lights. They may be the most famous mystery lights in America. They have been investigated by the government twice. They have been written into a bluegrass song that half the pickers in the country know. And as of tonight, nobody has given an answer that everybody accepts.

I am going to give you the three kinds of answers people have offered over the years. The old stories, which I will tell you as stories, plainly marked. The government's answer, which I will give you fairly, because it deserves to be heard. And the answer that a couple of scientists came up with not very long ago, which nobody quite expected. And at the end of it, I am going to tell you why the people who know that country best have always given the same piece of advice about those lights, whatever they are. Never follow them. You will understand it by the time we are done.

Let me start with the plain history, the part we can put a date on.

The first time the lights show up in print, as far as anybody has found, is in September of nineteen thirteen. The Charlotte Daily Observer ran a piece about mysterious lights that people had been seeing rise up over the summit of Brown Mountain at night. Now, you have to understand what that country was like back then. It was remote. There were no paved highways running through it. There was a railroad down in the Catawba River valley and some logging and a few small communities scattered through the hollows, and a great deal of wild, steep, empty land. When people in those communities said they had seen lights rising over a mountain that nobody lived on, it got attention.

Now, some folks will tell you the lights go back a lot further than nineteen thirteen, and I want to deal with that honestly. You will read in a great many places that a German engineer named Gerard de Brahm, who surveyed in the southern colonies before the Revolution, wrote about strange lights in the North Carolina mountains in seventeen seventy-one. The quote that gets passed around has him saying that the mountains give off nitrous vapors, which are carried by the wind, and that when those winds meet, the vapors catch fire. It is a wonderful old quote, and it shows up on just about every sign and website about Brown Mountain. But historians argue about this one. Some of the people who have gone back and looked closely say that de Brahm was not writing about Brown Mountain at all, and may not have been describing anything anybody actually saw there. So I give it to you as a thing people say, and not as plain history. The guide to North Carolina that the government writers put together in the nineteen thirties, during the Depression, also says the lights were seen before the Civil War. That may well be so. But the first time we can put our finger on them in a newspaper, with a date, is nineteen thirteen.

It got enough attention that a United States congressman from North Carolina, a man named E. Y. Webb, asked the federal government to look into it. And in October of nineteen thirteen, the United States Geological Survey sent a man down to Brown Mountain. His name was D. B. Sterrett. He spent a few days in the area, looking around and watching. And he came back with an answer.

He said the lights were locomotive headlights. Trains running through the Catawba Valley beyond the mountain, their bright headlamps being seen from certain places over the top of the ridge, so that it looked like a light was rising up off Brown Mountain itself.

And here is the skeptic's case, and I want to put it to you as strong as I can before we go any further, because it is a good case. Out there in that dark, with no houses on the ridge and nothing to judge distance by, a light ten or twenty miles away looks exactly like a light sitting right on the ridgeline. Your eye cannot tell the difference. A headlight coming around a curve on a far valley floor will seem to appear suddenly, hang for a moment, and then wink out when the train turns away. That is exactly what people describe. It is a perfectly ordinary explanation for a perfectly ordinary thing, and it fits what was there to be seen. Sterrett was a trained man doing his job.

But the people around Brown Mountain did not care for that answer. And three years later, something happened that made a lot of them throw it out entirely.

In the summer of nineteen sixteen, a great flood came through western North Carolina. It was one of the worst floods in the history of the mountains. Rivers came up higher than anybody had ever seen. Bridges went out. Railroads were washed away. For a good long while after that flood, the trains through that part of the country were not running at all, because there was no track left for them to run on.

And the lights kept coming.

That is what the people who lived there said. There is a letter in the record, dated the twenty-ninth of August, nineteen sixteen, from a hotel owner in that area named G. A. Loven, that was printed in the Lenoir newspaper in September. And in it, he insisted that the Brown Mountain lights had gone right on appearing during the time when the trains were not running. If there were no trains, he said, then the lights could not be train headlights. Even some of the people who had gone along with the government's explanation began to doubt it after that.

Now, the skeptic has an answer to that too. He will say that one man's letter in a local paper is not proof of anything. He will say that people who want to see a light will see a light. He will say that there are a great many kinds of lights in a valley at night besides trains. And that is fair. But the people of Burke County had a point to make, and they made it, and it was printed in the newspapers of the day.

And the skeptic has one more thing to say, and it is worth hearing. By the time the second government man came, in nineteen twenty-two, the automobile had arrived in those mountains. Roads were being cut through the valleys. Every year there were more cars on them, at night, with their lamps on. If the lights had been a rare thing in nineteen thirteen, the skeptic says, then you would expect them to get more common as the roads came in, and that is just about what people reported. A man standing on a mountain looking out over thirty miles of dark country, with cars and trains and houses scattered all through it, is going to see lights. The question was never really whether there were lights. The question was whether any of them were something more.

So in nineteen twenty-two, the government sent another man. This one was named George R. Mansfield, also with the Geological Survey. And he went about it more carefully. He spent time in the area, he watched the lights on a number of nights, and he used a map and a surveying instrument called an alidade, which is a kind of sighting telescope that lets you take an exact bearing on something far away. Every time he saw a light, he took its direction and then checked the map to see what was out there along that line.

And here is what he concluded. He said that about half of the lights he saw were the headlights of automobiles, on the roads in the valleys beyond. About a third of them were locomotive headlights. And the rest were stationary lights, lamps in houses, and brush fires, and things of that kind. His report said, in so many words, that the Brown Mountain lights were clearly not of unusual nature or origin. That report was printed, and then it was reprinted by the government decades later, in the nineteen seventies. As far as the official record goes, that is the government's answer. Headlights, trains, house lamps, and fires.

And for a lot of people, that settled it. But not for everybody. Not by a long way. Because by nineteen twenty-two, Brown Mountain already had something that no government report can take away from a place. It had its stories.

I am going to tell you some of them now, and I want you to hear them for what they are. These are folk stories. Some of them are old and some of them are not so old, and nobody can prove a word of any of them.

The oldest one, folks say, goes back before there were any white settlers in that country at all. The story goes that a long time ago there was a great battle fought on Brown Mountain, between the Cherokee and the Catawba, and that a great many warriors were killed there on the mountainside. And the lights, the story goes, are the spirits of the women of those nations, carrying torches, searching through the night for their husbands and their sons who never came home. Some tellers put a date on that battle, hundreds of years ago. Historians argue about whether there was any such battle at all, and I would not lean on it. But it is the oldest story people tell about those lights, and you will hear it at every overlook.

There is another story, and it has a darker turn to it. The story goes that around eighteen fifty, a woman living near Brown Mountain disappeared. Some tellers call her Belinda. And folks said her husband had done away with her, because he had taken up with another woman. A search was made, and the people searching the mountains at night began to see strange lights moving out on the ridge, and some of them said it was her spirit, come back to haunt the man who killed her, or to lead the searchers to her. And the story goes that years later, a pile of bones was found under a cliff on the mountain, a woman's skeleton, under a heap of stones. The husband, the story goes, was long gone by then. Nobody can prove any of it. I have never seen a court record or a newspaper from that time that names her. But every family I ever heard tell it told it the same way, with the lights leading somebody to the bones.

And then there is the one in the song. Scotty Wiseman, who was a North Carolina man and part of the old radio duo Lulu Belle and Scotty, wrote a song called The Brown Mountain Light, and it has been recorded by a great many people since, the Kingston Trio among them, and the Country Gentlemen, and a good many bluegrass bands. The song tells of a man who went hunting up on Brown Mountain long ago and got lost and never came home. And an old enslaved man who had worked for him went up the mountain with a lantern, night after night, to look for him, and went on looking long after there was any hope, until he died himself. And the light you see on the ridge, the song says, is that old man still out there with his lantern, still searching for the master who never came back.

You will notice something about every one of those stories. In every single one of them, the light is somebody looking for somebody who is lost. Warriors' wives, searching for the dead. Searchers, looking for a missing woman. An old man with a lantern looking for a lost hunter. That is what the people of those mountains made of the lights. Not monsters. Not spaceships. A search party that never ends.

I grew up hearing a lot of stories about lights out in the woods at night, and so did a lot of you, wherever you come from. It is one of the oldest kinds of story there is. In the old country, in England and Ireland and Scotland, where a great many mountain families came from, they had a name for them. Will-o'-the-wisp. Jack-o'-lantern. Foxfire, some places, though that is a different thing up here. A light out over the bog or the marsh at night, bobbing along, just ahead of you. And the old warning that went with it was always the same. Do not follow it. Folks said the light would lead a traveler off the path, deeper and deeper into the marsh, always just out of reach, until he was lost or drowned in the mire. The story goes that the light was a wandering spirit, or a trickster, or the soul of somebody who could not rest. Our mountain people brought that warning with them across the ocean. And when they saw lights over Brown Mountain, they already knew what you were supposed to do. You stayed on the road. You did not go after them.

Now, before I tell you why that old warning is better advice than people realize, let me give you the third kind of answer. Because the story of the lights did not end with George Mansfield.

People never stopped watching. After the highways came, the state put in an overlook on Highway one eighty-one, up north of Morganton, specifically for people who wanted to see the lights. There is another view of the ridge from a place called Wiseman's View, on the rim of the Linville Gorge, and others along the Blue Ridge Parkway. On a clear autumn night you will find people out at all of them, in folding chairs, with thermoses of coffee. Some of them see nothing at all, night after night. Some of them see lights they are sure are headlights. And some of them see something they cannot account for.

The descriptions that come back are a strange mix. Some people see a single light. Some see several at once. They are described as white, red, yellow, orange, and blue. Some are as small as a candle flame. Some look like balls of fire. Some hang still. Some rise up from the ridge into the sky, and some drift low along the slope, and some seem to split apart. Some flare up and go out in a second. Some last for minutes.

And in the summer of two thousand sixteen, a couple of scientists from Appalachian State University, up in Boone, published something that surprised a lot of people. Their names were Daniel Caton and Lee Hawkins, from the physics and astronomy department. They had put out a set of cameras near Brown Mountain, time-lapse cameras running through the night, in more than one location, so that if anything showed up they could compare the angles. And on the night of the sixteenth of July, two of their cameras both caught the same light at the same time. A round, glowing light, that came on and went off almost instantly, four times over a few minutes.

And Dr. Caton said that they had gone through the things that could have explained it, the man-made sources and the natural ones, and that they could not find a match. He did not say it was a ghost. He was careful about that. He said one possibility they were thinking about was something like ball lightning, a rare kind of lightning that forms into a glowing ball and drifts for a few seconds before it disappears. A thing that real scientists have seen and recorded, and still do not fully understand. He said they wanted to keep watching.

So that is where it stands today. The government said headlights, trains, house lamps and fires, and for a great many of the lights people have seen over the years, the government was very likely right. The old stories say a search party of the dead, out with their lanterns. And a pair of physicists with cameras say that on at least one night, they caught something they could not explain. I am not going to tell you which to believe. I will tell you that all three can be true at the same time. Most of the lights can be headlights, and a few of them can be something else.

And I would be leaving out a piece of the history if I did not tell you that Brown Mountain has drawn some characters over the years. Back in the nineteen fifties and sixties, there was a man named Ralph Lael, who had been in the furniture business down around Hickory, and who opened up a little roadside museum on Highway one eighty-one, near the mountain, that he called the Outer Space Rock Shop. He told a magazine that the lights were made by beings from another world, that they had taken him inside Brown Mountain, and later, he said, on a trip to the planet Venus. He kept what he said was a mummified alien in a glass case in his shop, and people would drive out to see it. After he died, the accounts say, it went missing, and all that is left of it is a few blurry photographs. I do not tell you that to make fun of the man. I tell you because it is part of the story of that mountain, and because it shows you how strong the pull of those lights can be. When a thing goes unexplained long enough, people will fill the empty space with whatever they have.

But I promised you I would tell you why the people who know that country always said never follow the lights, and here it is. And it has nothing to do with ghosts.

Look at where those lights appear. Brown Mountain sits right alongside some of the wildest, steepest, most broken country in the eastern United States. The Linville River comes down off the high mountains and cuts its way through a gorge something like thirteen miles long, and in that stretch it drops nearly two thousand feet. The Forest Service set it aside as a wild area all the way back in nineteen fifty-one, one of the first places in the East to get that kind of protection, and it has been kept wild ever since. There are no roads down in it. There are no lights down in it. There is nothing down in it at night but rock and water and trees. It is so deep and so rugged that people call it the Grand Canyon of the East. The walls of that gorge drop off in cliffs. The trails down into it are steep, and rough, and some of them are not much more than a scramble over rocks and roots. There are places where you can step off the edge of a ledge in the dark and fall a very long way. The river at the bottom is swift and cold and full of boulders. The forest is thick with laurel and rhododendron, the kind of thicket the old people called a hell, because if you got into one you might not get out. Cell phones do not work in a lot of it. And at night, out there, there is no light at all except what you carry.

Every year, people get lost in that country, and get hurt in it, and the rescue squads in Burke County and the surrounding counties have to go in after them, often in the dark, often carrying somebody out on a litter up a slope you would not want to climb in daylight. That is not a legend. That is the ordinary work of the people who keep that country.

And the rescue people will all tell you the same thing about getting lost out there at night. If you ever do find yourself off the trail in the dark, in country like that, the worst thing you can do is keep moving. You stop. You sit down. You put on every warm thing you have. You answer when people call for you, and you blow a whistle if you carry one, three blasts at a time. You wait for daylight. A person who stays in one place is a whole lot easier to find than a person who is wandering around in the laurel trying to get somewhere, and a whole lot less likely to walk off a ledge they never saw. That is the same lesson those old stories were trying to teach, from the other end. The light out in the dark is not your friend. Stay where you are.

And here is what those old stories really were, if you think about it. They were a warning wrapped up in a ghost. Do not go after lights in the dark. Do not leave the road. Do not go down off the overlook into the woods because you think you saw something on the ridge and you want to get closer. The light is further away than it looks. It always is. The mountain is steeper than it looks. The gorge is deeper than it looks. And the woman in the story who disappeared on that mountain was found under a cliff.

And the pull to go looking is real. Anybody who has stood at that wall and seen something come up over the ridge knows the feeling. You want to get closer. You want the picture nobody else has got. You want to be the one who finally settles it. That feeling is exactly what the old warning was made for.

So stay at the overlook. Bring a chair and a thermos and a warm coat. Watch the ridge. If something comes up over it, round and orange and silent, watch it for as long as it lasts. Take a picture if you can. And then let it go.

I think about that old man in the song, sometimes, going up the mountain with his lantern every night, looking for somebody who was never coming home. I think about the women in the old story, carrying torches across the battlefield. Every story those mountains ever told about the lights was about a search that never ended. And I think the people who made up those stories understood something about that country that the government men did not need to understand. That it is a place where people get lost. Where people go out after dark looking for somebody and do not come back themselves.

Whatever the lights are, headlights or lightning or something nobody has a name for yet, the advice is the same as it was a hundred years ago, and three hundred years ago, and in the old country before that. Watch them from the road. Do not follow them in.
