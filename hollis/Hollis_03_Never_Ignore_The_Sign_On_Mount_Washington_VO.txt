Treeline, on a trail in the White Mountains of New Hampshire. Below, the last of the spruce trees, bent and stunted, no taller than a man's waist. Above, nothing but broken gray rock going up into cloud. And right there at the edge, nailed to a post, a yellow sign. Big black letters. It says stop. It says the area ahead has the worst weather in America. It says many have died there from exposure, even in the summer. It says turn back now if the weather is bad. Most people who reach that sign read it, take a picture of it, and keep on walking. Some of them are still up there in the records, by name.

That sign stands on the trails leading up to Mount Washington, the highest peak in the northeastern United States, and every word on it is true. I want to tell you about three people who went past that sign, or past the place where a sign like it would one day stand. One in eighteen forty-nine. One in eighteen fifty-five. And one not that long ago, in two thousand fifteen, a young woman with every piece of modern gear money could buy. I want to tell you what the old people who lived in the shadow of that mountain believed about it, long before there was a trail or a sign. And by the end I am going to answer the question that every one of these stories raises. How can a mountain that is not even very high, by the standards of the world, kill so many people, and kill them in summer? When you have the answer to that, you will understand why the sign says what it says. Stay with me.

Now, I said the mountain is not very high, and that is the first thing the skeptic will say, so let me say it for him. Mount Washington is six thousand two hundred and eighty-eight feet tall. That is a fine mountain for New England. But out west, in Colorado, there are towns higher than that. There are highways that go higher than that. People drive over passes in the Rockies, on their way to somewhere else, that are a good deal higher than the very top of Mount Washington. So how can anybody call that the worst weather in America?

Here is the answer, and it is plain geography. Mount Washington sits at a crossroads. Storms coming across the country from the west, storms coming up the coast from the south, storms coming down out of Canada, a great many of them pass right over or right near the White Mountains, and Mount Washington is the tallest thing standing in their way. The Presidential Range runs roughly north and south, like a wall, and the wind coming at it from the west has to go up and over. When air is squeezed up and over a ridge like that, it speeds up, the way water speeds up when you put your thumb over the end of a garden hose. So the wind on the summit is not the wind in the valley. It can be a nice day down at the bottom, with folks having a picnic by the river, while up on top it is blowing a gale, with the cloud so thick you cannot see the rock in front of your boots.

The numbers on that mountain are hard to believe until you have stood up there. The summit is in fog something like sixty percent of the time. Winds of hurricane force, seventy-five miles an hour or more, blow up there on more than a hundred days in an average year. The temperature can drop below freezing in any month of the year, including July. There have been snowstorms on that summit in the middle of summer. And on the twelfth of April, nineteen thirty-four, the men who ran the weather observatory on top recorded a gust of wind at two hundred and thirty-one miles an hour. For more than sixty years, that was the fastest wind ever measured on the surface of the earth, anywhere. It was finally beaten in nineteen ninety-six, during a cyclone off the coast of Australia. It is still the fastest wind ever recorded by human beings standing on the ground and watching their instruments. The story the observatory people tell is that the men on duty that day knew full well nobody would believe it, and the instrument was tested afterward to make sure it had told the truth. It had.

That is the mountain. Now let me tell you about the first man it killed, as far as the records go.

His name was Frederick Strickland. He was twenty-nine years old, an Englishman from Bridlington, on the Yorkshire coast. He had come over to America and was traveling, and in October of eighteen forty-nine he set out to climb Mount Washington. Back in those days there was no cog railway, no road to the top, no hut, no shelter, and certainly no sign. There were a couple of rough bridle paths that guides used in the summer season to bring visitors up on horseback. October was past the season.

The accounts say he set out with a guide and another man, and when the weather turned bad up high, with snow and ice coming on, the others turned back. Strickland did not. He went on alone. And somewhere up there, he lost the path. Searchers found his body later, down in the Ammonoosuc Ravine, on the western side of the mountain. The accounts say he had fallen and struggled a long way through the snow and the brush and the water before he died. The date usually given is the nineteenth of October, eighteen forty-nine.

He is the first person recorded as dying on Mount Washington. He was not the last. Not by a long way. Since then, the count kept by the people who track these things has gone up past a hundred and sixty. Some fell. Some drowned in streams swollen by storms. Some were caught in avalanches in the ravines in winter. Some had heart attacks. But a great many of them, more than any other single cause, died the way Frederick Strickland died. Of cold and wet and wind. Of exposure. Of what the doctors call hypothermia, which only means the body has lost more heat than it can make, and has begun to shut down.

Hold onto that word, exposure, because it is the word on the sign, and it is the key to the whole thing.

Six years after Strickland, in the fall of eighteen fifty-five, a young woman went up the mountain, and her story is the one that people in New Hampshire still tell. Her name was Lizzie Bourne. Elizabeth Green Bourne. She was twenty-three years old, from Kennebunk, Maine. Her family was well known there. They were staying for a while at the Glen House, a big hotel at the foot of the mountain on the eastern side, the way well-to-do families used to come up into the White Mountains in those days for the air and the views.

On the afternoon of the fourteenth of September, eighteen fifty-five, Lizzie set out to climb Mount Washington with her uncle, George Bourne, and her cousin, Lucy. Now you notice I said the afternoon. That is the first part of this story that the people who tell it always come back to. They started late. They went up a road that was being built at the time toward the summit, and then on up the rocks above it. By the time they got above the trees, it was getting on toward evening. And the weather came in.

The cloud came down. The wind came up. The light went. And they could not see the summit, or the way to it, or the way back down. They were somewhere on the upper slopes, in the open, in the wind and the cold and the dark, with no idea how close they were to anything.

And here is the part of this story that has always stayed with me, and it is plain history, because the family wrote about it afterward. Her uncle, George Bourne, did not sleep that night. He went to work in the dark, in that wind, gathering stones, and piled them up into a rough wall, to try to break the wind for the two young women. He worked at it all night, the accounts say, to try to keep them alive. And sometime in the night, Lizzie Bourne died there behind that wall of stones.

When the morning came and the cloud lifted, they found out where they were. They were only a few hundred feet from the Summit House. There had been a building, with people in it, and fire, and shelter, almost close enough to call out to. They had not been able to see it. Lizzie Bourne died within sight of safety, in the dark, without ever knowing it was there.

Her father, the judge, wrote afterward that he wished no change be made to the wall his brother had thrown up in his great anxiety to protect the girls from the fury of the gale. There is a monument on that spot today. People going up the Cog Railway or the Auto Road pass close to it. It has been there in one form or another for a very long time. If you go and stand there on a clear day, you can see the top of the mountain plain as anything. That is the thing that gets people. How close it is.

And here is something worth thinking about. In the years after Lizzie Bourne died, the mountain got a great deal easier to reach. The carriage road her party had walked up beside was finished not long after, in the early eighteen sixties, and it is still there today as the Mount Washington Auto Road. You can drive your own car up it now, and a lot of folks do, and come home with a bumper sticker that says so. Then at the end of the eighteen sixties came the Cog Railway, a little steam engine that pushes a car straight up the western slope on a toothed track, up grades so steep the passengers lean back in their seats. There were hotels on the summit. There is a big building up there today, with a snack bar and a gift shop and a post office, and on a fine day in summer the top of the mountain is crowded with people who came up by car or by train.

That is a wonderful thing in a lot of ways. It means a grandmother who could never climb a mountain can stand on top of one and look out over four states. But it does something else too, and the rangers and the rescue people up there will tell you about it. It makes the mountain feel tame. You stand in a gift shop on top, with a hot dog in your hand, and it is hard to believe that the slope right outside the window has killed more than a hundred and sixty people. And the folks who hike up from below, they know there is a building at the top. They know there is a road. They figure if things get bad, they will just get to the top and catch a ride down. Sometimes that works. Sometimes the cloud comes in and they cannot find the top, the same way Lizzie Bourne could not.

There are people who live up there too, and have for a long time. The Mount Washington Observatory has kept people on that summit year round since the nineteen thirties, all through the winter, when the road is closed and the railway is shut down and the only way up is on foot or on a snow machine. They take the weather every hour. They put out a forecast for the higher summits that every serious hiker in New England reads before they go. Those observers have a habit, in winter, of going out on the observation deck in hundred-mile-an-hour wind just to see if they can stand up in it. Most of the time they cannot. They lean into it at an angle you would not believe, and then they get knocked flat. They are the people who know the mountain best, and you will notice they treat it with the most respect of anybody.

And in the winter, there is the other danger, the one the sign does not even mention. On the eastern side of the mountain is a great bowl carved out by ancient glaciers, called Tuckerman Ravine. Snow blows off the summit plateau all winter and piles up in that bowl, deep and steep, and in spring people come from all over to climb up and ski down it. And that same snow slides. The ravine has its own avalanche center, with forecasters who go out every day in the season and rate the danger. People have died in avalanches up there, and people have died falling on the ice, and people have died when great blocks of ice broke off the headwall and came down on them. It is one mountain, with a dozen different ways to hurt you, and the weather is the one that ties all of them together.

I will come back to Lizzie. But first I want to take you further back, to before any of these people came, because the mountain had a reputation long before there were hotels at the foot of it.

The Abenaki people lived in the lands around the White Mountains, and the high peaks were not places you went lightly. Folks say the old name for the mountain was Agiocochook, and you will see that name written in a lot of the old books, though historians argue about exactly what it meant and which people used it. The story goes that the summit was the home of the Great Spirit, or of powerful beings, and that the people did not go up to the very top, out of respect, or out of fear, or both. Nobody can prove how old that belief is, or how widely it was held. But it is one of the things the early white settlers wrote down about the mountain, and it says something that the first people to know Mount Washington did not see it as a place to go for a nice afternoon walk.

The first white man on record to climb it was a fellow named Darby Field, in sixteen forty-two. The story goes that he came back down talking about finding gems up there, crystals shining in the rocks, and that he believed he had found diamonds. They were not diamonds. But the idea of treasure on the mountain never quite went away, and it turned into one of the best known legends of the White Mountains. The legend of the Great Carbuncle.

The story goes that somewhere high up on the mountains, hidden among the cliffs, there is a great red jewel, a carbuncle, that glows with its own light in the dark, so that on certain nights you can see it shining from far off, like a coal. Men went up looking for it. Some never came back. Folks say the jewel was guarded by a spirit, or a curse, and that the closer you got to it, the thicker the mists came down, until you could not see your own hand. Nathaniel Hawthorne, the writer, made a story out of it back in the eighteen thirties, about a group of seekers who go up into the mountains to find the jewel, each for his own selfish reasons, and what becomes of them.

Now nobody can prove there was ever a glowing jewel on Mount Washington, and I would not go up there looking. But I have always thought there was something true in that old story, true in the way legends are true. A light shining up on the mountain. People drawn up toward it, past the point where they should have turned around. The mist closing in. That is a pretty good description of how the mountain has been killing people for a hundred and seventy-some years.

So let me tell you about the third one, because she is the one who teaches the lesson most plainly.

Her name was Kate Matrosova. She was thirty-two years old. She lived in New York City and worked in finance, a trader for a big international bank. She was fit, strong, and she had climbing experience. She had been on big mountains before. She came up to the White Mountains in February of two thousand fifteen with a plan, a hard one. She was going to do what is called a traverse of the northern Presidentials. The idea was to start before dawn, go up Mount Madison, then along the high ridge over Mount Adams and Mount Jefferson, and on to Mount Washington, and then down. Four summits, around sixteen miles, almost all of it above treeline, in winter, in one long day. Going fast and light, the way mountaineers do on big peaks around the world.

She started up on the morning of the fifteenth of February, two thousand fifteen. That was Presidents' Day weekend.

The forecast for that day was terrible, and it was known to be terrible. A fierce cold front was coming through. The forecasts were calling for temperatures well below zero up high, and winds building through the day to well over a hundred miles an hour. Some people have said afterward that she believed she could get across the ridge before the worst of it arrived. Accounts differ on what she knew and what she was thinking. Nobody can ever fully know that. What the records show is that she started up Mount Madison in the dark, and that the weather came in faster and harder than even the forecast said.

Sometime that afternoon, up on the ridge between Madison and Adams, she activated her personal locator beacon. That is a little device that sends a signal to a satellite, and on to search and rescue, that says, I am in trouble, here is where I am. The signal came through. People knew she was up there. People knew roughly where. And they could not get to her.

The search and rescue crews who went up that night were turned back by the conditions. The winds up on that ridge were over a hundred miles an hour. The temperature was something like thirty degrees below zero, before you even reckoned the wind into it. At those numbers, the wind chill is off the bottom of any chart you have ever seen. People who are trained, and equipped, and roped together, and who do this for a living, could not stand up and move forward in it. One team later worked its way up into winds that were measured at a hundred and eight miles an hour.

They found her the next day, near Star Lake, in the col between Madison and Adams. She was gone.

People argued a great deal afterward, about her decisions, about the forecast, about her gear, about whether she should have turned back at a certain point, whether she could have dropped down off the ridge into the trees on one side or the other. A lot of very experienced mountain people have written about it, and they do not all agree, and I am not going to stand here and pick it apart. She was by all accounts a brave and capable person. She had climbed mountains much higher than that one. And Mount Washington and its neighbors killed her anyway, on a single winter afternoon, a few miles from a parking lot, with a rescue beacon in her hand.

So now let me keep the promise I made you at the start. How does a mountain this size kill people, even in summer, even people who know what they are doing?

The answer is in that word on the sign. Exposure.

Down in the valley, in summer, you walk out of the car in a T-shirt and shorts. It is warm. The trail starts in the woods, out of the wind, in the shade. You climb for a couple of hours, and you sweat, and your shirt gets wet through. And then you come out above the trees, and everything changes in a few steps. There is nothing between you and the sky. The wind is coming straight at you off the ridge, with nothing to stop it. And if a cloud comes down, and they come down fast up there, now you are wet, in a wind, in fog, at forty degrees, maybe less. And wet clothes in wind pull the heat out of a body faster than almost anything except cold water.

At first you just feel chilled. Then you start to shiver. Then your hands stop working right, your fingers get clumsy, you cannot work a zipper. And here is the cruel part. As the cold goes deeper, it starts to take your judgment. People who are getting hypothermic make bad decisions. They get confused. They get stubborn. They keep going when they ought to turn around, or they sit down to rest when they ought to keep moving. They lose the trail in fog that they could have followed easily an hour earlier. Some of them start to feel warm, near the end, and take off clothes. The very thing you need most, your clear head, is the first thing the cold takes from you.

That is how Lizzie Bourne died within a few hundred feet of a hotel in September. That is how Frederick Strickland went on alone and lost the path in October. And that is why, every year, search and rescue crews in the White Mountains go up after people who started out on a nice summer morning in sneakers with a bottle of water.

So the rule is the one on the sign. And it is not complicated. When you get to treeline, you stop. You look up. You look at the sky, and the cloud, and you feel the wind. And if the weather is bad, or turning bad, you turn back. Not after the next rise. Not after you get a little closer. Right there. The summit will be there next week. It has been there a long time.

And before you ever reach the sign, you carry what you need for the worst weather in America, not the weather in the parking lot. A warm layer. A hat. Gloves. A real rain jacket, and pants that will keep off the wind. Food. Water. A map, and the knowledge to use it in fog. You check the higher summits forecast that the observatory puts out, not the forecast for the town down below. You start early, not in the afternoon. And you let somebody know where you are going and when you will be back.

None of that is exciting. None of it makes for a good story. That is exactly the point. The good stories, on that mountain, are almost all about the people who did not do it.

I think about that wall of stones, sometimes. George Bourne in the dark, in that wind, piling up rocks all night long with his bare hands to keep the girls alive. It is about the most human thing I know of. Doing the only thing you can think of to do, all night, against something far bigger than you. And in the morning, the cloud lifting, and there it is. The Summit House. A few hundred feet away. Close enough to throw a stone.

That is what the sign is trying to tell people. That the mountain does not look dangerous. That the danger is in the weather, not the rock, and the weather can change in the time it takes you to eat a sandwich. That you can be a few hundred feet from safety and never see it. That the old people who would not go to the top might have known something after all.

The sign is still there. People still stop and take a picture of it. Most of them turn around when they should. And every year, a few of them do not.
