He discovered the highest mountain in the eastern United States. Then it killed him. A June evening in the Black Mountains of North Carolina. The fir trees stand so thick that the light goes out of the woods an hour before it leaves the sky. An old man in a long coat is coming down off the ridge alone, fast, with a storm behind him. He knows these mountains better than anybody alive. Below him he hears water, a creek running down through the rhododendron, and he does what the old advice says to do. He follows it. Eleven days later, a bear hunter finds him.

The old man's name was Elisha Mitchell. And the mountain he was coming down that night carries his name today. Mount Mitchell. The highest point in the eastern United States, the tallest mountain east of the Mississippi River. He is buried on top of it. And the story of how he got there, and how he died, is one of the strangest stories these mountains have to tell. It is a story about pride, and an argument between two educated men that got out of hand. But underneath all of that, it is a story about one of the oldest pieces of advice in the woods, and why that advice can kill you.

His pocket watch stopped at eight o'clock. That is almost all we know about the last minutes of his life, and it is enough. By the end of this story, you will understand exactly what happened to him in the dark, and why the piece of advice he followed, the one you have probably heard since you were a child, follow the water downhill and it will lead you out, is the advice that search and rescue teams in these mountains wish nobody had ever written down. And you will know what to do instead, because the man who found his body knew it, and so did every old hunter in those hollows.

Let us start with the man, because he was a remarkable one.

Elisha Mitchell was not a mountain man. He was born in Connecticut, in seventeen ninety-three, and he was educated at Yale. He came south as a young man to teach at the University of North Carolina, in Chapel Hill, and he stayed there for the rest of his life. He taught mathematics, and then chemistry, and geology, and mineralogy, and just about every science the university had a use for. He was also an ordained Presbyterian minister. He was a big, energetic, curious man, the kind of professor who could not sit still in a classroom when there was a whole state out there that nobody had properly measured.

And in those days, North Carolina had not been properly measured. Nobody knew for certain how high the mountains in the western part of the state actually were. Most people back east believed that the highest mountains in the country east of the Rockies were up in New Hampshire, in the White Mountains, with Mount Washington standing over them all. But Mitchell had a notion that the mountains in his own state might be higher. And he set out to prove it.

In eighteen thirty-five, he went up into the Black Mountains, a short, high range northeast of Asheville. They are called the Black Mountains because of the trees. Up high, they are covered with spruce and fir, dark evergreens so dense that from a distance the whole ridge looks black against the sky. Mitchell went up there with barometers, which in those days was the way you measured height. You took a reading of the air pressure at the bottom and a reading at the top, and you worked out the difference with mathematics. And when he did the numbers, he found that the Black Mountains were higher than Mount Washington. Higher than anything in the East.

That part is plain history. He came back over the following years and measured again, and refined his numbers, and his work was published, and people began to accept that the highest peak in the eastern United States was in North Carolina, and that Elisha Mitchell was the man who had found it.

And then, in the eighteen fifties, a former student of his picked a fight.

The man's name was Thomas Clingman. He had studied under Mitchell at Chapel Hill years before, and he had gone on to become a politician, and a congressman, and a man with a very high opinion of himself. Clingman was also interested in the mountains. And in the mid eighteen fifties, he began to claim that Mitchell had gotten it wrong. Not about the height exactly, but about which peak was the highest, and about who had been the first to measure it. The heart of Clingman's argument was that Mitchell, back in eighteen thirty-five, had not actually stood on the highest summit at all. That he had climbed a different peak, a lower one, and measured that, and that Clingman himself was the first to properly measure the true high point.

Historians argue about the details of that quarrel to this day, and I am not going to drag you through every letter they wrote back and forth in the newspapers. Accounts differ on just who said what, and when, and on which peak was which. But the shape of it is plain enough. A young man with political ambitions was publicly telling the world that an old, respected professor did not know which mountain he had climbed. And the old professor was not going to let that stand.

So in the summer of eighteen fifty-seven, Elisha Mitchell went back to the Black Mountains. He was sixty-three years old. He meant to settle it. The accounts say he wanted to retrace his old route, and to find the local men who had guided him up there more than twenty years before, and get them to say on the record where they had taken him. He did not go up alone that summer. There were family and helpers with him, and they set up camp in the mountains.

And on Saturday, the twenty-seventh of June, eighteen fifty-seven, Mitchell set out alone, to go over the top of the range and down the other side, to the settlements along the Caney River, where some of those old guides lived. It was a walk he believed he knew.

He never got there.

Now I want to give you what the records actually show about that night, because it is very little, and the very little is what makes it so haunting.

When he did not come back, at first nobody worried much. He was a strong walker and he knew people on the far side of the mountain, and it was assumed he had stopped over with one of the families down on the Caney River. It was a few days before his son and the others realized he had not arrived anywhere. And then the search began.

It was a big search, by the standards of those mountains. Men came up from Asheville and from the settlements all around. Mountain people who had lived their whole lives in those hollows went up into the fir thickets, and the laurel, and the rock. They searched for days.

And I want you to think for a moment about what a search like that meant in eighteen fifty-seven. There were no radios. There were no helicopters, no dogs trained for the work, no maps of those ridges worth the name. There was no trail to the top of the Black Mountains the way there is now. There were men on foot, in wool clothes, in the rain, pushing through laurel and fir so thick that a searcher could pass within a few yards of a man lying on the ground and never see him. They would spread out and holler. They would fire their rifles and wait to hear if anything answered. At night they would gather at a camp and compare where they had been, and in the morning they would go out again. It was slow, hard, wet, dangerous work, and every man who went up there knew it could just as easily be him that went over a ledge in the fog. And still they went, day after day, for a man most of them knew only as the professor who came up every so often with his instruments.

And it was not until the eighth of July, by most accounts, eleven days after he set out, that they found him.

The man who found him was a hunter named Thomas Wilson. Everybody called him Big Tom.

I will come back to Big Tom in a moment, because he deserves his own telling. But here is what he found. Elisha Mitchell's body was lying in a pool at the bottom of a waterfall, on a fork of the Caney River, high up on the side of the mountain. He had fallen over the falls in the dark, and he had died there in the water.

The records show a few details that tell the rest. A diary was found on him, and the last entry in it was from earlier that same evening. And his pocket watch had stopped at about eight o'clock. Now, in late June in those mountains, eight o'clock is about when the light is going, especially in thick woods, especially under storm clouds. So the people who pieced it together afterward believed that a storm came up as he was crossing high on the mountain, and he hurried to get down, and the dark came on, and somewhere in that dark he came to the top of a waterfall he could not see, and he went over it.

That is the whole of what is known. The rest is reasoning. But the reasoning has held up for well over a hundred and fifty years.

They carried his body out and buried him in Asheville. But a lot of people felt that was not right. The next year, in eighteen fifty-eight, his remains were taken back up and buried on the very top of the highest peak in the Black Mountains, the summit he had spent his life arguing over. And in the eighteen eighties, the United States Geological Survey went over the whole matter, and they sided with Mitchell, and they officially named the mountain for him. Mount Mitchell. Six thousand six hundred and eighty-four feet. The highest point in the East.

Thomas Clingman did not come away empty-handed either. There is a peak in the Great Smoky Mountains named for him. Clingmans Dome. It is the highest point in the Smokies, and the highest in Tennessee, and there is a famous observation tower on top of it. And if you want to know how close that old argument really was, Clingmans Dome is only about forty feet shorter than Mount Mitchell. The two men who could not agree are standing on their two mountains still, looking at each other across the ridges, forty feet apart.

Now let me tell you about Big Tom Wilson, because there is no telling this story without him, and because he is the place where the plain history starts to run into the old mountain way of telling things.

Big Tom Wilson was a mountain man through and through. He lived up along the Caney River, in the shadow of the Black Mountains, and he was known all through that country as the finest hunter and tracker of his day. One account says he killed more than a hundred bears in his lifetime. He guided travelers and scientists up into the high country. He knew those mountains the way most people know the inside of their own house. And when the search for Mitchell had been going on for days, and the searchers from town were getting nowhere, it was Big Tom who found the trail.

Now here is where the stories begin, and I want you to hear them as stories. Folks say Big Tom tracked the professor over ground where nobody else could see a thing. The story goes that he found a broken twig here, a scuffed bit of moss there, a place where a boot heel had slid on a wet root, and that he followed those signs for miles, through fir thickets so dense you had to crawl, until he came out at the top of the falls and looked down, and saw the old man in the water. Some tellers have him saying that the professor had followed the creek, and that any mountain man could have told him not to. Nobody can prove what Big Tom said. But the people up along the Caney River told it that way for generations, and it made Big Tom Wilson famous. There is a mountain named for him too. Big Tom. It stands right there in the Black Mountains, near the man he found.

And the mountain people had other stories about those black ridges. I grew up hearing that the high fir country was a different place from the rest of the mountains, a place that did not want you there. Folks say that up in the dark balsam woods, the fog comes in so fast and so thick that a man can lose his way between one tree and the next. The story goes that the old hunters would not stay up in the balsams after dark if they could help it, and that they marked their way up with blazes on the trees so they could find it back down. Folks say the Black Mountains turn a man around. That you can walk all day in what you are sure is a straight line and come out exactly where you started. Nobody can prove there is anything strange about those mountains. But anybody who has been up in a spruce-fir forest in the fog knows the feeling the old people were talking about. Every tree looks like every other tree. Every direction looks the same. And the only thing that seems to make sense is the sound of water running downhill.

And those black woods have changed since Mitchell's day, in a way that makes the high country look even more like a place out of an old story. The fir that grows up there, the Fraser fir, grows wild in only a handful of places in the world, all of them on the highest peaks of the southern Appalachians. The old mountain people called it the she-balsam. And starting in the middle of the last century, a tiny insect that had come over from Europe, the balsam woolly adelgid, got into those firs and began to kill them. It spread through the high peaks over the following decades, and whole mountainsides of mature fir trees died standing up. Scientists have also argued over how much the acid rain and the air pollution drifting in from the west helped weaken those forests. Historians of the mountains and the people who study the trees still argue about how the blame should be divided. But anybody who went up on Mount Mitchell in the nineteen eighties and nineties remembers what it looked like. Gray, dead trunks standing by the thousand in the fog, like the bones of a forest. Young trees have come back in a good many places since. But there are still spots up there where, on a foggy evening, the high ridge looks very much like the old stories said it did. A place that does not want you.

Which brings me to the advice. And to the skeptic, who I want to let speak first.

The skeptic says, now wait. Following water downhill is good advice. Everybody knows it. It is in survival books. Water runs downhill, and downhill is where the valleys are, and the valleys are where the roads and the towns and the people are. If you are lost in the mountains, find a creek and follow it down, and sooner or later you will walk out. That is common sense.

And I will give the skeptic his due. In some country, that is not bad advice. On gentle land, with broad valleys and slow rivers, a creek may well lead you to a farm or a road.

But the Appalachian Mountains are not gentle land. Not where the creeks start. Up high, the creeks in these mountains do not wander down through meadows. They drop. They come off the high ridges through narrow, steep, rocky ravines, and those ravines are choked with rhododendron and laurel so thick you have to crawl, and they are full of slick moss-covered rock, and every so often the creek goes over a ledge. A waterfall. Sometimes a little one, a few feet high. Sometimes one high enough to kill you. And you do not see them coming, especially in the dark, especially in fog, because you are down in the bottom of the ravine with the water, and the waterfall is just the place where the ground disappears.

The people who do search and rescue in these mountains will tell you that a great many of the lost people they go looking for are found in drainages. Down in the creek bottoms. Because that is where people go when they are lost. They hear the water, and they think of the old advice, and they head down into the ravine.

There is a whole field of study about this now, believe it or not. Searchers have gone back through thousands of old cases, of people who got lost and were found, and they have written down what lost people actually do. A man named Robert Koester put a great deal of that work together in a book called Lost Person Behavior, and search teams all over the country use it to decide where to look first. What that kind of work shows, again and again, is that lost people do not wander at random. They follow things. They follow trails, and roads, and fences, and power lines. And they follow drainages. Downhill is easier on tired legs, and water feels like it must be going somewhere, and so people go down. In flat country, that may bring them out. In steep country, it very often brings them into the worst ground on the mountain. The searchers know that. That is why, when someone goes missing high up in these mountains, the creek bottoms below where they were last seen are some of the very first places the teams go to look. And the ravine gets steeper, and thicker, and wetter, and it gets harder to climb back out, and then they come to a drop they cannot get down and cannot get around. Or they come to a drop in the dark and they never see it at all.

That is what happened to Elisha Mitchell, as near as anybody can tell. A brilliant man, a man who knew those mountains with his instruments better than anybody alive, did the most natural thing in the world when the light was going. He followed the water down. And the water went over a ledge.

So I promised you what to do instead, and here it is. This is the rule, and it is the title of this story. Never follow a creek down a mountain in the dark.

If the dark catches you up high in these mountains, and you are not on a trail you know, the best thing you can do is very often the hardest thing to make yourself do. Stop. Find a sheltered spot, out of the wind, away from the creek and away from any edge. Put on every warm layer you have. Sit on your pack, so the cold ground does not pull the heat out of you. And wait for the light. A night out in the woods is long, and it is cold, and it is miserable, and you will feel foolish. But you will be alive in the morning, and in the morning you will be able to see.

If you are lost in daylight, the rescue people's advice is much the same. Stay put, if you can, especially if somebody knows where you were headed. Make yourself easy to find. If you must move, move up, not down. Ridges are open, and you can see from them, and the trails in these mountains very often run along the ridges. A ridge will not drop you off a waterfall. And if you must follow water, stay up on the side slopes, well above the creek, where you can see what is ahead of you, and never, ever do it in the dark.

And before any of that, the oldest rule of all. Tell somebody where you are going, and by which way, and when you will be back. Elisha Mitchell's people thought he was safe with friends on the other side of the mountain, and they lost days before anybody went looking. Days that, in a different story, might have mattered.

Mount Mitchell became North Carolina's first state park, back in nineteen fifteen. You can drive almost to the top of it now, up a road off the Blue Ridge Parkway, and walk a short path to the summit. There is an observation deck up there, and on a clear day you can see for a very long way, ridge after blue ridge going off toward Tennessee and Virginia. And right there near the top, there is a stone marking the grave of Elisha Mitchell. People walk past it every day in the summer, eating ice cream from the gift shop, taking pictures of the view. Most of them do not stop to read it.

The waterfall is still there too, down on the side of the mountain, in the fir and the rhododendron, a long way from any road. They call it Mitchell Falls. It is not a famous waterfall. It is not on postcards. Most people who visit the mountain never see it.

I think about that old man sometimes, coming down off the ridge in the storm at the end of that June day, sixty-three years old, with his whole life's work and his good name riding on the trip. I think about how sure he must have felt. He had measured these mountains. He had written the book on them. And I think about the sound of that water coming up out of the dark ravine below him, the way it must have sounded like the way home. It is the most natural sound in the world. It is the sound the mountains make when they are going somewhere. And the mountains, as the old hunters knew, do not always go where you are going.

Big Tom Wilson lived to be an old man. He died in nineteen hundred and eight, up on the Caney River, in the shadow of those same black ridges. And his mountain and the professor's mountain still stand side by side, the hunter and the scholar, the man who could read the woods and the man who could measure them. It was the hunter who knew enough not to follow the water. And it was the hunter who came home.
