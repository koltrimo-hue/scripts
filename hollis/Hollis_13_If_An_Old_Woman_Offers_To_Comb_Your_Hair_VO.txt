The Cherokee had a name for that old woman. They called her Utlunta. In English, Spearfinger. And she may be the most frightening creature in all of the old stories of these mountains, older than any ghost story the settlers brought with them, older than the painter and the haints and the boogers. She is a story the Cherokee were telling around their fires in these very mountains long before any European ever saw them. And the reason she is so frightening is not her claws or her teeth. It is that she looks like someone you would trust.

By the end of this story, you will know what Spearfinger really was in the old telling, a creature of stone with a secret hidden in her right hand, and how the people finally found it. You will know who wrote these stories down, and how close they came to being lost forever, which is plain history and a hard piece of it. And you will know why I think this old story, of all the old stories, still has something to say to every parent and grandparent who takes a child into the woods. Some of you are going to hear a lesson your own grandmother tried to teach you, and never quite said out loud.

So let me start with the plain history, the part with dates on it, because without it, we would not have the story at all.

The Cherokee are one of the great peoples of the southern mountains. For a very long time before the Europeans came, their country covered a huge stretch of the southern Appalachians, the mountains of what we now call western North Carolina, east Tennessee, north Georgia, and the upcountry of South Carolina. The Great Smoky Mountains were the heart of it. The towns sat in the river valleys, and the hunting grounds ran up into the high ridges, and every mountain and every river and every deep pool had its name and its story.

Then the settlers came, and the land was taken, piece by piece, treaty by treaty, over the course of a hundred years and more. And in eighteen thirty-eight, the United States government did the thing that the Cherokee have never forgotten and never should. Soldiers rounded up the Cherokee people from their homes, held them in stockades, and marched them west, more than a thousand miles, to Indian Territory in what is now Oklahoma. Thousands of them died along the way, of cold and hunger and sickness. That march is called the Trail of Tears, and it is plain history, and it is one of the most shameful things this country ever did.

But not every Cherokee went west. A number of families hid out in the most remote, rugged corners of these mountains, up in the hollows and the coves where the soldiers could not easily follow. Others held onto land in North Carolina through different arrangements. And from those families came the people who are now the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, who still live on their own land in the mountains of western North Carolina, at the edge of the Great Smoky Mountains, around the town of Cherokee. If you have ever driven into the Smokies from the North Carolina side, you drove right through their homeland.

And it was among those Eastern Cherokee, in the late eighteen hundreds, that a man named James Mooney came to listen.

James Mooney worked for the Smithsonian Institution, for the Bureau of American Ethnology, which was the part of the government that studied the native peoples of this country. He was an Irishman's son from Indiana, and he had a gift for listening. Starting in the late eighteen eighties, he spent a great deal of time with the Eastern Cherokee in the mountains of North Carolina, and he wrote down everything they would tell him. Their history, their medicine, their sacred formulas, and their stories. His most important teacher was an old Cherokee medicine man named Swimmer, who carried a vast knowledge of the old ways in his head, and who shared a great deal of it. In nineteen hundred, Mooney published what he had gathered in a long report called Myths of the Cherokee. More than a hundred and twenty-five years later, it is still the great book of Cherokee stories, and most of what we know about Spearfinger comes from it.

And there is another remarkable piece of history tucked inside that one. Swimmer did not only carry his knowledge in his head. He had written a great deal of it down himself, in a notebook, in the Cherokee language, using the Cherokee writing system. That writing system had been created in the early eighteen hundreds by a Cherokee man named Sequoyah, who could not read or write in English, and who worked out, entirely on his own, a symbol for every syllable of his own language. Within a few years of his finishing it, a great many Cherokee people had learned to read and write in their own tongue. It is one of the most extraordinary achievements in the history of this continent. And so when James Mooney came to Swimmer, Swimmer was able to show him pages of sacred formulas and medicine that he had written in Sequoyah's letters. Mooney bought that notebook, and it went back to Washington with him. So some of what we know of the old Cherokee world was written down by a Cherokee man, in a Cherokee alphabet, before any outsider ever saw it.

Think about how close that came to being lost. The people who carried those stories had been driven from most of their country. Their children were being sent to boarding schools where they were punished for speaking their own language. The old men and women who knew the stories by heart were dying. If James Mooney had not come up into those mountains when he did, and sat with Swimmer and the others, and written it all down, a great deal of it would be gone. A great many Cherokee people have kept those stories alive in their own families and their own tellings ever since, and the way they tell them is theirs. But Mooney's book is the record that survived on paper.

So now, the story. And I want you to hear it in the right voice. This is a story. It is an old and sacred story to the people who first told it, and I am telling it as it was written down, as best I can, with respect.

The story goes that long ago, in the high mountains, there lived a creature that looked like an old woman. But she was not a woman at all. Her whole body was covered with a skin as hard as stone. No arrow could pierce it, no spear, no blade. And on her right hand, her forefinger was long and sharp, like an awl or a spear point. That is where her name came from. Spearfinger. With that finger she stabbed her victims, and what she wanted from them was their liver. That was her food.

She could take any shape she liked, the story goes, but most often she took the shape of an old woman. A harmless old grandmother, walking along the trail, or sitting by the edge of the village. And she went about the mountains looking for children, and for anyone who had wandered away by themselves.

The old people said she had a favorite time of year. The autumn. In the fall, the Cherokee would burn the dead leaves off the ground under the chestnut trees, to make it easy to gather the nuts. The smoke would drift up through the woods, and the story goes that Spearfinger would see it, and know that there were people there, and children among them. And she would come.

She would walk up to a child who had wandered off a little way, and she would be kind. She would smile and talk sweetly. She would say, come here, my grandchild, let me comb your hair. Let me look for lice in your head, the way an old grandmother would do. And she would take the child's head in her lap, and stroke the child's hair, and sing softly, until the child fell asleep. And then, the story goes, she would slip her long stone finger in and take the child's liver. And here is the most terrible part. She did it so cleverly that there was no wound and no pain. The child would wake up and go home, and seem fine. But in a little while, the child would begin to waste away, and sicken, and die. Nobody would know why. Nobody would even know it had happened.

And I want you to notice the season in that story, because it is a real one. The chestnut trees the old people burned the leaves under were American chestnuts, and in those days they were everywhere in these mountains. Some folks say one tree out of every four in the southern Appalachian forest was a chestnut. Every fall, they dropped a great harvest of nuts that fed people, and hogs, and deer, and bears, and turkeys. Gathering chestnuts in the autumn woods was something whole families did together, Cherokee and settler alike, children and all. Then, in the first half of the last century, a blight came over from Asia and killed nearly every one of those great trees, all up and down the mountains, and the chestnut woods were gone. But the story of Spearfinger is set in that old forest, in the chestnut season, when the woods were full of smoke and full of families and full of children wandering a little way off to fill their baskets. It was the time of year when a child was most likely to be out of sight.

The story goes that Spearfinger was very strong, and could lift and split great rocks. She wanted to build a bridge of stone across the sky from one mountain to another, and she began it, up on the high ridges. Mooney wrote that her home was in the high rocky country, and the tellers connect her with the great cliffs of Whiteside Mountain, in the mountains south of the Smokies near where the towns of Highlands and Cashiers are today. Whiteside Mountain is a real place, and if you have ever seen it, you understand why the old people put a monster there. It has a great bare face of pale stone, hundreds of feet high, sheer as a wall. The story goes that her bridge was struck by lightning and the stones were scattered all down the mountainside, and that you can still see them there.

Whiteside Mountain is a wild place even today. The trail around the top runs right along the edge of those great cliffs, and in some years parts of it are closed in the spring, because peregrine falcons nest on the ledges of that stone face, and the people who look after the mountain want to give them their peace. Standing up there, with the cliff dropping away beside you and the wind coming up the face, and the falcons crying somewhere below, you can understand why the people who lived in those valleys for thousands of years thought something old and dangerous and made of stone might live up there.

There was also a creature in the old stories who was something like her mate, or her kin, called Stone Man, another monster with a skin of rock, who walked the mountains carrying a cane. But Stone Man is a story for another night.

And the Cherokee woods were full of other beings too, and not all of them were dangerous. The ones the old people talked about most were the Little People. The story goes that they lived in the rocky places and the laurel thickets of the high mountains, a people about knee high, with long hair, who loved music and dancing and drumming. Folks say you could sometimes hear their drums in the mountains at night, from a ridge where nobody lived. Mostly they were kind, and they would help a lost child find the way home, and take care of them until they could be returned. But the story goes that you were never to follow them, or go looking for them, because a person who went to live with the Little People for long could never quite come back to the world of ordinary people again. And there was an old rule that if you found something lying in the woods, a knife, a tool, a trinket, you were to say out loud, Little People, I would like to take this. Because if you did not, the story goes, it might belong to them, and they would not take kindly to its being stolen. Nobody can prove there were ever Little People in the Smokies. But I have known folks, not Cherokee folks either, who said it out loud before they picked something up off the forest floor, just in case. And I have never once laughed at them.

Now, how did the people ever stop a monster that no weapon could pierce? This is the part of the story I love best.

The story goes that the people held a great council to decide what to do about Spearfinger. And they made a plan. They dug a deep pit across the trail, and covered it over with brush and earth so it could not be seen. Then they built a fire nearby, so the smoke would rise, because they knew she would come to the smoke. And the hunters hid in the woods with their bows, and waited.

And sure enough, here she came. An old woman walking down the trail, gray and bent. But the hunters knew her. She walked onto the covered pit, and fell through into it, and was trapped. And then the hunters came out and shot at her with everything they had. And every arrow struck her stone skin and broke into pieces, and fell to the ground. Nothing could hurt her. She raged in the pit and tried to climb out.

Then a little bird came. In the old telling, it was a titmouse, the small gray bird with the crest on its head. And the titmouse sang a little song that sounded, in the Cherokee language, like the word for heart. Heart, heart, heart. And the hunters thought the bird was telling them where to aim, at her heart. So they aimed at her chest, where any creature's heart would be. And the arrows broke against her chest the same as before. The story goes that the people were angry with the titmouse for misleading them, and cut out its tongue, and that is why, to this day, the titmouse has a short tongue, and why the Cherokee said it was a liar.

But then another little bird came down. The chickadee. The tiny bird with the black cap that every one of you has seen at your feeder in the wintertime. And the chickadee flew right down and landed on Spearfinger's right hand. On the hand with the long stone finger. And the hunters understood. That was where she kept her heart. Not in her chest, like any honest creature, but hidden in her right hand, the hand with the spear finger, the one she used to kill. She kept her right hand closed tight, always, to protect it. And when the chickadee landed there, a hunter let fly with an arrow and struck her in the hand, right where the finger joined it, and found her heart. And Spearfinger fell dead in the pit.

And the story goes that ever since, the Cherokee have honored the chickadee as the bird that tells the truth. Some tellers say that when a chickadee comes near the house and sings, it is bringing news, of a friend coming, or of something that needs to be known. Nobody can prove it. But the next time a chickadee comes to your feeder, you might think of it.

Now, I promised I would raise the skeptic's objection, and here it is.

The skeptic says, that is a fine old tale, Hollis, but it is a children's story. A monster with a stone finger and a heart in her hand. What does a story like that have to do with real danger in the real woods? Nobody believes in Spearfinger.

And of course the skeptic is right that there is no stone woman in the Smoky Mountains. But I think he is missing what that story is for. Because if you take the monster out of it and look at what is left, the story is very plain about what it is warning against. A child who has wandered away from the others, alone, in the woods. A stranger who seems kind and harmless. Who wants to touch the child, and hold the child, and lull the child to sleep. Who does her harm so quietly that nobody even knows it has been done until long afterward. And who keeps her real nature hidden, closed up tight in her hand, where nobody thinks to look.

Every old culture on earth has a story like that one. Little Red Riding Hood and the wolf dressed as the grandmother. The witch in the gingerbread house in the woods. They are stories built by people who loved their children, to teach the children something they could not quite bear to say plainly. Not every kind face is a kind person. Stay with the others. Do not go off alone. Do not go with a stranger, however sweet they seem.

And the story has a second lesson in it, one that I think is just as wise. Notice who saves the people in the end. Not the strongest hunter. Not the biggest weapon. Every arrow broke. They were saved by paying attention to the smallest things in the woods. A little bird that knew where the danger really was. The people who survived were the ones who listened to the forest. And there is a third lesson folded inside that one, which I have always thought was the cleverest part of the whole story. The hunters first aimed where any sensible person would aim. At the chest. Where a heart is supposed to be. And they were wrong, because the danger was not where they expected it to be. It was hidden in plain sight, in the hand she kept closed, the very hand she used to do her harm. A great many real dangers in the woods are like that. They are not where you are looking. They are in the calm water at the top of the falls, or the friendly face at the campfire, or the shortcut down the creek in the dark. The old stories keep telling us the same thing in different clothes. Look where you do not expect to look.

The old Appalachian settlers who came later had their own versions of that warning. I grew up hearing that you do not let a child out of your sight in the woods, not for a minute. Every family up here had a story of a child that wandered off from a berry patch or a picnic and was not found for a day, or was never found at all. And there are real cases in the records of these mountains, children who walked away from their families in the Smokies and were never seen again, and I have told some of those stories on this channel. The folks in the hollows did not need a stone woman to know that a child alone in the woods is in danger.

So let me give you the rule. It is in the title of this story, and it is an old woman's rule, older than any of us. If an old woman offers to comb your hair in the woods, run. And what it means, for us, today, is this.

When you take children into the woods, you keep them close. You keep them where you can see them, every minute. Not twenty yards ahead on the trail where they are around a bend. Not back at the car while you go look at the view. The people who search for lost children in these mountains will tell you that a great many of them simply walked a little way ahead, or a little way behind, or off the trail after something that caught their eye, and in thick woods, a few dozen yards is all it takes to be lost.

You teach your children what to do if they do get lost. Stay in one place. Hug a tree, the searchers teach them, and stay with it, and wait. Answer when people call your name, even if you are afraid you are in trouble. Give them a whistle to carry, and teach them to blow it, three times. A child who stays put is found. A child who wanders is much harder to find.

And you teach them, gently, the oldest lesson of Spearfinger. That a stranger who wants them to come away from their family, however friendly, however much they seem like a grandmother or a nice man, is someone to walk away from, right back to the people they came with. That they can always say no to a grown-up they do not know. And that they will never, ever be in trouble for coming back to you.

I think about that old woman in the smoke, sometimes, at the beginning of this story, sitting on a log and patting her lap. Come here, child. Let me comb your hair. It is the gentlest picture in the world. That is what makes it so terrible. And I think about the Cherokee grandmothers who told that story to their grandchildren on autumn nights, with the smoke of the leaf fires drifting through the woods, for longer than anybody can count. They were not trying to frighten the children for the fun of it. They were trying to keep them alive.

The chickadees are still in these mountains. You can hear them in the winter woods, calling their own name, chick-a-dee-dee-dee. The Cherokee are still here too, on their own land at the edge of the Smokies, still telling their stories in their own way. And Whiteside Mountain still stands there with its great stone face, and if you go and look up at it on a gray autumn day, with smoke from somebody's chimney drifting through the valley, it is not hard at all to imagine an old woman coming down the trail.
