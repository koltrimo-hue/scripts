More than a hundred people died in these mountains, three hundred miles from the ocean, because of a hurricane. A small house sits beside a creek in western North Carolina, the kind of creek you could step across in August. It is dark. It has been raining for three days. The power is out. A family sits in the kitchen with a flashlight, listening to the rain on the tin roof, and somebody says they will see how it looks in the morning. Outside, the sound of the creek changes. It is not rushing anymore. It is grinding. Rocks the size of cars are rolling along the bottom. And the water is already at the porch steps.

That was the night of the twenty-sixth going into the twenty-seventh of September, two thousand twenty-four. The storm was called Helene. And most of you watching this remember the pictures. Towns in the mountains of North Carolina and east Tennessee that looked like they had been bombed. Roads that were simply gone, with the river running where the asphalt used to be. Houses sitting in the middle of creeks. Whole communities cut off for days and weeks, with no power, no water, no phone, no way in or out.

What a lot of people do not know is how most of the people who died that night actually died, and how many of them were waiting for morning. Here is the hardest fact in this whole story. The records show that at least thirty-eight people in North Carolina alone drowned as creeks and rivers rose to the highest levels anybody had ever recorded, and at least twenty-three more were killed when whole mountainsides let go and came down as landslides. A great many of them were at home. By the end of this story, you will know the sound that the old mountain people listened for, the one they had a name for, and you will know the one thing to do the moment you hear it. And you will hear about a group of factory workers in Tennessee who were told to go home, and the road they took.

So let us start with the rain, because the rain started before the storm.

Hurricane Helene came ashore on the Gulf Coast of Florida, in the Big Bend, late on the night of the twenty-sixth of September, as a powerful hurricane. Most people, when they hear the word hurricane, think of the coast. Wind tearing off roofs, the ocean coming up over the beaches. And Helene did all of that in Florida. But then it did something that hurricanes do more often than people realize. It kept going. It raced north, inland, across Georgia, still a strong storm, and it carried an enormous load of tropical moisture with it, straight toward the southern Appalachian Mountains.

And the mountains were already soaked. In the days before Helene ever arrived, a separate band of heavy rain had settled over western North Carolina and the surrounding country and poured down on it. The ground was full. The creeks were already up. Every hillside in those mountains was holding all the water it could hold. So when Helene's rain arrived on top of that, there was nowhere for the water to go but straight down the slopes and into the creeks.

Then the mountains did the rest. When wet air from the south hits a wall of mountains, it is forced upward, and as it rises it cools, and as it cools it wrings itself out like a rag. The Blue Ridge, the Black Mountains, the high ridges all through that country, squeezed the rain out of Helene in amounts that are hard to take in. Some places in the mountains of western North Carolina recorded more than two feet of rain over those few days, and some gauges recorded a good deal more than that. Two feet of rain is what some places get in half a year. It came down in about three days.

Now I want you to picture what that means in mountain country. Flat country floods slowly. The water spreads out across the fields, and it rises an inch at a time, and people have hours, sometimes days, to get out of the way. Mountain country does not work like that. In the mountains, every drop of rain that falls on a slope runs downhill into a hollow, and every hollow runs into a creek, and every creek runs into a bigger creek, and they all come together in the narrow valley bottoms. And the narrow valley bottoms are exactly where the people live. Where the roads run. Where the towns were built, because that was the only flat land there was.

So when all of that water came off all of those mountains at once, it did not rise. It came down the valleys like a wall.

And here is a part of this story that does not get told often enough. People were warned. In the days before Helene arrived, the forecasters were saying, as plainly as forecasters ever say anything, that this could be one of the most serious weather events in the history of the southern mountains. They were talking about catastrophic flooding, and landslides, days ahead. As the rain came down that night, the weather service sent out its most serious kind of flood warning, the kind they call an emergency, for town after town. A great many people heard those warnings. And a great many of them did what people almost always do with a warning about something they have never seen. They figured it would be bad, but not that bad. Not here. Not on our creek. That is not foolishness. It is how human beings are made. We measure what is coming against what we have already lived through. And nobody living in those mountains had ever lived through anything like what was coming down the valleys that night.

The records show that rivers and creeks all over western North Carolina reached heights that had never been recorded before, in a great many places higher even than the great flood of nineteen sixteen.

And I want to stop on that year for a moment, because nineteen sixteen is the flood the old people in those mountains measured every other flood against, for more than a hundred years. In July of nineteen sixteen, two tropical storms came up out of the south, one right behind the other, and the second one stalled over the Blue Ridge. The records show that a weather station at a place called Altapass, up near the crest, measured more than twenty inches of rain in a single day, which stood for a long time as one of the heaviest single days of rain ever recorded in this country. The French Broad came up through Asheville and took out the bridges and the railroad. The Catawba came down out of the mountains and drowned the valleys below. Dozens of people died across the region, and there are still stories told in families about it. That was the flood that was supposed to be the worst that could ever happen. That was the mark. Helene went past it. The French Broad River came up through Asheville and put the old River Arts District deep under water. The Swannanoa River, east of Asheville, came down through the town of Swannanoa so high that local law enforcement said the water was at least ten feet deep in some places. In Buncombe County, which is the county around Asheville, the flash flooding and the river flooding and the landslides were directly responsible for at least thirty-seven deaths. More than in any other county.

And then there were the landslides. When a mountain slope gets that wet, the soil on it turns to something like wet concrete, and it lets go. It comes down the hillside all at once, mud and rock and whole trees, moving faster than a person can run, and it does not stop for a house. The geologists call it a debris flow. People in those mountains heard them coming that night, a roar that some said sounded like a freight train, and then the slope was on top of them. The records show that at least twenty-three people in North Carolina died in landslides.

And there was Chimney Rock. Some of you have been there. It is a little tourist village down in Hickory Nut Gorge, on the Rocky Broad River, below a famous rock tower, and it runs right along the river, shops and restaurants and little inns on the bank, with the water running by underneath the back porches. Downstream is Lake Lure, a pretty lake town where they filmed part of a movie a lot of you will remember. That night, the Rocky Broad came down Hickory Nut Gorge with the landslides from the slopes above, and the reports afterward said that the majority of the buildings in Chimney Rock were destroyed. Main Street was largely just gone.

Now I promised you a story from Tennessee, and here it is, and I want to tell it to you carefully, because people are still arguing about it.

Over the state line, in Unicoi County, Tennessee, there is a town called Erwin, on the Nolichucky River. On the morning of Friday, the twenty-seventh of September, the Nolichucky was rising fast, fed by all that rain coming down off the mountains of North Carolina. In an industrial park near the river, there was a plastics factory called Impact Plastics. And that morning, the workers there were sent home.

What happened next is where the accounts divide. The company has said that the employees were let go at least forty-five minutes before the worst of the flood hit the industrial park. Some of the surviving workers and the families of the people who died have said that the warning came too late. What is not in dispute is that the river came up over the road out of that industrial park. The only road out. Workers were caught in their cars and in the floodwater. Some of them climbed up onto a flatbed truck and held onto pipes on the back of it as the water rose around them, and the accounts say some of them held on for as long as six hours. Some of them were swept away.

The news reports at the time said that eleven people were swept away, and that only five were rescued. The state's workplace safety agency, in its later report, said that six people died, five employees and one contractor, and that six were rescued after the flood carried them something like half a mile downstream. Accounts differ on the exact numbers. In the spring of two thousand twenty-five, that state agency found that the company's managers had used reasonable diligence in sending the employees home and giving them time to leave. The families' lawyers said they strongly disagreed. I am not going to tell you who was right. I will tell you that a road that was dry when you started down it can be underwater before you reach the end of it, and that in a mountain flood, the difference between leaving early and leaving a little later can be everything.

And on that same morning, not far away, the river came up around Unicoi County Hospital. Patients and staff ended up on the roof of the hospital, waiting for rescue, with the brown water running past below. They were taken off by helicopter. Dozens of them.

In the days after, the mountains did what the mountains have always done in hard times. With the roads gone, people came in any way they could. Neighbors cut their way through fallen trees with chainsaws to reach the folks at the heads of the hollows. Volunteers brought in supplies on ATVs and on foot, and the accounts say some of them packed food and water up into the cut-off communities on the backs of mules, the way supplies came into those hollows a hundred years ago. Private pilots flew in small planes and helicopters full of water and diapers and medicine. Churches turned into shelters and kitchens. People who had lost their own houses were out the next morning helping dig out somebody else's. A great deal of that never made the national news. But the people who lived through it will tell you it was the thing that got them through.

That is the plain history, and there is a great deal more of it than I can tell you in one sitting. Hundreds of people killed across the whole Southeast, by most counts, and more than a hundred of them in North Carolina alone, though the counts kept changing for months as the searchers worked and the officials sorted out what to count. Whole communities in the hollows that nobody could reach for a week or more. People walking out of the mountains on foot, carrying their children, because there was no road left to drive.

Now let me give you the skeptic's turn, because he has a point to make.

The skeptic says, this was a once-in-a-thousand-year storm. Nothing like it had ever been recorded in those mountains. You cannot blame people for not seeing it coming. And you cannot teach a lesson about a thing that will not happen again in our lifetimes.

And the skeptic is right that Helene was something extraordinary. But here is the thing he misses. The mountains flood all the time. Not like Helene. But a summer thunderstorm that stalls over one hollow for an hour can send a wall of water down a creek in that hollow, while the sun is shining a mile away. It happens every year somewhere in these mountains. People drown in their cars every year at low-water crossings that they have driven through a hundred times. Campers get caught every year on creek banks below storms they never saw. The lesson of Helene is not about Helene. It is about water in mountain country, which always moves faster than you think it will.

And here is something else the skeptic should hear. A flash flood is not just a lot of water. It is a lot of water carrying everything in its path. Logs, whole trees, boulders, cars, propane tanks, pieces of houses. The water that came down those valleys during Helene was so full of debris that it acted more like a moving wall of rubble than a river. It took out bridges by piling trees up against them until they broke. It knocked houses off their foundations. A person caught in water like that is not swimming. They are being hit by everything the water is carrying. That is why the people who study floods say the only safe distance from a mountain flood is up and out of its way, and why the time to get up and out of its way is before it gets to you.

And the old mountain people knew it. Which brings me to the folklore.

I grew up hearing the old people talk about tides. Not the ocean kind. In a good many parts of these mountains, the old word for a flood was a tide. The big tide. The tide of nineteen sixteen. The tide of nineteen forty. Folks would say, that was the year the tide took the mill, or that was the tide that washed out the road. And there were sayings that went along with it, passed down through families, and I want you to hear them for what they are.

Folks say you never build in the bottom. I heard that one all my life. The story goes that the old settlers built their cabins up on the bench of the hillside, a little way above the creek, and put their corn in the bottomland, because the corn could be replanted and a family could not. Folks say the creek remembers how high it has been, and that it will come back to that mark some day, and that if you want to know where not to build, you look for the line of old drift and dead leaves hung up in the tree branches along the creek, and you build above it. There is something to that one, and nobody needs to prove it.

Folks say that you can hear a tide coming before you can see it. The story goes that the old people would wake in the night when the sound of the creek changed. A creek in normal flood rushes and roars. But a creek in a real tide, they said, begins to knock and grind, because it is rolling boulders along its bottom, and you can hear them, and you can feel them through the floor of the house. When you hear the rocks, the old people said, you do not wait. You get the children up and you go up the hill. Nobody can prove that every flood gives that warning. But I have talked with people who lived through mountain floods, and some of them said it was the rocks they remembered, that deep knocking sound coming up out of the dark.

Folks say a flood will come down a hollow with no rain falling where you stand. The story goes that a man could be sitting on his porch in the sunshine and hear a roar up the hollow, because a cloudburst had broken over the head of the hollow, miles up, and all that water was on its way down. That one is not folklore at all. That is exactly how a flash flood works in mountain country.

And folks say a lot of things about the signs before the rain. That when the leaves on the trees turn over and show their pale undersides, a storm is coming. That when the cows lie down in the pasture, it will rain. Some of those have something to them, and some of them do not, and after all these years nobody can sort them out. But the ones about the water, the ones about where to build and what to listen for and when to go, those were not superstitions. Those were lessons, paid for in lives, and passed down so nobody would have to pay for them again.

The trouble is that a great many of the people living in the valley bottoms of those mountains on the night Helene came were not raised on those lessons. Some of them were. Some of them were from families that had been in those hollows for two hundred years. But a great many were newer to the mountains, retirees and young families and people who had moved to Asheville and the towns around it because it is a beautiful place to live. They had houses on the creek because a house on the creek is the prettiest house there is. And nobody had ever told them about the tide.

So let me keep the promise I made you at the start. Here is the rule. If the creek is rising at night in the mountains, do not wait for morning.

What that means, in plain terms, is this. If you live in a valley bottom, or near a creek, or below a steep slope, and the rain has been heavy for days, and the creek is coming up, you do not go to bed and decide to see how it looks in the morning. You leave. You go to higher ground while the road is still open and while you can still see where you are going. If the warning comes, you take it, and you do not wait for a second warning to make sure the first one was serious.

You never drive into water over a road. Not even if it looks shallow. Not even if you have driven through that spot a hundred times. The weather service has been saying it for years, and they are right. Turn around, do not drown. It takes only about six inches of moving water to knock a grown person off their feet, and only a foot or two to float a car and carry it away. A great many of the people who die in floods in this country die in their vehicles. And at night, you cannot see that the road under the water is not even there anymore. In Helene, a great many roads simply were not there anymore.

If you live below a steep slope, you watch it. Cracks opening in the ground, trees tilting, mud and water coming out of the hillside where they never did before, a sudden roar above you, those are the signs of a slide. You do not wait to see what happens next.

Keep a weather radio in the house, the battery kind, and keep it on when the big rain is coming. When the power goes out and the cell towers go down, which they did all through those mountains during Helene, that little radio may be the only voice you hear from the outside world all night. You keep your shoes and your keys and a light by the bed when the rain is heavy, the same as you would for a fire. You know your way up the hill, the way you would get out on foot if the road went. And if you ever find yourself trapped by rising water, you go up. Up the hill, up into the attic with something to break through the roof, up onto the roof. The patients and staff at the hospital in Erwin went up onto the roof, and the helicopters came for them.

And if you are visiting the mountains, camping beside a creek in the summer, you remember that the storm that floods your campsite may be raining on a ridge you cannot even see. If the water starts rising, or turning muddy, or carrying sticks and leaves, you move your camp up the bank and away, right then.

I think about that family in the kitchen at the beginning of this story, sometimes. The flashlight. The rain on the tin roof. Somebody saying they would see how it looked in the morning. That is the most natural thing in the world to say. Nobody wants to go out in a storm at night. The house feels safe. The creek has been there your whole life and never come up past the garden. And I think about the old people who built their cabins up on the bench of the hill, and put the corn in the bottoms, and woke up in the night when they heard the rocks. They were not smarter than we are. They had just seen the tide.

The mountains are rebuilding. Chimney Rock is coming back. The roads are being cut again into the sides of the gorges. People who lost everything are putting their lives back together, and the folks in those hollows helped each other through it in ways that never made the national news. That is the mountain way too, as old as the other lessons.

But the creeks will come up again. Maybe not like Helene. But they will. The water has always remembered how high it once was. All we have to do is remember with it.
