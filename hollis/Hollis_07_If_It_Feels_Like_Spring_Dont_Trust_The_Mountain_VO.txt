That was the middle of March, nineteen ninety-three. And a lot of you watching this will remember it, because it was not just the Smokies. It was the storm they called the Storm of the Century. It came up out of the Gulf of Mexico and ran up the whole eastern side of the country, from Florida to Canada, and it buried the South in a way the South had not been buried in living memory. Places that see a dusting of snow once every few years got a foot or more. Birmingham, Alabama, was shut down. Interstates were closed for days. Roofs fell in. And up in the high country of the Great Smoky Mountains, at the very moment it hit, there were hundreds of people out in the backcountry. Spring break hikers. School groups. People who had looked at the calendar and seen March, and packed for spring.

More than two hundred of them came down off that mountain by helicopter. One group was saved by a piece of paper a pilot dropped out of the sky. And here is the part that I still find hard to believe. With all those people stranded in one of the worst storms in American history, not one hiker in the park died. By the end of this, you will know what the ones who made it did differently, which is not what most people would guess, and which goes against every instinct you have, and you will know the old mountain saying about March that every one of those hikers should have heard before they ever left home.

So let us begin with the week before, because that is where this story really starts.

The early part of March, nineteen ninety-three, was mild across a lot of the South. Not unusually so, but pleasant. The kind of weather that makes people think winter is about done. The redbuds were getting ready to bloom down in the valleys. It was spring break season, when colleges and high schools let out for a week, and the Great Smoky Mountains National Park was filling up the way it always does that time of year. People came to hike the Appalachian Trail along the high crest of the Smokies. People came to camp at the backcountry sites down in the hollows. School groups came on trips that had been planned for months.

The Appalachian Trail through the Smokies is a special stretch of that long path. For about seventy miles it runs along the very crest of the range, right along the state line between Tennessee and North Carolina, and it climbs higher there than anywhere else on the whole trail from Georgia to Maine. It goes right over Clingmans Dome, the highest point in the park. Along that crest, the trail is spaced out with shelters, a few miles apart, and in those years hikers were expected to stay in them. Thru-hikers heading north to Maine time their starts so that they reach the Smokies in March and April, which means that every spring the high crest fills up with people who have been walking for a couple of weeks already, who are strong and tired and lean, and who are carrying as little weight as they can stand. A lot of them had sent their heavy winter gear home by then, or never brought it. It was spring, after all.

And the forecasters saw the storm coming. That is worth saying plainly, because it is part of what made this storm different from the ones before it. In the days leading up to it, the weather people were warning that something very big was taking shape in the Gulf, and that it was going to come up the East Coast with snow, and wind, and cold. It was one of the first times a storm that size had been forecast that far ahead, and people in the cities paid attention. Grocery stores were cleaned out. But a forecast does not do you much good if you are already three days' walk into the backcountry, or if you heard it and decided that snow in the South in the middle of March could not possibly amount to much.

It began on Friday, the twelfth of March, and through the night and into Saturday the thirteenth, it came down on the mountains with everything it had.

Up on the highest peaks of the Smokies, the snow did not come by the inch. It came by the foot. On Mount Le Conte, one of the tallest mountains in the park, where there is a lodge that stays open much of the year, the reports put the snowfall at something like four and a half to five feet. Accounts differ on the exact number, depending on where and when it was measured. And it was not light, dry snow. It was heavy and wet in places, and it came with wind that piled it into drifts much deeper than that. The temperatures fell hard behind it. Trees all over the high country came down under the weight of it, across the trails, across the roads. The main road over the mountains, the one that crosses the crest at Newfound Gap, was buried and closed.

And all through that country, up on the ridges and down in the hollows, there were people who were suddenly not going anywhere.

The newspapers of the day reported several groups of hikers stranded in the park, buried under drifts of four feet and more. Among them was a large group of high school students from Michigan, more than a hundred of them, who had come down to the Smokies on a school trip. There was a group of college students from Tennessee, nine of them, out on a spring break hike. There were couples and small parties and people hiking alone, scattered up and down the Appalachian Trail and the side trails, in shelters and in tents and in some cases in nothing much at all.

Think about what that meant for them. A trail you walked in on the day before is gone. Not hard to follow. Gone. Buried under snow up to your waist, in a forest where every tree looks like every other tree. The trail signs are covered. The blazes on the trees are plastered over with snow. The shelters are filling up with drifts through the open front. Your tent is collapsing under the weight. Your sleeping bag, which was fine for a spring night, is not fine for a night well below freezing, with wind. Your boots, which were fine for a muddy March trail, are soaked through. You can hear trees coming down in the dark. And you have food for the number of days you planned for, and not one day more.

Some of the people up there tried to walk out. That is the most natural thing in the world to want to do. But walking in snow that deep is not walking. It is wallowing. You sink to your thighs, to your waist, with every step, and you have to pull your leg up out of the hole and push it forward and sink again. A strong person can wear themselves out in a quarter of a mile. And when you wear yourself out like that, you sweat, and your clothes get wet from the inside, and then you stop, and the cold comes in.

I want to say a word about what the cold actually does, because most of us have never been cold in the way those hikers were cold, and it helps to understand it.

The body makes heat by burning food, and it holds onto that heat with whatever is wrapped around it. When you are wet, the water pulls heat away from your skin many times faster than dry air would. When the wind is blowing, it strips away the thin layer of warm air your clothes are trying to hold next to you. Wet and wind together, at temperatures just above or below freezing, will chill a person faster than a still, dry, bitter cold day. That is why the old outdoorsmen have a saying, cotton kills. A cotton shirt or cotton blue jeans soak up water and hold it against you and never dry out. Wool, and the modern synthetic fabrics, stay warm even when they are damp. A lot of the spring hikers of nineteen ninety-three were dressed in cotton.

When the body starts to lose that fight, it does something clever and cruel. It pulls the blood in from the hands and feet and the skin, and keeps it in the middle, to protect the heart and the brain. That is why your fingers go numb first. And if the hands and feet stay cold long enough, below freezing, the tissue in them actually begins to freeze. That is frostbite. The toes go white, and then hard, and then, later, black. Some of it can be saved. Some of it cannot. The people who lost toes in the Smokies that March lost them to that slow, quiet process, over a night or two of wet boots in deep snow.

The ones who did best, by and large, were the ones who stayed where they were. Who got into a shelter, or got their tent dug in and propped up, and pooled their food and their sleeping bags and their body heat, and waited. Waiting is hard. It goes against everything in a person. But in that storm, waiting was what kept people alive until help could reach them.

And help did come. That is the part of this story that I like best to tell.

As soon as the weather broke enough to fly, the helicopters came. The National Guard, the park service, and others. Over the next days, crews lifted people off the mountain, one group after another, setting down wherever they could find a clearing or lowering a line where they could not. By the end of it, the reports say more than two hundred people had been airlifted out of the high country of the Smokies. It was one of the largest rescue operations the park had ever seen.

It was not tidy work. The helicopters could only fly when the clouds lifted and the wind settled, and in the mountains in a storm like that, those windows open and close fast. Pilots were setting down on ridges and in clearings they had never landed in before, with snow blowing up around the rotors so thick they could hardly see the ground, and trees all around them waiting to catch a blade. Some of the people they lifted out were taken to the Air National Guard base. Others, the ones in worse shape, were flown straight to the hospital at the University of Tennessee Medical Center in Knoxville. In at least one group, the accounts say, a couple of the hikers were flown to one place and the rest of their friends to another, and they had to find each other again once it was over. I would imagine that for the crews who flew those missions, it was some of the hardest flying they ever did, and some of the most worthwhile.

The Michigan students were among them. They had been through a very hard time. Some of them were badly frostbitten, and some of them, the accounts say, lost toes and parts of their feet to the cold. But they came off that mountain alive.

There is one story from those days that people who were there still tell. One of the pilots flew over a group of stranded hikers in a helicopter that did not have a hoist. He could see them down there in the snow, but he had no way to bring them up. So he wrote a note by hand, and he dropped it down to them out of the sky. The note told them that help was coming, that they had been seen, that they should hold on. The people on the ground said afterward that the note meant everything. They had been sitting in that snow wondering if anybody in the world knew where they were. And then a piece of paper came fluttering down out of the sky to tell them somebody did.

And here is the thing that I want you to hear, because it is the most remarkable fact in this whole story, and it is plain history. With all of those hundreds of people stranded in the high country of the Smokies, in one of the worst storms in the recorded history of the eastern United States, no hikers died in the park during that blizzard. Not one. There was frostbite. There was misery. There were people who never forgot it as long as they lived. One man who was rescued said years later that he came away from it believing that tomorrow is a gift. But everybody who was up on that mountain when the snow started came down off it.

Elsewhere in the country, the storm was not so merciful. Across the whole eastern United States, it killed something like three hundred people, by most counts, from heart attacks while shoveling, from car wrecks, from cold, from roofs falling in, from storms at sea. But in the Smokies, the rangers and the guardsmen and the pilots, and the hikers themselves who had the sense to stay put, got everybody home.

And down in the valleys, the mountain towns were having their own hard week. In western North Carolina and east Tennessee, the power went out across huge stretches of country, and in a good many of the hollows it stayed out for days. Roads were blocked by fallen trees and drifts. The National Guard was called out to clear the way and to reach people who were snowed in. Old folks up at the heads of the hollows burned their wood and waited, the way their parents and grandparents had waited out hard winters before them. A great many people in those mountains still date things by that storm. They will tell you something happened the year before the blizzard, or the year after it.

Now I said I would raise the skeptic's objection, and here is where it goes.

The skeptic says, well, that was the storm of the century. It is called that because it happens once in a hundred years. What happened in nineteen ninety-three is not a lesson about March in the mountains. It is a freak. You cannot plan your life around something that may not happen again until all of us are dead and gone.

And it is true that the nineteen ninety-three storm was something special. But the skeptic is wrong about the lesson. Because the lesson is not that a once-in-a-century storm can hit the Smokies in March. The lesson is that the high mountains are still in winter in March, every single year, whether or not the valleys know it.

Here is what I mean. Down in Gatlinburg or Cherokee or Asheville, in March, it can be sixty or seventy degrees on a sunny afternoon. The daffodils are up. People are in shirtsleeves. But the high crest of the Smokies is more than a mile above sea level, and up there the temperature runs a good deal colder than it does in the valley. As you go up a mountain, the air cools as you climb, and on the highest peaks of the Smokies it can easily be twenty degrees colder than in the town below. So a pleasant spring afternoon in Gatlinburg can be a cold, raw, windy day up on the crest, with snow still lying in the shady places. And when a real winter storm comes through, which it does in March in most years, though not nearly as badly as it did in nineteen ninety-three, the high country gets it as winter, not as spring.

The rangers in the Smokies rescue people every spring who walked up into the high country dressed for the weather in the parking lot. Not in a storm of the century. In an ordinary cold March rain, at forty degrees, with wind, which is about the most dangerous weather there is for a person who is not dressed for it. That is not a freak. That is March.

And the old mountain people knew all about this. That brings me to the folklore, and I want to give it to you in the right voice.

I grew up hearing about the little winters. Every family up here had them. The story goes that spring in the mountains does not come all at once. It comes in fits and starts, and after every warm spell there is a cold snap, and the old people had a name for every one of them. Redbud winter, when the cold comes back just as the redbuds bloom. Dogwood winter, when the dogwoods flower and the frost comes again. Locust winter. Blackberry winter, late in the spring, when the blackberry briars are in bloom and you need your coat one more time. Some families had a whippoorwill winter, and a linsey-woolsey britches winter, which was the very last cold snap, when you could finally put away your heavy winter underclothes. Folks say you should never plant your garden until all the little winters have come and gone, and the old timers would tell you how many there were going to be that year by watching the trees.

Now, nobody can prove that the dogwoods know when the frost is coming. But there is something real in those old sayings. Spring in the mountains really does come in waves, with cold snaps between them, and the old people who lived up in the hollows watched that pattern every year of their lives, and they gave it names so they would not forget it. Every name was a warning. Do not trust the warm spell. Winter is not done.

And there were other weather signs that folks swore by. Folks say a ring around the moon means bad weather is coming, and the more stars inside the ring, the more days until it comes. That one, as it happens, has something to it. A ring around the moon is caused by high, thin clouds made of ice crystals, and those clouds often run out ahead of a storm system. Folks say that if it thunders in the wintertime, it will snow within seven days. I could not tell you whether that one holds up, and nobody can prove it. Folks say that when the cattle bunch up and lie down in the field, and the hogs carry straw in their mouths, a storm is coming. The story goes that the old people could smell snow in the air. I have known people who said they could, and I have no reason to doubt them.

What the old people did not have was a weather radio, or a phone that could tell them what the forecasters were seeing three days off. We have that. And the cruel thing about the hikers of nineteen ninety-three is that a lot of the information was out there. The forecast was out there. The old people's little winters were out there. The fact that the high country runs twenty degrees colder than the valley was out there. What was missing was somebody putting all of it together before they shouldered their pack and walked up into the snow line.

So let me keep my promise. Here is the rule. It is in the title of this story. If it feels like spring, do not trust the mountain.

What that means, in plain terms, is this. Until well into May, when you go up into the high country of the southern mountains, you pack for winter. Not for the weather in the parking lot. For the weather on top. A warm insulating layer. A hat and gloves. A rain jacket that will actually keep out rain and wind. A sleeping bag that will keep you alive below freezing if you are staying out overnight. Extra food, a day or two more than you think you need, because in the mountains, plans change.

You check the forecast before you go, and not the forecast for the town. The forecast for the high elevations. The park puts it out, and so do the weather services. And if there is a big storm coming, you do not go up, no matter how many months you have been planning the trip, and no matter how warm it is down in the valley that morning. The trail will be there next week.

You tell somebody exactly where you are going and when you expect to be out, and you do not change the plan without telling them.

And if a storm catches you up there anyway, you remember what saved the hikers of nineteen ninety-three. You get to shelter, and you stay there. You do not try to fight your way out through deep snow. You pool your warmth with the people you are with. You keep your sleeping bag dry. You eat, because food is heat. And you wait for the weather to break and for help to come, because when people know you are up there, help will come.

The shelters along the crest of the Smokies are still there, most of them. Stone walls and a roof and an open front. Hikers sleep in them every night of the year. And every March, people walk up past the daffodils in Gatlinburg into country that is still in winter, wearing shorts, carrying a light bag, sure that spring has come. Most of the time, they are lucky. The weather holds. They come down sunburned and happy.

I think about that pilot, sometimes. Flying over the white mountains in a machine that could not lift anybody, looking down at a handful of people in the snow, and doing the only thing he could do. Writing on a piece of paper. We see you. Hold on. It is about the best thing one person has ever done for another in those mountains, as far as I know. And it reminds me of what those people needed most, sitting up there in the drifts. Not just food, and not just warmth. They needed somebody to know where they were.

The old people named every little winter so they would never forget that spring is a promise the mountains do not always keep. That is all the rule is, really. The valleys may be in spring. The mountain keeps its own calendar.
