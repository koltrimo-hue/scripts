A Saturday night in eastern Kentucky, high on a sandstone ridge. A group of young people have hiked out to a rock point to watch the stars come out over a deep gorge. There is a cooler. There is laughing. Somebody has a flashlight, and somebody else has a phone with the light on. Below the rock, it is so dark you cannot tell how far down the trees go. One of the young men gets up to go and stand right at the edge, to look out, or to step a little way off from the others in the dark. His friends hear his footsteps on the rock. Then they do not hear them. And nobody hears him fall.

Before I go on — I put together something that goes with these stories. It is called the Holler Field Manual, the real rules for staying alive out in these mountains, not the legend part, the part that actually keeps you breathing. Link is in the description. Now, back to it.

That is the Red River Gorge, in the Daniel Boone National Forest of eastern Kentucky. It is one of the most beautiful places in the whole Appalachian country, and one of the strangest. And for decades, it has had a reputation among the rescue people and the rangers and the folks who live around it that does not show up on the postcards. People die there. Not in great numbers. Not in disasters. One at a time, a young person here, a young person there, year after year. And when the people whose job it is to carry them out sat down and looked at the records, they found that the same story was being told over and over again, almost word for word.

So here is what I am going to do. I am going to tell you what the Red River Gorge is, and why it is shaped the way it is, because the shape of it is the whole danger. I am going to give you the plain numbers that the Forest Service put together, and they are hard numbers to hear. I am going to tell you the old legends of the Gorge, because it has a great many, a lost silver mine and a haunted tunnel among them, and I will mark them plainly as legend. And by the end, I am going to tell you the rule, the one in the title, and who exactly it is meant for, because the records are very clear about who it is meant for. Stay with me.

Let us begin with the land itself.

The Red River Gorge is about an hour east of Lexington, Kentucky, where the rolling bluegrass country starts to break up into hills and hollows. It is not a single canyon the way you might picture it. It is a whole tangle of deep, narrow valleys, cut down into a high, flat plateau by the Red River and the creeks that run into it. The plateau on top is capped by a thick layer of hard sandstone. And over millions of years, the water and the weather have carved that sandstone into cliffs, ledges, rock shelters, pillars, and arches. More than a hundred natural stone arches, by most counts, more than almost anywhere else east of the Rocky Mountains. Some of them are huge, great spans of rock you can walk across, with the treetops far below. Some of them are small, hidden back in the woods where only a few people ever see them.

Under the cliffs, all through the Gorge, the softer rock below the sandstone cap has worn away, and left great overhangs. Rock shelters, people call them. Some of them are big enough to park a house under. They stay dry when it rains, and out of the wind, and they face out over the creeks, and people have been living in them, or camping in them, for thousands of years. Archaeologists working in the dry dust of those shelters have reported finding some of the oldest evidence anywhere in eastern North America of people growing their own plants, seeds of gourds and squash and native grains saved and planted by people who lived there long, long before any European set foot on this continent. Much later, in the early eighteen hundreds, men came into those same shelters to dig the dirt out of the floors and boil it down for saltpeter, which they needed to make gunpowder. You can still find the old wooden vats and the troughs they used in some of the shelters. And later still, around the turn of the last century, the timber companies came in and cut the big trees off the ridges and out of the hollows, and the logging railroads ran up the creeks. So the Gorge that looks so wild today has been lived in, and worked, and cut, and lived in again, for a very long time. It is wild now because it was let go, and because people fought to keep it that way.

And here is the thing about that country that you need to understand. On top of the ridges, it is mostly flat. You walk out along a ridgetop trail through the pines and the oaks, and it feels like a walk in a park. The ground is level. The trees are close around you. And then, without any warning at all, the trees stop, and the ground stops, and there is a sheer drop of fifty or a hundred feet or more, straight down a sandstone cliff into the gorge. There is often no slope leading up to it. There is no gradual steepening. Just level woods, and then air.

In daylight, that is breathtaking. People come from all over the country to stand on those rock points and look out. The Red River Gorge has become one of the best known rock climbing places in the world. Climbers come from Europe and from Asia to climb those sandstone walls. Hikers come by the hundreds of thousands. Families come to Natural Bridge, a great stone arch in the state park at the edge of the Gorge, and ride the sky lift up and walk across it. In daylight, the edges are easy to see, and most people are sensible about them.

At night, it is a very different place.

I want to say a word about why that is, because it is not only that it is dark. Our eyes are poor tools at night. It takes the human eye something like half an hour or more to adjust fully to the dark, and every time you look at a bright light, a phone screen, a lantern, a campfire, you lose a good part of that adjustment all at once. So people sitting around a fire or a lantern at a campsite near a cliff are close to blind the moment they step out of the circle of light. And in the dark, we lose our sense of depth. A cliff edge with trees growing right up to it looks exactly like more trees. The far side of the gorge, a quarter mile away, looks like it could be the next ridge over, right there in front of you. A flashlight beam shining out over an edge simply disappears into nothing, and your mind does not always understand what that nothing means. In the daytime, your eyes tell you where the ground ends. At night, they lie to you. And the Gorge does not leave a lot of room for mistakes.

Now let me give you the numbers, because they are the part of this story that is plain history, and they are the reason for the rule.

The Forest Service, which manages the Red River Gorge, kept records of the deaths in the Gorge for a long stretch of years. And according to those records, from nineteen sixty to two thousand five, there were seventy-three deaths reported in the Red River Gorge. Seventy-three people in forty-five years, in one small corner of one national forest in Kentucky.

And when they looked at who those people were, and how they died, a pattern jumped out of the records that nobody could miss. Most of them were young men. Most of them fell from cliffs. Most of them fell at night. And in a great many of the cases, alcohol was part of the story. The records also showed that nineteen of those deaths came after the person had climbed over a barrier, a railing or a fence put up specifically to keep people back from a dangerous edge, to get out onto a rock outcrop beyond it.

The Forest Service records showed something else, too. Nine out of every ten of the people injured or killed in the Gorge in those years came from Ohio and from northern Kentucky. Not from the mountain communities right around the Gorge. From the cities and the suburbs a few hours north. People who had driven down for the weekend. A rescue coordinator who worked the Gorge described the typical person they get called out for at night as a young man somewhere between eighteen and twenty-five.

And it did not stop in two thousand five. The deaths have gone on since then, every so often, in the same places and in the same ways. People have fallen from the top of the rock at a place called Chimney Top, one of the most popular overlooks in the Gorge. Chimney Top Rock is a good example of what I mean. You drive out a gravel road along a ridge, park, and walk a short, easy path out through the pines to a great sandstone point that sticks out over the gorge, with the Red River winding along far below. It is one of the finest views in Kentucky, and it takes about ten minutes to get to from the car. There is a railing along part of it. And past the railing, the rock keeps going, out toward the edge, and on a warm evening there are almost always people out there past the railing, sitting with their feet hanging over. One field report from a few years back counted four or five more people who had fallen from the top of that one rock in the years after two thousand five. Ten minutes from the car, on an easy path.

People have fallen from the arches. A young man from Ohio, twenty-four years old, died after falling something like forty feet at Whittleton Arch. There have been young women too. Every year or so, the newspapers in Lexington and Cincinnati and Louisville carry another one.

And every time, somebody has to go and get them. The Gorge is covered by volunteer search and rescue squads from the counties around it, along with the Forest Service and the state police. These are mostly local folks, men and women with day jobs, who keep ropes and litters and radios and go out when the phone rings, at any hour, in any weather. When somebody falls from a cliff in the Gorge at night, those people have to find the spot, often from a confused or hysterical phone call from friends who do not know where they are, and then they have to get down to the bottom of a cliff in the dark, through rhododendron and boulders, and then they have to bring the person back out. Sometimes that is a rescue, with a badly hurt young person in a litter, and a long careful haul up a rope system, hour after hour, through the night. And sometimes it is not a rescue at all. The people who do that work have been doing it for a very long time, and they have carried a great many young people out of that Gorge. You will notice that they are the ones who repeat the rule most often.

I am not going to give you their names. Their families have heard this story enough. What I want you to see is the shape of it. Because it is the same shape every time.

Now, the skeptic will speak up here, and I want to give him his turn.

The skeptic says, well, that is not the Gorge's fault. Those were young men drinking at night on top of cliffs, climbing over fences. That is just plain foolishness. You could fall off any cliff anywhere if you were drunk in the dark. It is not a story about the woods. It is a story about bad decisions.

And the skeptic is not wrong about the decisions. But I think he misses the point. Because the reason the Red River Gorge kills young people at night, and not, say, the Smokies or the Blue Ridge, has a great deal to do with the Gorge itself. It is the shape of the land. Those level ridgetops that end in sheer drops with no warning. The trees right up to the very edge, so that you cannot see the drop until you are on it. The rock shelters and the campsites that sit right near the cliffs. The fact that the Gorge is close enough to the cities that people can drive down after work on a Friday and be up on a ridge by dark. And the fact that, in the daylight, it all looks so gentle. A young man who walked a trail at three in the afternoon and saw how level and easy it was, and who walks it back at midnight, is not walking the same trail. He just thinks he is.

And here is the other thing the skeptic misses. It does not take a drink and a railing to get into trouble up there. Think of a perfectly sober person who steps off the trail in the dark to relieve themselves, or to look for a better spot for a tent, or to go after a dog that ran off, and walks a few steps into what looks like more woods. In that country, a few steps is all it takes.

So that is the plain history. Let me tell you some of the legends now, because the Gorge has plenty of them, and they belong in this story too.

The oldest legend in that country is the legend of John Swift's silver mine. The story goes that back in the seventeen hundreds, before Daniel Boone and the long hunters came through the Cumberland Gap into Kentucky, a man named John Swift and his men came into those hills and found a vein of silver. Some tellers say they mined it for years, coming and going, and that they melted the silver down into bars and hid them in the rocks, in caves and under ledges, meaning to come back for them. The story goes that Swift kept a journal, describing the landmarks, the creeks and the rocks and the arches that would lead a man back to the mine. And the story goes that Swift went blind in his old age and could never find his way back, and died without telling anybody exactly where it was.

Nobody can prove any of it. Historians argue about whether John Swift was even a real person, or whether there was ever any silver, or whether the journal that has been passed around for two centuries is anything but a tall tale. Accounts differ on almost every detail, even on what century he was supposed to have come through. But the legend has never died. There is a Swift Camp Creek in the Gorge. There is a Swift Silvermine Arch. And people have been crawling under ledges and digging in rock shelters all over eastern Kentucky for more than two hundred years looking for that silver. Some of the old men who grew up near the Gorge will tell you their granddaddies spent half their lives looking for it.

And then there is the tunnel. If you drive into the Gorge from the north on Kentucky Route seventy-seven, you will come to a hole in the side of the mountain. It is called the Nada Tunnel. It is about nine hundred feet long, one lane wide, unlit, and rough-walled, and when you drive into it, you cannot see the other end at first. It was built back around nineteen ten and nineteen eleven, by a lumber company called the Dana Lumber Company, for a logging railroad that hauled timber out of the hollows to a sawmill. The crews blasted and dug it from both sides of the ridge, by hand and with dynamite, and met in the middle. The name of the tunnel, and of the little logging town it led to, came from flipping the name of the company around. Dana became Nada. After the timber was cut out, the railroad was shut down around nineteen twenty, the tracks were pulled up, and the tunnel became part of the road. That part is plain history.

But folks say the Nada Tunnel is haunted. The story goes that men died building it, in the blasting and the cave-ins, and that their spirits are still down there in the dark. People say they have heard voices in the tunnel, and footsteps, and the sound of picks on stone. People say their car died in the middle of the tunnel for no reason, and that they felt hands on the windows. Some say you can see a light at the far end that is not the far end at all. Nobody can prove a word of it, and I am telling it to you as a ghost story. But every carload of college kids driving down to the Gorge on a Friday night knows about the Nada Tunnel, and most of them roll down their windows and holler as they go through.

And I want you to notice something about both of those legends. The silver mine, and the tunnel. They are both stories about people who went down into the dark places of that country, under the ledges and inside the rock, and did not come back out. The old people who told those stories may not have known it, but they were telling the same story the Forest Service records tell. The Gorge is a place where the ground has a way of opening up under you.

There is one more piece of history I want to give you, because it has something to say about how much people love that country. Back in the nineteen sixties, the government had a plan to build a dam on the Red River and flood a large part of the Gorge, to make a lake. There was a long, hard fight over it. People who loved the Gorge, hikers and naturalists and folks from the communities around it, fought to save it. The accounts say that William O. Douglas, a justice of the United States Supreme Court and a great outdoorsman, came down and hiked the Gorge himself in the fall of nineteen sixty-seven, to draw attention to it and help stop the dam. In the end, the dam was never built where it would have drowned the Gorge. The arches and the cliffs are still there because people cared enough to fight for them.

That is worth remembering when you think about the danger. The cliffs are not the enemy. They are the reason people go there. The Red River Gorge is not a place to stay away from. It is a place to be careful in.

So let me keep my promise. Here is the rule. Never walk the cliffs of the Red River Gorge after dark.

And I want to tell you exactly what that means, because the people who carry the bodies out of there have said it plainly enough for years.

If you are going to be up on a ridgetop in the Gorge, plan to be off the edges before the light goes. Watch the sunset from an overlook if you want to, but leave yourself time to walk back to your car or your camp while you can still see the ground. If you are camping, do not camp near a cliff. Pick a spot well back from any edge, and before it gets dark, walk around your camp and learn where the edges are. And when you get up in the night, and everybody does, you take a light, and you do not go wandering off past the edge of your camp in the dark, not for any reason.

Carry a real headlamp, with fresh batteries, not just the light on your phone. A phone light does not show you far enough ahead, and the battery dies, and you need your hands.

Know where you are. Cell service in the Gorge is poor in a great many places, down in the hollows especially, and if something does go wrong, the people coming to help you need to know which trail, which overlook, which arch. Before you head out, tell somebody back home the name of the trail you are walking and the place you plan to camp. Write it down for yourself, too. A frightened friend on a phone at midnight, trying to tell a dispatcher where they are, with the battery running down and no idea of the name of the rock they are standing on, is a very common call. A few words written down before dark can save the searchers an hour, and an hour can matter.

Stay on the trail, and stay behind the railings. Those barriers are not there to spoil your view. Every one of them is there because somebody, at some point, went over that edge. The records say nineteen people died after climbing over barriers like that. Nineteen.

And the hardest part of the rule, the part the rescue people repeat most often, is this. If you are going to drink, do not do it up on the ridge. Do it back at the campground, or the cabin, or the town, on level ground, far from any edge. Alcohol and cliffs in the dark have killed more young people in the Red River Gorge than anything else in its history.

And if you are the friend of the young man who wants to go stand on the edge at midnight to show off, or to see the stars better, you be the one who says no. You be the one who grabs his arm. A great many of the people who died there had friends standing ten feet away.

I think about the hook of this story, sometimes. That group of young people on the rock at night, with the cooler and the laughing and the stars coming out over the gorge. There is nothing in that picture that is wicked, or wrong. It is about as fine a thing as being young can be. That is what makes it so hard. They did not go up there to take a risk. They went up there because it was beautiful. And the edge was just there, in the dark, a few steps away, the way it always is in that country.

The arches are still standing. The silver, if it was ever there, is still hidden. The cars still go honking through the Nada Tunnel on Friday nights. And every so often, on a Saturday night, the phone still rings at the rescue squad, and they go up the ridge with their lights and their ropes to look for somebody who stepped off into the dark. The Gorge does not change. It does not want anything from anybody. It is just shaped the way it is shaped. All we can do is learn where the edges are, while there is still light to see them by.
