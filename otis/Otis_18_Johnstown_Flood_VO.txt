On a Friday afternoon at the end of May, a railroad engineer named John Hess was sitting in his locomotive in a little town in the mountains of Pennsylvania when he heard a sound he had never heard in his life.

It was coming down the valley behind him. A roar, the men who lived through it said, like a hundred freight trains at once, and under the roar, a grinding and a cracking, like a whole forest being torn up by the roots. Because that is what it was.

John Hess did not wait to see it. He tied down the cord of his train whistle, so it would scream and not stop screaming. And he threw his engine into motion and raced down the track toward the towns below, with that whistle wide open, warning every soul he passed.

Behind him, a wall of water, as tall as a house in some places and taller in others, was coming down the valley at the speed of a running horse. Full of trees, and houses, and freight cars, and people.

It was heading for a town called Johnstown. And about fifty-seven minutes after the dam broke, it got there.

When I was a young man, friend, the old folks had a saying. When it rained hard, real hard, for days, and the creeks came up brown and the roads washed out, somebody would stand in the door of my store looking out at it and say, well, it's raining like the Johnstown flood.

Everybody knew what that meant. Even folks who could not have told you where Johnstown was. For two or three generations of Americans, the Johnstown flood was just the word for the worst thing water could do.

And I never thought about it much, until a lady who traded with me told me her grandmother had been in it. Let me start at the bottom of the valley. With the town.

Johnstown, Pennsylvania, sits down in a deep bowl in the Allegheny Mountains, right where two rivers come together, the Little Conemaugh and Stony Creek, to make the Conemaugh River. The mountains come up steep all around it. The town filled up the flat ground at the bottom like water fills a cup.

And in eighteen eighty-nine, it was a working town. A steel town. The Cambria Iron Company had its big mills there, some of the biggest in the country, making iron and steel for the railroads. Thousands of men worked in those mills. Welshmen and Germans and Irish and folks from the farms around, and more coming every year from across the ocean. Counting all the little boroughs packed in around it, something like thirty thousand people lived in that valley.

And Johnstown flooded. It always had. Two rivers meeting in a bowl, in a country of steep mountains and heavy rain. Most springs, the water came up in the streets. Folks were used to it. They moved the furniture upstairs, and waited, and mopped up after. Hold on to that, friend. Folks were used to it. It is going to matter more than anything.

Now let me take you up the valley. Fourteen miles up the Little Conemaugh, up past the town of South Fork, up in the hills.

There was a lake up there.

It had not always been there. Back before the Civil War, the state of Pennsylvania had built a canal across the state, and canals need water. So the state built a dam up on a creek in those hills, an earth dam, to make a reservoir to feed water to the canal. And it was not a small dam, friend. The accounts make it something like seventy feet high and the better part of a thousand feet long. A great wall of packed earth and rock, across a whole valley. When it was built right, it was one of the biggest earth dams in the country. It took years to build. And almost as soon as it was finished, the railroad came through and made the canal worthless. Nobody needed a canal anymore.

So the dam just sat there. The state sold it to the Pennsylvania Railroad. The railroad did not want it. The dam was neglected. It broke partway once, in the eighteen sixties, but the lake was low then and not much harm was done. Later on, a man bought the property and, the accounts say, took out the big iron pipes at the bottom of the dam, the ones that let the water out in a controlled way, and sold them for scrap.

Hold on to those pipes, friend. The pipes that let the water out. They were gone.

And then, in eighteen seventy-nine, some very rich men from Pittsburgh had an idea.

Pittsburgh was about sixty miles away, the biggest iron and steel city in the world, and it was smoky and dirty and loud, and the men who had made their fortunes there wanted somewhere to go in the summer. Somewhere cool. Somewhere with a lake for fishing and sailing. And that old dam, with a little repair, would make a beautiful lake, up in the cool green mountains, just a train ride from the city.

So they formed a club. They called it the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club.

And friend, I want you to hear who was in that club. The accounts say there were about sixty members by the time of the flood. They fixed up the old dam. And the accounts say they fixed it cheap. They filled the breaks with whatever came to hand, rock and mud and brush and hay, and even, by some accounts, manure. They did not put back the pipes at the bottom. And they made two changes that would matter more than anything else.

First, they lowered the top of the dam. Cut it down a few feet, so that the road across the top of it would be wide enough for two carriages to pass. So the club members and their families could drive across to their cottages in comfort.

And second, they put screens across the spillway. The spillway is the low place at the side of a dam, where water can run out if the lake gets too high, so it does not go over the top. And the club put iron screens across it, the accounts say, to keep their fish from escaping out of the lake. Because they had stocked that lake with expensive fish, for their sport.

So now, friend, you have an old dam, patched with mud and brush. With no pipes at the bottom to let water out. With the top cut lower. And with screens across the only other way out for the water, screens that would catch every stick and leaf and log that floated down.

And behind it, the lake. About two miles long. The biggest man-made lake in that part of the country. With a clubhouse on the shore, and cottages, and sailboats, and fine ladies with parasols, and children playing on the lawn.

And fourteen miles below it, at the bottom of the valley, thirty thousand people who worked in the mills.

The accounts say he even joined the club himself, so he would have some say. And around eighteen eighty, he sent an engineer up to look at the dam. Not much was done. Morrell died a few years later. And the dam sat there, year after year, holding back the lake, and every spring the water came up in the streets of Johnstown, and every spring folks said, one of these days that dam is going to go. The accounts say six to ten inches of rain fell in a day. Every creek in those mountains came up out of its banks. And the lake behind the South Fork dam started to rise.

The next morning, Friday, the thirty-first, the president of the club, a man named Elias Unger, looked out at the lake from the clubhouse. And the lake had come up something like two feet overnight. And it was still coming up.

And to give Elias Unger his due, friend, and I want to, he did not sit still. He got every man he could find, club workers and Italian laborers who were working nearby, and they went at that dam. They tried to raise the top with a plow and shovels, piling dirt on it. They tried to cut a new spillway on the other side, to let water out. The screens would not come out. They were jammed with everything the storm had brought down.
