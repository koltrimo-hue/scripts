On the first day of August, nineteen twenty-seven, a borrowed car came bouncing down a dirt road out of a little valley in southwest Virginia, headed for the city of Bristol.

There were three grown people in it, and a baby. A tall, nervous man with a tremble in his hands. His wife, a dark-haired young woman with a voice like a church bell, nursing the baby on her lap. And the wife's cousin, a girl of eighteen, holding a guitar, and so far along with her own first child that she could barely get comfortable in the seat.

They had a flat tire on the way, the story goes. They had to ford a river. It took them most of the day to go about twenty-five miles.

And when they got to Bristol, they climbed the stairs to the upper floor of a building on State Street, where a man from a big record company in New Jersey had set up some microphones in a room with blankets hung on the walls. And they sang him a few old songs they had grown up with.

They were paid, by the accounts, about fifty dollars a song. And then they drove home.

And in the years that followed, those three people, the Carter Family, would change American music so completely that a good many folks who study it say country music as we know it started in that room, that week.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I grew up on the Carter Family, friend. Everybody in these hills did.

When I was a boy, there was a wind-up phonograph in my grandmother's front room, a big wooden one with a crank on the side, and a stack of records in a cabinet underneath. And half of those records were the Carter Family. I would sit on the floor in front of that thing and crank it up and listen to Sara Carter sing about weeping willows and lonesome pines and little log cabins, and Maybelle's guitar under it, and A. P. coming in on the bass every so often like a man who had just remembered he was supposed to be singing.

And later on, when I had my store, I would leave the radio on behind the counter in the evenings. And sometimes, late, if the weather was right, I could pick up a station from way down on the Mexican border. And there would be the Carters again.

And then a strange thing happened, friend. The older I got, the more I learned about those three people, the sadder their songs sounded to me. Because their own lives turned out to be a song. One of the sad ones.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to tell you the story of the Carter Family through their songs. A song for each part of it. The way you would tell it if you were sitting around a radio.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you about the last song. The one they are best remembered for. And I am going to tell you what I believe it was really about. Because I think it was about them.

Let me start in Poor Valley.

Up in the far southwestern corner of Virginia, in Scott County, there is a long narrow valley that runs along under a ridge called Clinch Mountain. The folks who lived there called it Poor Valley, and they meant it. The good farmland was on the other side of the mountain. Poor Valley was rocky and thin. A family there raised a little corn on the hillsides, and kept a cow and some chickens, and the men went off to work on the railroad or in the timber or wherever they could find a wage, and came home on Saturday. Clinch Mountain stood over the whole valley like a wall, blue in the evening. And you will hear that mountain in a good many of the old songs, friend. Folks sang about it the way other folks sang about the sea. And in a little settlement there called Maces Spring, in eighteen ninety-one, a boy was born named Alvin Pleasant Carter.

Everybody called him A. P., or Doc.

And from the time he was a boy, his hands shook. A tremble, a palsy, that he had all his life. And there is a story that was told in that valley about why, and I give it to you exactly as it was given to me. The story is that when his mother was carrying him, she was out under an apple tree, and lightning struck the tree, and it ran through her. And the baby was born with a tremble in his hands, and in his voice when he sang.

I cannot tell you if that is so, friend. But I can tell you that folks in Poor Valley believed it. And I can tell you that when you hear A. P. Carter come in on those old records, you can hear the tremble.

A. P. was a restless man. He could not sit still. He worked at a lot of things. He sold fruit trees, walking up and down the hollows of Virginia and Tennessee, going house to house with his catalogs. And everywhere he went, he listened. He listened to what people sang. Old women on their porches. Men in the fields. Church singing on Sunday. And he wrote the songs down, or carried them home in his head.

Hold on to that, friend. A man walking the hollows, collecting songs. That is going to be the whole Carter Family before we are done.

Now the first song. Wildwood Flower.

On one of his fruit tree trips, the story goes, A. P. heard a girl singing. A girl over in the next valley, playing an autoharp, singing an old song. Her name was Sara Dougherty. She was a tall, dark, quiet girl with the deepest, plainest, most beautiful singing voice anybody in those mountains had ever heard. Not fancy. Not pretty, exactly. Strong, and lonesome, and true. Like a church bell in a cold valley.

And A. P. Carter fell for her. And in nineteen fifteen, they were married. She was about sixteen. He was about twenty-four.

They lived in Poor Valley, and they had babies, and they sang. At church. At home. At gatherings. Sara sang lead and played the autoharp, and A. P. sang bass, when he felt like it, and the rest of the time walked around the room.

And then there was the cousin.

Her name was Maybelle Addington. She was Sara's cousin, about ten years younger. And she could play the guitar like nobody had ever heard. And in nineteen twenty-six, she married A. P.'s younger brother, a man named Ezra, who everybody called Eck. So now Maybelle was a Carter too.

And Maybelle had worked out a way of playing the guitar that was all her own. She played the tune, the melody, down on the low strings with her thumb, and at the same time she brushed the higher strings with her fingers for the rhythm. So it sounded like two guitars at once. Folks later called it the Carter scratch. And every country guitar picker who came after her learned it, or learned from somebody who learned it from her.

Wildwood Flower was one of the first songs she ever played that way on a record. And if you ever learned to play guitar in this country, friend, there is a good chance it was one of the first songs you learned too.

So that was the three of them. A. P., the restless song hunter. Sara, the voice. Maybelle, the guitar.

Now the second song. Bury Me Under the Weeping Willow.

In the summer of nineteen twenty-seven, a man named Ralph Peer came south. He worked for the Victor Talking Machine Company, the biggest record company in the country. And he had figured out something that most of the big city record men had not. That the folks in the country, in the mountains and the farms of the South, would buy records too. Records of their own kind of music. Old songs. Fiddle tunes. Hymns. If somebody would only make them.

So Ralph Peer brought his recording machines to Bristol. Bristol is a town that sits right on the line between Tennessee and Virginia. The state line runs right down the middle of its main street, State Street. You can stand with one foot in Tennessee and one foot in Virginia. And Peer rented space up on the upper floors of a building on the Tennessee side, a building that belonged to a hat company, and he hung blankets on the walls to deaden the echo, and set up his microphones.

And he got the word out. There was a story in the Bristol newspaper, the accounts say, that told about a mountain musician who had made thousands of dollars in a year from making records. The musician was a fellow named Ernest Stoneman, from up in the Blue Ridge of Virginia, a carpenter who played the harmonica and the guitar and the autoharp and sang the old songs. Peer had brought him along to Bristol to help, and to record. And when folks read in the paper how much Ernest Stoneman had made, friend, the hat company stairs got crowded. Farmers, coal miners, preachers, fiddlers. Folks who had never thought of their music as anything but something you did on a Saturday night. Thousands of dollars, friend. For singing. In nineteen twenty-seven, in those mountains, that was more money than a man could make in ten years of farming. And musicians came from all over the hills to try their luck.

And A. P. Carter heard about it. And he talked Sara and Maybelle into going.

That is the car ride I told you about. The first of August, nineteen twenty-seven. A borrowed car, the accounts say. A dirt road. A river to ford. Sara with her baby on her lap, nursing him. That baby was a boy named Joe, just a few months old. He grew up to sing the old songs himself, with his sister Janette, many years later. Maybelle, eighteen years old and seven months along with her first child, with her guitar. A. P. driving, and fretting, the way he always fretted.

They climbed the stairs in that hat company building. And Ralph Peer, the accounts say, was not sure what to make of them at first. They looked like what they were. Country people, in their country clothes, plain as could be.

And then Sara Carter opened her mouth.

Ralph Peer said later, the way it has come down, that as soon as he heard Sara's voice, he knew. That was it. That was the sound.

They recorded four songs that first day. And a few weeks after that, in September, back home in Poor Valley, Maybelle had her baby, a little girl named Helen. So that baby, friend, was at the big bang of country music too, in her way, riding along in that car before she was even born. And Bury Me Under the Weeping Willow was one of them. The next day they came back and did two more. And then they got in the car and drove home to Poor Valley, and went back to their gardens and their babies, and waited to see if anything would come of it.

And a few days after the Carters, in that same room, a young man named Jimmie Rodgers recorded his first two songs too. A railroad brakeman from Mississippi, thin and sick, with a guitar and a yodel. And he went on to be the first great star of country music, the Singing Brakeman, the Father of Country Music. And he died of tuberculosis in a hotel room in New York City not six years later. But that is another story. Though I will give you one piece of it, because it belongs here. In nineteen thirty-one, Ralph Peer put Jimmie Rodgers and the Carter Family together in a recording studio in Kentucky. The two biggest acts of that week in Bristol, side by side. And they made a few records together, little skits and songs, with Jimmie joking and the Carters singing along. And if you ever hear them, friend, listen to Jimmie Rodgers. He sounds happy. He was already very sick. He knew it. He kept working right up to the end, because he had a family to provide for, and the accounts say that on his last recording trip he had to rest on a cot between songs. He was thirty-five when he died.

In those two weeks in Bristol, the accounts say, Ralph Peer recorded something like nineteen different acts, and some seventy-odd songs. And out of that one room on State Street came the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers both.

That is why folks call it the big bang of country music, friend. And that is why Bristol calls itself where it all began.

Now the third song. Keep on the Sunny Side.

The records came out. And they sold. And they kept selling. Folks all over the South, and then all over the country, bought those Carter Family records and played them on their wind-up machines in their front rooms, the way my grandmother did. And Ralph Peer brought them back to record again, and again. Over the next years, the Carter Family recorded something like three hundred songs.

And A. P. kept on hunting. That was his gift, friend. He did not write most of the songs. He found them. He went up and down those mountains, the way he had with his fruit trees, and listened to old women and old men and church folks, and wrote down the old ballads and hymns that had been carried over from Scotland and Ireland and England two hundred years before, and the songs that mountain people had made up since. And then he fixed them up, and put them together, and the Carters sang them. And Ralph Peer copyrighted them in A. P.'s name, which was how the business worked then.

And here is a part of the story that does not get told enough, friend. A good deal of that song hunting, A. P. did not do alone. He did it with a young Black man named Lesley Riddle.

Lesley Riddle was a guitar player from over around Kingsport, Tennessee, who had lost a leg in an accident when he was young. And he could play, and he had a memory for songs like nothing you ever saw. And A. P. would take him along on his song hunting trips. A. P. could not read music, and could not always remember a tune once he heard it. So he would find the song and get the words, and Lesley Riddle would listen and learn the tune, and carry it home in his head, and teach it to the Carters. And Lesley Riddle taught Maybelle some of his guitar playing too, the accounts say.

A white man and a Black man, walking the back roads of Virginia and Tennessee together in the nineteen twenties and thirties, hunting songs. That was not a common thing in those days, friend. And for a long time, Lesley Riddle got almost no credit for it. I want to give him some.

Keep on the Sunny Side was the Carter Family's theme song. They opened their shows with it. It was an old gospel song, about keeping your hope up even in the hard times. And the hard times came. The Depression came down on the whole country. Record sales fell off. The accounts say record sales in this country fell by something like nine parts out of ten in just a few years. Folks who could not buy flour were not buying phonograph records. And the record companies cut back, and some went under. And the Carters went out and played anywhere that would have them. Schoolhouses. Churches. Little halls up the hollows.

A. P. made up the handbills himself, the accounts say. And at the bottom of them, he put a line I have always loved. It said, the program is morally good.

The program is morally good, friend. That was the selling point.

I knew a man who went to one of those schoolhouse shows as a boy, and he told me about it at my counter. He said there were maybe sixty people in that room, on the benches, and coal oil lamps, and the three of them up front. Sara sitting very still and straight with the autoharp on her lap, never smiling. Maybelle with the guitar. And A. P. wandering around, looking out the window, coming back in on the chorus. He said, Otis, it was like church, except you didn't have to dress up.

Now the fourth song. I'm Thinking Tonight of My Blue Eyes.

And here, friend, is where this story turns. Up to now, I have told you a happy story. Three people, a car ride, a room on State Street, and fame. This is where it goes wrong.

A. P. was gone a lot. Song hunting. Fretting. Restless. And Sara was at home in Poor Valley with the children. And the accounts say she was not happy. And somewhere in there, in the early nineteen thirties, Sara Carter fell in love with another man.

His name was Coy Bayes. And here is the hard part. He was A. P.'s cousin.

And in time, Sara left A. P. She took the children, and went back to live near her own people. And Coy Bayes's family, the story goes, did not like it one bit, and they moved away, all the way out to California, and took Coy with them. To get him away from her.

But here is the strange thing, friend. The Carter Family did not stop. Sara and A. P. were not living together anymore, but they kept on singing together. For years. Because it was their living. Because the records and the shows were the only money any of them had. So they would get up on a stage together, a man and the wife who had left him, and sing songs about true love and lonesome hearts and faithful sweethearts, and then go their separate ways after.

I cannot imagine, friend. I truly cannot.

And then came the radio.

At the end of the nineteen thirties, there were radio stations just across the border in Mexico, the border stations, they called them. Very powerful ones, much more powerful than the American government allowed stations in this country to be. And at night, their signals went out all over America, as far as Canada. And the Carter Family went down to Texas and sang on one of those stations, for a few years, the whole family, children and all.

And I will tell you who owned the biggest of those stations, friend, because you will not believe it. A man named John Brinkley, who called himself a doctor, and who had gotten rich in Kansas by claiming he could restore a man's youthful vigor by putting goat glands in him. I am not making that up. The state of Kansas had taken away his license, and the government had taken away his radio station, so he went across the river into Mexico and built the most powerful radio station in North America, and sold his cures over it. And to bring in listeners, he put on the best country music he could find.

So the Carter Family, singing hymns and the old songs, morally good, went out over the air every night from the station of the goat gland doctor. I heard that station myself, as a boy, late at night, on a radio with the window open. You never knew what you would hear. A preacher. A man selling baby chicks. A fellow selling some cure. And then, out of all that static, Sara Carter, clear as a bell, singing about the lonesome pine.

And the story that has been told, and I give it to you as it was told, is that one night on that border radio, Sara Carter sang a song called I'm Thinking Tonight of My Blue Eyes. And she sent it out over the air to Coy Bayes, out in California. And Coy Bayes heard it, wherever he was. And he came back for her.

Sara Carter and Coy Bayes were married in nineteen thirty-nine. The divorce from A. P. had come through not long before.

And the Carter Family, the original three, kept on recording a few more years. And then, in the early nineteen forties, it was over. Sara went to California with Coy. A. P. went home to Poor Valley. And Maybelle went on, with her daughters.

Now the fifth song. The one I promised you. Can the Circle Be Unbroken.

That song, friend, the Carters recorded in nineteen thirty-five. A. P. had found an old hymn, and reworked it, the way he did, and made it something new. It is a song about a funeral. About a mother who has died, and the family watching the hearse carry her away. And the singer asks, will the circle be unbroken, by and by. Will the family be together again, in the sky.

It became one of the most famous songs in this country, friend. Folks sing it at funerals still. They sang it at the end of every Grand Ole Opry show for years. And in nineteen seventy-two, a young rock and roll band from California got together with Maybelle Carter, by then an old woman, and a whole bunch of the old country musicians, and made a record album named after it. Will the Circle Be Unbroken. And it was a big hit, and young people all over the country who had never heard of the Carter Family found them through it.

And here is what happened to the circle.

Maybelle Carter went on with her three daughters, Helen, June, and Anita. Mother Maybelle and the Carter Sisters. They went to Nashville and played on the Grand Ole Opry. And a young guitar player named Chet Atkins played with them. And Maybelle's daughter June grew up and married a singer named Johnny Cash, in nineteen sixty-eight. June Carter could write a song too, friend. She helped write one of the biggest hits Johnny Cash ever had, a song about falling in love like falling into a ring of fire. The girl who grew up singing in the car with her mother and her aunt and her uncle, writing songs for the Man in Black. And Maybelle and her girls toured with Johnny Cash for years, and were on his television show. I watched that show, friend, on the little television on the shelf behind my counter. And when Mother Maybelle came out and sat down with her guitar or her autoharp, an old woman by then, in a plain dress, the whole store would go quiet. Grown men who had heard her on their grandmothers' phonographs, standing there with their groceries, watching her play Wildwood Flower on national television. One fellow said to me, Otis, she hasn't changed a bit. And she had changed a great deal, she was gray and heavy and slow. But I knew what he meant. The guitar had not changed at all. And in the nineteen sixties, when young people in the cities discovered folk music, they discovered Maybelle Carter too, and she played the big folk festivals, and the college kids sat at her feet. The girl from the backseat of that car in nineteen twenty-seven.

A. P. Carter went home to Poor Valley. He ran a little country store there, friend, the accounts say. Just like me. Sold groceries and odds and ends to the folks in the valley. And he lived alone, mostly. And the accounts say he never really got over Sara. He died in nineteen sixty, at home in Maces Spring.

And on his gravestone, his family put a gold record. And under it, the words, Keep on the Sunny Side.

Sara lived out in California with Coy Bayes for a long time. She did not sing much anymore. And when she died, in nineteen seventy-nine, they brought her home. Back to Poor Valley. And they buried her in the same churchyard as A. P. Carter.

And one of A. P. and Sara's daughters, Janette, started something in the nineteen seventies, right there in Maces Spring. A music hall, in an old building near the family place, where folks could come every Saturday night and hear the old music, the Carter music, played the old way. No electric instruments. Just folks playing and singing and dancing on the floor. They called it the Carter Family Fold. And folks come from all over to it.

Now I want to give you the other side, because I always try to.

There are people who will tell you the Carter Family story has been told too sweet. That A. P. got credit and copyrights for a great many old songs he did not write, that had belonged to the mountains for two hundred years, and that he and the record company made money on what was really everybody's. And there is truth in that. And that Lesley Riddle, who did so much of the work, got almost nothing. And that is true too.

But I will tell you what I think, friend. Those old songs were dying. The old women who knew them were dying, and their grandchildren were listening to the radio and did not want to learn the old ballads. And A. P. Carter walked up those hollows and wrote them down and got them on records before they were gone. If he had not, a great many of those songs would be in the ground with the old women who sang them. I do not care much whose name was on the copyright. I care that the songs lived.

Now I promised you the old stories, and there are a few.

Folks said Sara Carter never smiled on a stage. Not once. That she sat there like a statue and sang like an angel and never cracked a smile. I believe that. Every man I ever talked to who saw her said the same.

Folks said A. P. would wander off in the middle of a show, sometimes right out the door, and come back in time for the chorus. I believe that too.

And folks said that when A. P. was an old man, alone in his store in Poor Valley, he would sometimes play the old records, the ones with Sara singing, in the evenings. I cannot prove that one, friend. Nobody was there to see it. But I have thought about it more than once, standing behind my own counter in the evening, with the radio on.

So I promised you I would tell you what I believe Can the Circle Be Unbroken was really about.

Here is where I come down.

On the record, it is about a family at a funeral, wondering if they will all be together again in heaven. That is what the words say.

But I believe, friend, that when Sara and A. P. and Maybelle sang it, in nineteen thirty-five, with Sara and A. P. already broken apart and still standing side by side at the microphone, it was about them. Will the circle be unbroken. Three people who had come down a dirt road together in a borrowed car, with a baby and a guitar, and made something that would outlive all of them. And then came apart, the way families do.

And I will tell you how it came out.

The circle broke. It broke in their lifetime. A man and his wife came apart, and the band came apart, and they went to three different corners of the country.

But then Sara came home to be buried in Poor Valley, in the same ground as A. P. And Maybelle's girls kept singing the songs. And Janette opened the Fold. And every Saturday night in Maces Spring, people still come and sit on the benches and play the old songs. And somewhere in this country, tonight, a child is sitting with a guitar, learning Wildwood Flower, thumb on the low strings, fingers brushing the high ones, the way an eighteen-year-old girl played it in a borrowed car on the way to Bristol.

So the answer to the question, friend, the way I see it, is no, and yes. The circle broke. And the song did not.

That's as far as the story goes, friend, and about as far as I'll take you tonight.
