Madison County sits up in the far western corner of North Carolina, right on the Tennessee line. It is steep country, friend, about as steep as these mountains get. And up in the northern end of it, there is a valley along a stream called Shelton Laurel Creek. The Laurel country, folks call it.

And the valley was named for the people who lived in it. The Sheltons. They had been up there for generations. By the time of the war, a great many of the families in that valley were Sheltons, or married to Sheltons, or kin to them some way. Folks said you could not throw a rock in Shelton Laurel without hitting a Shelton, and if you missed, you hit his cousin.

They were small farmers. Corn and hogs and a little garden. Most of them did not own slaves. Most of them could not have afforded one, and a good many would not have wanted one. They had their land and their kin and their church, and they did not much care what happened down in Raleigh, and they cared even less about what happened down in Richmond.

Hold on to that, friend. It is the root of the whole thing.

When the war came, in eighteen sixty-one, North Carolina went with the Confederacy. But the mountains did not all go with it. A good deal of the mountain country was divided right down the middle. Some families sent their sons to fight for the South. Some families wanted nothing to do with it. And some, especially up along the Tennessee line, were for the Union outright. East Tennessee, just over the ridge, was full of Union men. And Shelton Laurel looked toward Tennessee a good deal more than it looked toward Raleigh.

And East Tennessee had already shown what that war would be like up here. In the fall of eighteen sixty-one, Union men over there had burned railroad bridges, hoping a Union army would come in behind them. The army did not come. So you had a valley full of people who did not want this war. And in the spring of eighteen sixty-two, the Confederate government passed a law that made the war want them.

It was called conscription. The draft. Every white man of fighting age, with some exceptions for those who could afford them, could be made to serve.

At first it was men from eighteen to thirty-five. By that fall, they had raised it to forty-five.
