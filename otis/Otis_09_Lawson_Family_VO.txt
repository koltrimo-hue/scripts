Ten days or so before Christmas, a tobacco farmer loaded his wife and his seven children into a car and drove them to the city.

He took them into a store and bought every one of them new clothes. A suit for the boys. Dresses for the girls. Shoes. Then he took them to a photographer's studio, which was not a thing a tobacco family did, not in nineteen twenty-nine, not with money as tight as money was that winter. And he stood them up in front of the camera in their new clothes, the mother holding the baby, the big girl beside her, the little ones down in front.

Nobody smiled. Folks did not smile for pictures much in those days.

On Christmas afternoon, he killed all of them but one. His wife. Six of his seven children. The youngest was four months old. Then he walked off into the woods behind the house and shot himself.

And that photograph is still around. You can look at it today. And for close to a hundred years, people have been staring at it, trying to see in the faces what was coming.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I will tell you how this one came to me, friend, because it did not come the way most of them did.

Most stories came across my counter in somebody's mouth. This one came across it on paper. Somewhere along the way, a copy of that photograph got passed around the hills, the way things did, and one day a man laid one down on my counter, next to his tobacco money. He did not say anything at first. He just turned it around so it faced me.

And then he said, do you know who that is?

I did not. And he told me. And I have never been able to look at an old family picture the same way since.

I heard about the Lawsons a good many times after that, over the years. Folks with kin over toward Stokes County, in North Carolina, up near the Virginia line. Folks who had heard the song. Folks who had been taken, as children, to see the house. And I noticed something about every one of those tellings. Everybody wanted to talk about the photograph. His name was Charles Davis Lawson, and everybody called him Charlie. In the winter of nineteen twenty-nine he was forty-three years old. His wife was Fannie, and she was thirty-seven.

They had seven children. I am going to give you every name and every age, because I believe they ought to be said, and because I am going to ask you to remember them.

Arthur was the oldest. Most accounts say he was sixteen that Christmas, and that fits with the year he died, which I will come to. Some accounts make him older, nineteen. I give you both.

Then Marie. She was seventeen.

Then Carrie, twelve.

Maybell, seven.

James, four.

Raymond, two.

And the baby, Mary Lou. Four months old.

Nine people, friend. Mother and father and seven children, in a farmhouse in a county where tobacco was about the only way a family could make a living.

For years the Lawsons had been tenant farmers. That means they worked somebody else's land and paid with a share of what they grew, and it is about as hard a way to live as there is. They moved around some, the way tenant families did, from one farm to another. But by about nineteen twenty-seven, the accounts say, they had saved enough to buy a place of their own. A farm near Germanton, in Stokes County.

I want you to stop on that, friend, because it matters. A tenant family buying its own land, in those years, was a family that had worked like dogs and done without for a long, long time. Charlie Lawson was not a drifter or a drunkard or a man nobody knew. He was a farmer who had climbed up out of tenancy by his own hands. He went to church. Folks said he was strict with his children, strict the way a lot of fathers were then, but nobody I ever heard tell it said he was a wild man.

Which is the first thing about this story that does not fit.

Now, there is one more thing about Charlie that I have to put in front of you early, because it comes back. Some months before that Christmas, the way it is usually told, Charlie Lawson got hurt. Hold on to that head injury, friend. A great many people have hung this whole story on it. And there is a doctor in Baltimore who has something to say about it, before we are done.

Now. The first thing left behind. The photograph.

About ten days before Christmas, Charlie Lawson drove the whole family into Winston-Salem. That is the city, the big tobacco town, a good long way from a farm out in Stokes County in those days. And he bought them new clothes. All of them. Every child.

You have to understand what that meant in nineteen twenty-nine. That fall, in October, the stock market had crashed. The papers were full of it. Tobacco prices were bad and getting worse. A farm family in those hills got new clothes about as often as it got a new roof, which is to say, when the old one could not be patched anymore. And here was a man who had just bought his own land, with every reason in the world to hold on to every dollar, walking his family into a store in the city and buying the lot of them new.

And then he took them to a photographer's studio and had their picture made.

I have looked at that picture many a time, friend. Arthur, the oldest boy, standing tall in the back. Marie beside him, a grown young woman, in her new dress. Charlie, with his arms down at his sides. Fannie, holding Mary Lou. Carrie, twelve, standing straight. And down front, the little ones. Maybell, James, Raymond. Raymond is so small he looks like he does not know what is happening, and I expect he did not.

Now, when the man laid that photograph on my counter, I said what I expect you are thinking. I said, why. Why would a man do that, ten days before he killed them?

And there are two answers that people give, and they are two very different men.

The first answer is that Charlie Lawson already knew. That he had made up his mind, and he wanted one last picture of them all, dressed fine, the way he wanted them remembered. That the new clothes were burial clothes, and he was the only one who knew it.

The second answer is that he did not know yet. That he was a man trying, in the only way he had, to make one good Christmas. New clothes. A real picture. A family that looked like it had come up in the world. And that whatever broke in him, broke later.

I will tell you which of those I lean toward at the end. But I want you to look at that picture with me in your mind for a while yet. Because it is the last time that whole family was ever in one place, alive, and every one of them is looking straight at you.

Now let me take you to Christmas Day.

It was the twenty-fifth of December, nineteen twenty-nine, a Wednesday. And on a farm like that, Christmas was not like a Christmas you might know. There was no great pile of presents. There was a big dinner, if a family could manage one, and visiting, and the men might go out rabbit hunting in the afternoon. That was a common thing in those parts on Christmas Day. Rabbit hunting.

That morning, Charlie sent Arthur, the oldest boy, on an errand. He was to walk into Walnut Cove, the nearest town, with a friend of his, and buy shotgun shells. Arthur thought, the way it has come down, that he and his daddy were going rabbit hunting that afternoon.

So Arthur walked off down the road. And that errand is the only reason Arthur Lawson lived.

I want you to hold that in your mind, friend, because it is one of the hardest things in this whole story. Some folks have said that Charlie sent Arthur away on purpose. That the boy was nearly grown, big enough to stop him, and Charlie knew it. Others have said the boy was spared by nothing but chance. Nobody can tell you which. But imagine being that boy, walking home from Walnut Cove with a box of shells in your pocket, and spending the rest of your life wondering whether your father sent you away to save you, or to get you out of the way.

Now the cake.

Marie Lawson was seventeen, and she was in the kitchen that day, baking. A Christmas cake. Most of the accounts say it was a cake with raisins on it. I have heard it called other things too, and I will not argue over the recipe. What matters is that a seventeen-year-old girl spent part of her Christmas morning baking a cake for her family's dinner, and that the cake outlived her by many years.

Hold on to the cake, friend. It is coming back, and when it does, it is going to tell you something about the rest of us.

Some time in the afternoon, the two middle girls, Carrie and Maybell, set out from the house. They were going to visit their aunt and uncle, Charlie's brother's family, who lived not far off. Carrie was twelve. Maybell was seven. Two little girls walking down the farm road on Christmas Day to see their kin.

And I want you to think, just for a second, about that aunt and uncle. Somewhere down the road, in another farmhouse, there were people expecting two little girls for a Christmas visit. Maybe a plate set out. Maybe a little something saved back for them. And the afternoon went on, and the girls did not come, and nobody in that house had any reason yet to think it was anything but a late start.

Charlie was waiting for them by the tobacco barn, with a shotgun.

I will not give you more of that than the records give. The accounts say he shot them both, and then he struck them, to be certain. And then he did a thing that every account agrees on, and that I have never been able to understand, and I do not believe anybody ever will. He laid them out. He put them on their backs. He crossed their arms over their chests, the way you would lay out the dead for a funeral. And because there were no pillows in a tobacco barn, the accounts say he put stones under their heads.

Stones under their heads, friend. Like a pillow.

Then he went to the house.

Fannie was on the porch. He shot her there.

Marie was inside. The accounts say she screamed, and that the little boys, James and Raymond, ran and tried to hide. Marie was killed in the house. And then he found the little boys, four years old and two years old, where they had hidden.

And the baby. Mary Lou. Four months old. I will say it once and not again. He killed the baby too.

And then, friend, he did the same thing in the house that he had done in the barn. He laid them out. Arms crossed. Pillows under their heads. Every one.

Seven people, laid out like a funeral that had not happened yet. And their father walked out of that house and into the woods.

Now, I have heard folks say that the laying out proves something. Some say it shows he loved them, that a man who crosses his children's arms and puts a pillow under their heads is a man grieving even while he does it. Others say it shows the opposite, that it was cold, that it was a man tidying up.

I will tell you what I know about it, which is only this. In these mountains, in those years, when somebody died at home, the family laid them out. Washed them, dressed them, crossed their hands, put pennies on their eyes, and sat up with them through the night. Every grown person in Stokes County had seen it done. Every one of them had done it. Charlie Lawson had surely done it himself, for a parent or a neighbor.

So when he crossed those arms, friend, he was not inventing anything. He was doing what a mountain family does for its dead. The only thing wrong with it was that he was the one who had made them dead. And I believe that is the most frightening thing in this whole story. Not that he did something no man had ever done. That he did the ordinary thing, the decent thing, the thing his own mother taught him, right on top of the worst thing.

Now I want to stop here, and I want to tell you why I am telling it this way. You will find this story told in a good many places with a good deal more blood in it than I have given you. People like to linger on it. I am not going to. Because the thing about the Lawsons that ought to stop you cold is not how they died. It is that every one of them, that morning, had a reason to believe it was a good day. New clothes. A cake. A trip to see kin. A rabbit hunt. And the man who ended it was the same man who had bought the clothes.

Neighbors heard the shots. Christmas in farm country, a shot or two does not mean much, a man after a rabbit. But not like that. Not that many. Folks came. Kin came. And somewhere in that afternoon, Arthur came home from Walnut Cove.

I have never found an account I trust of exactly what Arthur saw first, or who found him, or who told him. I will not make it up.

They went looking for Charlie.

They found him in the woods, not far from the house. He had shot himself. I give you that path as it was given to me. I cannot prove it. Everything else he did that day, he did like a man doing chores.

That is the third thing left behind. And I believe it is the one that matters most. Because it tells you he knew there was an explanation. He started to write it down. And he could not, or would not, finish.

Now, what happened next is where this story stops being only about the Lawsons, and starts being about everybody else.

The funeral was held two days after Christmas, on the twenty-seventh of December. The family was buried together in the cemetery in that community, the Browder family cemetery, in a common grave. And the accounts say something like five thousand people came.

Five thousand, friend. To a farm funeral, out in the country, in the dead of winter, in a county where most folks did not own a car. They came from all over. Most of them had never laid eyes on a Lawson in their lives. They came because they had read about it in the papers, and they wanted to see.

I have heard men at my counter talk about that crowd with something like pride, as if it showed how much folks cared. And then it got worse. Because then there was the house.

After the funeral, the farmhouse where it happened was opened up to the public. There it is, friend. The cake comes back.

Marie's Christmas cake was still sitting there. And the people walking through that house picked at it. They pulled the raisins off the top and took them home, for keepsakes. Souvenirs. I have thought about that cake under glass more than almost anything else in this story. Because a man doing something terrible, out of sickness or shame or whatever was in him, that is a thing I can almost put in a box and say, well, that was one man. That is the rest of us.

I asked an old fellow about it once, a man who had been taken through that house as a little boy. I asked him what he remembered. He said the cake. Now, I want to be careful here, friend, the way I always try to be. That was nineteen twenty-nine. So I do not take that to mean there was nothing wrong with Charlie Lawson. But I do take it to mean this. The easy answer, the one everybody wanted, the one that would have let the whole county sleep at night, a hurt head that made a good man do a terrible thing, that answer was looked for. And here, friend, is where I have to turn this whole story around. Because up to now, I have told it the way it was told for sixty years. And the way it was told for sixty years was wrong about one thing. Not wrong about what happened. Wrong about who it happened to.

So for sixty years, that was where the story sat. A good farmer. A bump on the head, maybe. A photograph. A cake. Two broken sentences. And no reason.

And then, in nineteen ninety, a book came out.

It was written by a man named Bruce Jones and his daughter, Trudy Smith. They called it White Christmas, Bloody Christmas. And in it, for the first time in print, came the thing that some folks in Stokes County had been saying quiet for sixty years.

I am going to give this to you carefully, friend, because it is an accusation against a dead man and about a dead girl, and neither of them can answer it.

The book brought forward the words of people who said they knew something. One was a woman who had been a friend of Marie's. The other was a woman from the Lawson family, named Stella Lawson Boles, who said that at the funeral, as a girl, she overheard the women of the family talking. If she found out what that book says she found out, she found it out in December, with Christmas coming. And the accounts say she told the other women of the family. Which means, friend, that she was asking for help, the only way a woman in her place could ask. And I have never heard that anybody gave it. I do not say that to blame them. I do not know what anybody could have done, in nineteen twenty-nine, in a farmhouse in Stokes County. I only say that she told somebody. And then she went and stood in a photographer's studio, holding the baby, next to him.

It is not proved. It cannot be proved. Marie was never examined for it, not that anybody wrote down. The people who spoke were remembering things from sixty years before, from when they were young. Memories move, over sixty years. They get told and retold. And there are folks in Stokes County who resent the whole idea, who say it is a stain put on a family that cannot defend itself.

But I will also tell you this, because I promised you I would come back to those two broken sentences.

Nobody to blame but.

Troubles can cause.

If you are a man who has done the thing that book says, and your wife has found out, and your daughter is carrying a child that will tell the whole county what you are, then you are a man with a secret that is about to stop being a secret. I am not telling you that is what happened. I am telling you that it is the only explanation I have ever heard that makes those two sentences make sense.

Now let me give you the other side, as fair as I can, because I always try to.

I respect that. I do. But I will tell you what I always come back to.
