For almost thirty years, every single day, a widow walked a mile from her house to the mouth of a coal mine in West Virginia.

She was a small woman, an Italian, who had come across the ocean with her husband to a country where she barely spoke the language. And every day, she carried an empty bucket to the mine. And at the mine, she filled the bucket with coal. And she carried it home, a mile, and emptied it in her yard. And the next day, she went back.

Her husband had gone down into that mine one morning in December, nineteen hundred and seven. And he never came up. They never found him.

And over the years, the story goes, the coal she carried home piled up in her yard until it was a hill. A black hill, higher than her house. Folks said she was trying to dig him out, one bucket at a time.

Her husband was one of hundreds of men who died in that mine on the same morning. It was the worst mine disaster in the history of the United States. And most Americans have never heard its name.

Monongah.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I told you about Monongah once before, friend, in passing. When I told you about the Farmington mine, Number Nine, in nineteen sixty-eight, I said that just a few miles away, sixty-one years before, there had been something worse. And I said I would come back to it someday.

So here I am.

And I will tell you why I waited. Because Monongah is a hard one to tell. Not because of the numbers, though the numbers are terrible. Because of the names. Most of the men who died in that mine came from the other side of the world. Italy. Hungary. Poland. Russia. Greece. They had names that the coal company clerks could not spell and did not try to. Some of them were never written down at all. And when the bodies came up, there were a good many that nobody in this whole country could name, because the only people who could have named them were dead in the same mine, or back home across the ocean, waiting on a letter.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to tell you about Monongah from the bottom of the ocean voyage to the bottom of the mine. Who those men were, and why they came, and what happened to them on that one morning. And who was to blame.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you the true number of men who died at Monongah. Not the official number. The true one. And I am going to tell you why nobody will ever know it. Because I believe that is the most important thing about the whole story.

Let me start across the ocean.

In the years around nineteen hundred, friend, America needed men. Strong men, who would do hard, dangerous, dirty work for low pay. To build the railroads and the steel mills, and most of all, to dig the coal that ran all of it. And there were not enough Americans willing to do it at the pay the companies wanted to give.

So the companies looked across the ocean. And across the ocean, in the poor villages of southern Italy, and in the farm country of Hungary and Poland and the Austrian empire, there were millions of young men with no land and no work and no future, who had heard stories about America. That in America, a man could make in a day what he made in a month back home. That in America, a man could save up and send money home, and build his family a house, and maybe someday bring them over.

So they came. By the boatload. And a good many of them came because a man had come to their village first. In those years, there were labor agents, the Italians called them padrones, who worked for the coal companies and the railroads and went back to the old country, or met the boats in New York, and signed up young men to come and work. The padrone would arrange the ticket, the train, the job. And then he would take a cut of the man's pay for a long time after, for his trouble. Some of them were honest men helping their countrymen. Some of them were not. But either way, a boy from a mountain village in Calabria might find himself, a month after leaving home, at the bottom of a coal mine in West Virginia, without ever having decided much of anything himself. Through Ellis Island in New York harbor, with a tag pinned to their coats and maybe a few words of English. Ellis Island was where the government looked them over, friend. A man came off the boat after two weeks in the bottom of a ship, and walked up a long staircase into a great hall, and doctors watched him climb, to see if he was short of breath or lame. And at the top, they looked in his eyes and his mouth and listened to his chest, quick, one after another, thousands a day. And if a doctor saw something he did not like, he made a mark in chalk on the man's coat. An L for lame. An H for heart. An E for the eyes. And a man with a chalk mark could be sent back across the ocean. So those young men who came to Monongah, friend, had already been looked over and passed as strong and sound. They were the healthiest men their villages had. And a good many of them got on trains, with tickets the coal companies had arranged, and rode west and south into the mountains of West Virginia and Pennsylvania. Into coal country.

Hold on to those tags, friend. Pinned on their coats with their names on them, at Ellis Island. Because in a few years, at Monongah, a good many of those names were going to be lost for good.

Now the town.

Monongah is a little town in Marion County, in northern West Virginia, on the West Fork River, not far from the city of Fairmont. And around nineteen hundred and seven, it was a busy, crowded coal town. The coal company that owned the mines there was called the Fairmont Coal Company, a big, rich outfit, owned by some of the most powerful men in the state. The accounts say one of the men at the head of that company went on, a few years later, to sit in the United States Senate. That is how big those men were, friend. The men who owned the mines and the men who made the laws were very often the same men, or sat at the same dinner tables. And it had two big mines at Monongah, called Number Six and Number Eight. They were connected to each other underground. Miles of tunnels, running back under those hills.

And the men who worked them, a great many of them, were the immigrants. The accounts say that among the men in those mines were Italians, a great many of them, and Hungarians, and Slovaks, and Poles, and Russians, and Greeks, and Turks, and Americans, white and Black. A whole world in one hole in the ground. Men who could not talk to each other, working side by side in the dark.

And they lived in the company houses, in little neighborhoods, the Italians together and the Hungarians together, the way people do in a strange country. They had their churches and their saints' days and their own food and their own songs. Some had brought their wives and children over. A great many had not yet. A great many were young men, alone, saving every penny to send home to a mother or a wife in a village in Calabria or Galicia that they might never see again.

And here is something that matters, friend. In those days, a miner was paid by the ton of coal he loaded. And a good many miners brought along a helper. A son. A nephew. A boy from the old village who had just come over. To help load the coal, so the miner could load more tons and make more money. And those helpers were not on the company's books. The company did not pay them. The company did not write down their names. They just went down into the mine with the man who brought them.

Hold on to that, friend. Boys and young men, going down into that mine every morning, whose names were not written on any list. And some of them, the accounts say, were not young men at all. Some were boys. Twelve, ten, and by some accounts younger still. In those years, before the child labor laws had any teeth in the coal fields, a boy of ten going into the mine with his father was nothing anybody remarked on.

Now the morning.

It was Friday, the sixth of December, nineteen hundred and seven.

And here is a thing that the folks of Monongah have always told, and I give it to you as they told it. The sixth of December is the feast day of Saint Nicholas. And for a great many of the Catholic men from the old countries, that was a holy day, and a day of celebration. And the story is that a number of the men did not go to work that morning, because of the saint's day. That they stayed home, or went to church, or went to the saloon. And that those men lived because of it.

I cannot tell you how many. Nobody can. But I have heard it told by the grandchildren of men who were supposed to go down that morning and did not. And I believe it.

The men who did go down went in early, before daylight, the way miners did. And they worked through the morning. And I want you to understand something about how they saw down there, friend. In nineteen hundred and seven, a good many miners in this country still wore a little lamp on the front of their cap with an open flame. A wick, burning oil. A little fire on every man's head, in a place full of gas and dust. The safety lamps and the electric cap lamps that came later were not yet the rule. The companies and the men both knew there was gas in those mines. And they went down every morning with fire on their foreheads anyway, because that was how it was done.

And at about twenty-eight minutes past ten in the morning, Number Six and Number Eight exploded.

The accounts say there was one explosion, and then another, bigger, following it through the tunnels. And the force of it was so great that it blew out of the mouths of the mines like a cannon. It knocked down buildings on the surface. It threw heavy equipment around like it was nothing. Folks in the town said the ground shook like an earthquake, and that windows broke all over Monongah. The entrance to one of the mines was destroyed. The great fan that pushed fresh air down into the mine was wrecked.

And down below, in the dark, the mine filled up with poison gas. The miners called it afterdamp. The air that is left after an explosion, with no life in it. A man who was not killed by the blast itself had only a short time before the afterdamp got him. In some mines, friend, the miners carried canaries in little cages, because a canary would feel the bad air before a man did, and fall off its perch, and give the men a warning. That was how it was in a great many places in those days. A little yellow bird was the best warning system a coal miner had.

Now, friend, what set it off?

The investigators, afterward, could not say for certain. But the explanation most of them came to, the accounts say, was this. That a string of loaded coal cars, being pulled up out of the mine on a cable, broke loose. Came uncoupled. And went rolling back down the slope into the mine, faster and faster, and crashed. And that the crash tore down electric wires, or stirred up a great cloud of coal dust, or both. And a spark, from the wires or from somewhere, set off the gas and the dust.

And the coal dust was the killer, friend. A coal mine in those days was full of it. Fine black dust on every surface, every timber, every floor. And coal dust, stirred up into the air, will burn like gunpowder. One explosion picks up the dust ahead of it and turns it into the next explosion, and the next, racing through the tunnels faster than any man could run.

There was nowhere to run. The men in those mines did not have a chance.

Now the surface.

Within minutes, the whole town was at the mine. Wives. Mothers. Children. Men from the other mines nearby, who dropped their tools and came running. And they stood at the mouth of the mine, and the smoke was coming out, and the gas, and there was nothing anybody could do.

The rescuers went in as soon as they could get in, which was not soon. The air was poison. The tunnels were caved in. Men from other mines, brave men, volunteers, crawled into that mine with what little equipment they had in those days, and some of them were overcome by the gas themselves and had to be dragged back out.

And they began bringing up the dead. And I want you to know what those rescuers had to work with, friend, because it was almost nothing. There were no breathing masks to speak of in those days, not the kind a rescue team carries now. There were no trained rescue teams at all, hardly, not in this country. There were brave men with lanterns and wet rags over their faces, going into a mine full of poison air to find men they had worked beside the week before. Some of them, the accounts say, had to be carried back out themselves, overcome. And they went back in.

I am going to tell you this plainly, friend, and not dwell on it. The bodies that came up out of Monongah were, a great many of them, in terrible shape. Burned. Broken. Some so torn by the blast that nobody could tell who they had been. And they were brought up and laid out, in rows, in the buildings near the mine, and the families came and walked down the rows, looking.

And a great many families could not find their own. And a great many bodies were never claimed. Because the man's family was in Italy, or in Hungary, and did not even know yet that he was dead.

There is a story, and I give it to you as it has come down, that the company and the town ran out of coffins. That they had to send away for them, by the trainload. And that for days, the streets of Monongah were lined with coffins.

And they dug graves. On a hill above the town. Row on row. Some single. Some, the accounts say, so many in one place that they were more like trenches.

And out of all of that, friend, how many men came out of Number Six and Number Eight alive?

A handful. The accounts give different numbers, but it was only a very few. One of them, a Polish miner, the story goes, was found alive deep in the mine and brought out, and lived. Others were near the entrances and made it out. That was all.

Now the number.

When it was all over, the official count of the dead at Monongah was three hundred and sixty-two. That is the number the company and the state settled on. Three hundred and sixty-two men. That is the number you will find in the history books, the number on the markers. And by that number, Monongah is still the worst mine disaster in the history of this country.

But I promised you the true number, friend. And here is the trouble.

Remember the helpers. The boys and young men who went down with the miners, to help them load, who were not on the company's books. Nobody counted them going in. And when the mine blew up, nobody could count them coming out, because they were not on any list to check against. Remember the men whose names had been written wrong, or not at all. Remember the bodies that nobody could put a name to.

The folks who have studied Monongah, the historians and the families and the people in Italy who have looked into it, believe the true number of dead was a good deal higher than three hundred and sixty-two. Some say four hundred. Some say five hundred. Some say more. Because if you have a mine with men going down in it every morning whose names were never written down, then when that mine blows up, you will never know how many died. All you can know is how many you counted.

Three hundred and sixty-two, friend, is the number of men somebody bothered to count.

And here, friend, is where this story turns. Because up to now, I have told you what happened. Now I have to tell you what was done about it. And that is a shorter story than it ought to be.

Now, who was to blame?

There were investigations. The state looked into it. And the accounts say that the coal company, in the end, was not held responsible. The explosion was ruled an accident. Nobody went to jail. Nobody paid a fine that I have ever been able to find. And the families, a great many of them foreigners who did not speak English and had no lawyers and no money and no say in that county, got very little. The accounts say the company and others put together a relief fund, and some money came from all over, including from Italy, and was divided among the widows and children. But it was not much, spread over that many families. A few hundred dollars, here and there.

And the accounts say that Monongah left something like two hundred and fifty widows, and something like a thousand children without fathers.

A thousand children, friend. In one little town. Nineteen days before Christmas. I have thought about that Christmas in Monongah more than once. A town where nearly every street had a house with black crepe on the door. Churches holding funerals all through Advent. Children who had been promised a Christmas, with their fathers in the ground on the hill. I have read that folks from all over the country sent money and gifts that month, when the newspapers carried the story. I hope some of it reached those children. I expect it was not enough.

Now let me tell you about the rest of that December, because it was not over.

Monongah was on the sixth. Less than two weeks later, on the nineteenth of December, a mine over in Pennsylvania, called the Darr mine, exploded and killed more than two hundred men. And there were other mine disasters that same month, in Alabama and elsewhere. So many men died in the coal mines of this country in December of nineteen hundred and seven that folks came to call it Black December. And the accounts say that more miners died on the job in this country in nineteen hundred and seven than in any other year before or since. Thousands of them.

And the country, friend, for once, sat up and paid attention.

There was a great outcry in the newspapers. Congress started asking questions. And in nineteen ten, partly because of Monongah and Black December, the United States government created the Bureau of Mines. A government office whose job was to study mine explosions and how to stop them. To figure out the dust and the gas and the ventilation. To train rescue teams.

It did not have much power, at first. It was mostly there to study, not to make anybody do anything. You may remember I told you that, friend, in the Farmington story, that the study was still going on sixty-one years later. But it was a start. It was the first time the government of this country said, out loud, that men dying in the mines was its business.

Now I want to give you the other side, because I always try to.

There are folks who will tell you that the Fairmont Coal Company was no worse than any other coal company of its day. That in nineteen hundred and seven, nobody understood coal dust the way we do now. That the mines at Monongah were considered modern and well run for their time. That the explosion was a terrible accident that could have happened in any mine in the country, and in fact did happen in a great many of them that same month. And that it is not fair to judge men in nineteen hundred and seven by what we know today.

And there is truth in that, friend. I will not pretend there is not. The science was not there. The rules were not there. The whole country was digging coal as fast as it could, with men's lives as cheap as the coal.

But I will tell you what I always come back to. Somebody decided that the men loading the coal could bring helpers down into the mine without anybody writing their names down. Somebody decided that was all right. That it was cheaper. And that decision, friend, is why we will never know how many people died at Monongah. That was not the science. That was the bookkeeping. And bookkeeping is a thing people choose.

Now I promised you the old stories, and there are some.

There is the story of the widow with the bucket, which I gave you at the start. Her name was Caterina Davia. Her husband, Vittorio, the accounts say, was one of the men never found. And the story is that for nearly thirty years she walked to the mine every day and carried home a bucket of coal, and that the pile in her yard grew until it covered her little house. Folks called her the Monongah heroine. I have heard it said that she believed, in her grief, that if she took away enough coal, she would find him. I do not know what was in her heart. Nobody does. But I know a woman who carries a bucket a mile every day for thirty years is not crazy, friend. She is faithful.

Folks said that the men who stayed home for Saint Nicholas's Day never forgot it. That every year after, on the sixth of December, they went to church and lit a candle for the men who went down that morning. And their children did, and their grandchildren. I believe that one.

And folks said, and this is the one that has stuck with me the longest, that in the villages back in Italy, there were women who waited for years for letters from Monongah that never came. Who did not know for a long time that their husbands or sons were dead. Who kept waiting for the money order, the letter with the stamp from America, the word that he had saved enough and was sending for them. And it never came. And nobody from the company ever wrote to tell them why.

There were whole villages, friend, in the mountains of southern Italy, in places like Calabria and Molise, that lost a great many of their young men at Monongah, all on the same morning. Villages where nearly every family was waiting on somebody in West Virginia. And when the news finally came, it came to all of them at once. I have read that in one of those villages, they still remember Monongah the way folks here remember a flood or a war. A word every child there knows, for a place on the other side of the world that took their great-grandfathers.

There was a man who traded at my store for years, friend, an Italian fellow whose grandfather had come over to dig coal in those years, though not at Monongah. And when this story came up, he told me something his grandfather used to say. He said the old man told him that when he first came over, the bosses did not call the men by their names. They gave each man a number. A check number, that they hung on the board, and that was how the company knew who was in the mine. And the old man told his grandson, Otis, I was a number before I was ever a name in this country.

And he said, the ones who did not have a number at all, the helpers, those were the ones who disappeared.

I have thought about those women, friend, more than once, standing at my own post office window, handing letters across the counter. Thinking about the letters that never went.

So I promised you I would tell you the true number of men who died at Monongah.

Here is where I come down.

I do not know it. Nobody knows it. Nobody ever will. And that is the true answer, and I believe it is the most important thing in this whole story.

Because think about what it means, friend. In the worst mine disaster in the history of this country, we do not know how many people died. Not because the records burned. Not because it was so long ago. Because nobody ever wrote down the names of the men and boys who went down into that mine every morning. They were not worth writing down. They were helpers. They were foreigners. They were boys from villages nobody here could find on a map. They loaded coal that heated houses and ran railroads and made steel all over America, and when they died, the company did not know their names, because the company had never asked.

Three hundred and sixty-two is the number somebody counted.

The true number is somewhere out in the dark, in the old mine under those hills, and in the villages across the ocean where women waited for letters.

And Caterina Davia carried coal for thirty years, trying to find one of them.
