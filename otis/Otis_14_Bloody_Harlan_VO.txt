The men with the guns had already been through the house.

They had come in without knocking, looking for her husband. They went through the rooms. They looked under the beds and in the closets. They went through the family's things. They were deputies of the county sheriff, wearing badges, carrying rifles, and the coal companies paid their wages. And her husband was not there. He was hiding out in the hills, because he was a union man, and in that county, that year, a union man who got caught might not come home.

So when they were gone, she was alone in the house with her children. She was angry, and she was afraid, and she wanted to say something. And she looked around that house for a piece of paper to write it down on.

There was not one. Not a scrap. They were that poor.

So she took down the calendar off the wall. And on the back of it, she wrote a song.

And that song went out of a little house in Harlan County, Kentucky, and over the mountains, and up to New York, and onto records and picket lines and marches all over this country, for the rest of the century. And at the heart of it was one question. A question she meant for every single person in that county. Including folks a lot like me.

Which side are you on?

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

The first time I heard that song, friend, it was being sung in my own store.

It was during a strike. Not a big one, not a famous one. Just some of the men from a mine near me who had walked out over something or other, and were standing around my stove on a cold afternoon with nothing to do but talk. And one of them, an older fellow, started singing it, low, and the others picked it up. Which side are you on. Over and over.

And when they got to that question, every one of those men turned and looked at me.

They did not mean anything by it. Or maybe they did. But I will tell you, I stood behind that counter with a can of coffee in my hand and felt my face go hot. Because I was a storekeeper. And a storekeeper always wants to be on everybody's side. The miners bought from me. The foremen bought from me. The men who owned the mine sent their wives in to buy from me. I did not want to be on any side.

And that song, friend, does not let you do that.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to tell you about Harlan County in the nineteen thirties, the years they came to call Bloody Harlan. And I am going to tell it by that question. I am going to ask it of every person in this story. The miners. The deputies. The sheriff. The writers who came down from New York. The woman with the calendar.

And at the very end, I am going to answer it myself. Which side I would have been on, if I had kept a store in Harlan County in nineteen thirty-one. I have thought about it a long time, and I am not proud of every part of my answer. But I promised myself I would give it.

Let me start with the place.

Harlan County sits down in the southeastern corner of Kentucky, right up against Virginia. It is about as rough and steep as these mountains get. The highest point in the whole state of Kentucky is there, Black Mountain. And under all that ground, friend, there was coal. A great deal of it, and good coal.

For a long time, nobody could get it out. The county was too far back, too steep. And then, in the years just before the First World War, the railroad came in. And when the railroad came, the coal companies came right behind it. They bought up the land and the mineral rights. They opened mines up every hollow. They built coal camps, rows and rows of little company houses. And men came from everywhere to dig. From the farms all over these mountains. From the Deep South, a great many Black men looking for a wage. From across the ocean.

Some of those camps were not small, friend. Up near Black Mountain, a great steel company built a coal town called Lynch, and it grew into one of the biggest coal camps anywhere in the world, with thousands of people, its own hospital, its own power plant, its own everything. Folks there spoke a dozen languages. Right next to it, a farm machinery company built another camp called Benham. Those were company towns in the fullest sense. The company did not just own the mine. It owned the ground you walked on to get to it.

And I will tell you, some of the men who lived in the big camps said they were not bad places, in the good years. Clean houses, a doctor, a school. A paycheck. Better than a hardscrabble farm on a hillside, which is where a good many of them had come from. I give you that, because it is true, and because it matters for what comes next. It is one thing to live in a company town when the company is doing well. It is a different thing entirely when it is not.

And for a while, it was a boom. During the First World War and after, the country needed coal like it needed air, and Harlan County dug it.

But you have to understand what a coal camp was, friend, and I have told you this before in another story, so I will tell it short. The company owned the house. The company owned the store. The company paid the doctor and the preacher. In a good many camps, the company paid the men in scrip, little tokens good only at the company store. And when a man owed the company store more than he made, which a good many men did, he could not leave. He was bound to that camp the way a man is bound to a debt.

Now, the boom ended. By the end of the nineteen twenties, there were too many mines and too much coal, and prices fell. And then, in nineteen twenty-nine, the bottom fell out of the whole country. The Depression came down on Harlan County like a rockslide.

The mines cut back. Men worked two days a week, or one, or none. And in February of nineteen thirty-one, the coal operators of Harlan County cut the miners' wages by another ten percent.

Ten percent off of almost nothing, friend. Let me tell you what almost nothing meant. A miner in those days was mostly not paid by the hour. He was paid by the ton. He went down into the mine before daylight and loaded coal by hand, with a shovel, into a car, all day long, bent over in a seam that might not be high enough to stand up in. And at the end, the car went to the company scale, and the company told him what it weighed, and he was paid on that. A good many men believed the company scale was crooked, and a good many of them had reason to. And out of what he was paid, the company took the rent, and the doctor, and the store bill, and the price of the powder he used to blast the coal loose, and the lamp oil to see by. There were weeks a man worked every day and came out owing. Families in those camps were already hungry. Children were already sick. And now there was less.

So the miners did the only thing they had left. They started joining the union. The United Mine Workers came in, and men started signing up, by the hundreds and then by the thousands.

And the coal companies did what they always did. They fired the men who joined. They put them on a blacklist, so no other mine would hire them. And they evicted them. Threw them and their families out of the company houses and into the road.

So now I will ask my first question. The miners. Which side were they on?

That one is easy, friend. They were on the side of eating. They were on the side of their children. Most of them were not radicals and did not want to be. They wanted a wage that would feed a family and a scale that weighed their coal honest. And they found out that in Harlan County, wanting those things put you on a side whether you liked it or not.

Now the second question. The law. Which side was the law on?

The high sheriff of Harlan County in nineteen thirty-one was a man named John Henry Blair. And under Sheriff Blair, a great many of the deputies in that county were not ordinary deputies. They were mine guards. Men hired by the coal companies, paid by the coal companies, and then sworn in by the sheriff and handed a badge. So when a deputy came to throw a family out of a company house, or to break up a union meeting, or to search a man's home for union papers, he was wearing the badge of the county and drawing his wages from the coal operators.

The miners had a name for those men. They called them gun thugs.

Now, friend, I want to be fair, the way I always try to be. There were sheriffs and deputies in those mountains who were honest men trying to keep order in a county that was coming apart, with hungry men carrying rifles. And there were union men who did violent things too. Mines got dynamited. Men got shot from the brush on both sides. I will not stand here and tell you that every deputy was a devil and every miner a saint.

But I will tell you this. When the law in a county is paid by one side, it is not the law anymore. It is one side with a badge. And everybody in Harlan County knew it.

And I want to give you one more thing, the side of the men who owned the mines, because I promised I would ask the question of everybody. The coal operators would have told you they were fighting for their lives too. Coal prices had fallen through the floor. There were mines in other states that paid less and sold cheaper. A Harlan operator who signed a union contract and paid union wages, the way they saw it, would be undersold and put out of business inside a year, and then nobody in his camp would have any job at all. I heard a version of that argument at my counter more than once, from men who owned a small mine or two. And I will not pretend there was nothing to it. Times were desperate for everybody.

But I will tell you what the miners said back, and I never heard a mine owner answer it. They said, if your business can only survive by paying a man less than it takes to feed his children, and shooting him when he complains, then maybe your business ought not to survive.

And in the spring of nineteen thirty-one, it came to shooting.

There was a little town on the Cumberland River in Harlan County called Evarts. And Evarts was different from the coal camps, because the coal companies did not own it. It was an independent town. So it was one of the few places where miners who had been thrown out of their company houses could go. A good many of them ended up there, with nowhere else to go, angry and hungry.

On the fifth of May, nineteen thirty-one, the accounts say, a convoy of deputies came along the road near Evarts, in cars, escorting a strikebreaker and his belongings. And the striking miners were waiting for them along that road, with rifles.

Nobody can tell you for certain who fired first, friend. I have told you that about more than one gunfight in these mountains, and I will tell you again. But the shooting started, and it went on, the accounts say, for something like fifteen minutes. And when it stopped, there were dead men on the road.

The accounts generally count three of the deputies dead, and one of the miners. Among the deputies was a man named Jim Daniels, who the miners feared and hated about as much as any man in that county. The accounts say he went down early, shot in the head.

Some accounts put the number of dead higher. I will give you the count most of them agree on.

They called it the Battle of Evarts. And after it, friend, the whole thing got worse. The governor sent in the National Guard. Dozens of miners were charged with murder. Some of them went to prison for a very long time. And the coal operators had exactly what they needed. Proof, they said, that the union was a pack of killers.

The trials that came after were held in courtrooms where the coal operators had a great deal of say, and the accounts say the juries were picked accordingly. Several of the union men were sent away for life. Among them, the way it has come down, was the president of the local union at Evarts, an old man, who folks said had always argued against violence. Life in prison. Years later, some of those men were let out, and some were pardoned. But by then, friend, the strike was long over, and they had given the best of their lives to a penitentiary for a fifteen-minute gunfight that nobody could ever prove who started.

And that summer, the accounts say, somebody dynamited one of the soup kitchens where the strikers' families were being fed. I will let you guess which side folks at the time believed did that.

The United Mine Workers, battered and broke, pulled back out of Harlan County that summer. And the men who stayed on strike were left with almost nothing. Soup kitchens. Families living in tents and shacks. Children with no shoes going into winter.

And into that, friend, came another union. A smaller one, run by Communists, out of the cities, called the National Miners Union. They came in and organized where the big union had left. They set up soup kitchens of their own. And now the coal operators had something even better than killers to point at. They had Reds.

Now I want you to understand something about that, because it is a thing folks in the cities never understood about the mountains. The men who joined that union mostly did not know what a Communist was, and would not have cared much for one if they had met him. A man up a hollow in Harlan County in nineteen thirty-one did not join a union because of anything he read in a book. He joined because his children were hungry, and those were the people offering soup.

And I will tell you what happened to one of those people. His name was Harry Simms. He was a young organizer for that little union, nineteen years old, from up north. And in February of nineteen thirty-two, in the coal country not far from Harlan, he was shot, the accounts say, by a deputy, along a railroad track, and he died of it.

Nineteen years old, friend. He had come down into those mountains to help men he did not know feed children he had never seen. And there was a miner named Jim Garland, who was Aunt Molly Jackson's half brother, and he wrote a song about Harry Simms. And that song got sung too, for years after, wherever union people gathered. I mention it because I want you to understand that in Harlan County in those years, the songs were not decoration. Folks did not have newspapers that would tell their side, and they did not have money for lawyers. What they had was songs. A song was how a mountain family told the world what had been done to them, and made sure nobody forgot.

Hold on to that, friend. It is the reason Florence Reece's song matters as much as it does.

Now let me tell you about the woman with the calendar.

Her name was Florence Reece. She was born in nineteen hundred, in a coal camp in Tennessee, and she grew up in coal camps, and she married a coal miner. His name was Sam Reece. And in Harlan County in nineteen thirty-one, Sam Reece was a union organizer.

Which made him exactly the kind of man that Sheriff Blair's deputies were looking for.

So Sam was hiding out in the hills. And one night, the deputies came to the house. Florence was there with the children. The accounts say she had heard the deputies meant to arrest her, or her teenage boy, and hold them until Sam turned himself in, so she sent the boy away. And the men came in, and went through the house, looking for Sam, and for union papers, and for anything else they could use.

And when they had gone, she took that calendar down off the wall. And she wrote.

I want you to see that night the way I have always pictured it, friend, though I will tell you plainly this part is my own picturing and not anybody's record. A woman at a kitchen table, with a coal oil lamp. The house turned over. The children finally asleep, or pretending to be. Her husband out somewhere in the dark woods with men hunting him. And no paper. Not a sheet. So she reaches up and takes down the one thing on that wall that has nothing written on the back of it. A calendar, from some store or some company that gave them away, with a picture on the front and the days of the year on it. And she turns it over and writes, by lamplight, the angriest and plainest thing she knows how to say.

I have thought more than once, friend, that it may well have been a calendar given out by a store. Storekeepers gave them away to their customers every year. I did myself. I like to think it was not from a company store. But I expect it may have been.

Now, I am not going to sing it for you, friend. I have a voice that would empty a church. And I am not going to give you all the words, because they are hers, not mine. But I will tell you what the song does. It tells you that in Harlan County, there were no neutrals. That you were either a union man, or a thug for the sheriff, and there was no standing in between. And then it asks you, over and over, the one question.

Which side are you on?

She set the words to a tune she already knew. There is some argument about which tune. She said one time it was an old hymn. Another time, a folk singer remembered her saying it came from an old song out of Scotland. I expect she did not think much about it at the time. She had a song in her, and she put it to the first music that came.

And the song got out of that house, friend. It got sung in the soup kitchens, and on the picket lines, and in the union halls. Nobody had to print it. It went the way the old ballads always went in these mountains, one mouth to the next.

Now my next question. The writers from New York. Which side were they on?

In November of nineteen thirty-one, word of what was going on in Harlan County had gotten up north, and a group of writers came down to see for themselves. It was led by one of the most famous novelists in the country, a man named Theodore Dreiser. And with him came another well-known writer named John Dos Passos, and others. They held hearings. They sat in rooms in Harlan County and in the county next door and listened to miners and their wives tell what had been done to them. And they wrote it all down, and published it, in a book called Harlan Miners Speak.

And at one of those hearings, a woman stood up, a midwife and a miner's wife who folks called Aunt Molly Jackson. And she sang them a song she had made up herself, about how hungry the children were. They called it the Ragged Hungry Blues. And those writers from New York sat there and listened to a mountain woman sing about children starving, and I expect it was the first time some of them ever heard anything like it.

Aunt Molly Jackson went north after that. She sang in New York, in front of big crowds, raising money for the Harlan miners. A woman out of a coal camp, standing up in front of thousands of city people. That was something, friend.

Now, the writers were on the miners' side. No question about that. But I will tell you, the coal operators and the county did not take it kindly. The way it has come down, Dreiser got himself indicted down there before he left. Not for anything to do with the miners. The county officials said a young woman had been seen going into his hotel room, and they charged him over it. Dreiser laughed it off, and went home to New York, and never came back to stand trial. But I will tell you what every man at my counter who ever heard that story said. They said, that's how you do it in Harlan County. If you cannot answer what a man says, you find something else to hang on him.

So the writers came, and they wrote, and they went home. And the miners of Harlan County were still there.

Now, the middle of this story, friend, is the part where most folks think it must have gotten better. A new president came in, Franklin Roosevelt, in nineteen thirty-three. And in his first years, Congress passed laws that said, for the first time in this country, that working men had a right to join a union. A right. Written into the law of the United States. A couple of years later, in nineteen thirty-five, came the biggest of those laws, the one folks called the Wagner Act, after the senator who wrote it. It said a company could not fire a man for joining a union. It said a company had to sit down and bargain with the union its men chose. Everything those miners in Harlan had been shot at and starved for, all of it, was now the law of the land.

And Harlan County did not care.

Because a right on paper in Washington does not mean much on a dirt road up a hollow in Harlan County, when the man coming down that road with a rifle has a badge on, and the coal company pays him.

The sheriff changed. By the middle of the thirties, it was a man named Theodore Middleton. But not much else did. The gun thugs stayed. The union men still got shot at, beaten, run out of the county. Union organizers who came into Harlan were met at the county line and told to turn around, and the ones who did not turn around were sometimes sorry. Folks started calling it Bloody Harlan, and the name stuck so hard that people still say it today.

And so here is a place where the story turns, friend, because finally, somebody in Washington came down hard.

In nineteen thirty-seven, a committee of the United States Senate, led by a senator from Wisconsin named Robert La Follette, held hearings about what companies were doing to keep unions out. And the most famous hearings they held were about Harlan County. They called the coal operators. They called the deputies. They called the sheriff.

And they asked Sheriff Middleton about his money.

The record shows that the sheriff admitted his wealth had gone up by something over a hundred and two thousand dollars since he had become sheriff in nineteen thirty-four. A hundred and two thousand dollars, friend, in three years, in the middle of the Depression, on a sheriff's pay. He owned stock in coal companies now. He owned a farm. Businesses.

I ran a store for forty years. I know how to add. Those numbers do not add unless somebody else is putting in.

And there was testimony about one of the coal operators' men, a fellow named Ben Unthank. A witness described him to the senators as the operators' head road killer.

The head road killer. That is what a man said under oath, in front of the United States Senate, about the way business was done in Harlan County.

And a former deputy came up there and testified that the sheriff had offered him money to hide out during the investigation, so he would not have to talk. That man had both hands crippled by bullet wounds. He said other deputies had shot him.

Now, friend, I want you to imagine those hearings coming over the radio in a store like mine. Men standing around with their hats on, listening to a United States senator ask a Kentucky sheriff where he got a hundred thousand dollars. It was the first time a lot of mountain folks ever heard the government take their side out loud. I have talked to men who remembered it. They said it was like hearing somebody finally say what everybody already knew.

There was one man I knew, friend, who had a particular reason to remember those years, and I want to tell you about him because he was the nearest thing to me in this whole story. As a young fellow, he had worked as a clerk in a company store in one of the Harlan camps. Behind a counter, just like mine. Ringing up flour and lard and lamp oil against the miners' wages.

And he told me once, standing on the other side of my counter, that the worst part of the job was the ledger. Every family had a page in the company store ledger, and every week the store took what they owed out of the man's pay before he ever saw it. And he said that in the hard years, he watched page after page go deeper into the red, and there was nothing he could do about it. He said when a man joined the union and got fired, the company would close out his page, and the family would come in that last time, and he would have to tell the woman at the counter that her credit was finished. He said he could not look at them. He said, I was just a clerk, Otis. I didn't have a side.

And then he was quiet a while. And then he said, I've thought since that I did.

The jury could not agree. There were no convictions.

But friend, something had broken anyway. The country had seen it. And by the end of the nineteen thirties, the coal operators of Harlan County, one after another, signed contracts with the United Mine Workers. The union came into Bloody Harlan, at last, after the better part of ten years of shooting and starving and evicting.

They won, friend. It cost what it cost, and it took what it took, but they won.

And the song kept going.
