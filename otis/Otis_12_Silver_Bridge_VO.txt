It was two minutes before five o'clock on a Friday evening, ten days before Christmas, and the bridge was full.

Bumper to bumper, both ways, across the Ohio River. Folks coming home from work. Folks coming back from Christmas shopping, with packages on the seat beside them. A traffic light at the West Virginia end had gone red, and the line of cars sat there on the bridge, waiting, the way they did every evening at that hour.

A young woman named Charlene Wood was near the front of that line. The light turned green. She started up onto the bridge.

And she said later that the bridge started to shake. And she heard a sound like a boat had rammed into it underneath. And in front of her, the whole bridge folded down into the river, like somebody had pulled a tablecloth off a table.

In less than a minute, thirty-one cars and trucks went into the Ohio River. Forty-six people died. Two of them have never been found.

And to this day, there are folks who will tell you a monster did it.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I remember that Friday, friend. Not because I was there. Because of what I was doing when the news came.

The middle of December was the busiest time of the whole year in my store. Folks came in for their Christmas oranges and their candy and their nuts in the shell, and the post office window had a line out the door with packages going all over the country. And that Friday evening, I was behind the counter, wrapping somebody's parcel in brown paper and string, when a man came in out of the cold and said, the bridge at Point Pleasant fell in the river.

Just like that. The bridge fell in the river.

And everybody in that store stopped what they were doing. Because every one of us had driven over a bridge like that. Every one of us had sat on one at a red light, waiting, never once thinking about what was holding us up.

I have heard a great deal about that bridge in the years since. More than I ever wanted to. And I noticed that when folks talk about it, they mostly talk about one of two things. Some talk about the bridge, and the steel, and the engineers. And some talk about the thing with the red eyes that was seen around that town for a year before it fell.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to give you both. First the monster, because that is what most of you have heard of. Then the bridge, minute by minute. And then the steel, all the way down to the one little piece of it that let go.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you which of those two explanations the people of that town chose to believe. And why I think the true one is a good deal more frightening than the monster.

Let me start with the town.

Point Pleasant, West Virginia, sits right where the Kanawha River runs into the Ohio. It is an old river town, friend, as old as anything in these parts. There was a battle fought right there in seventeen seventy-four, before there even was a United States, and folks in Point Pleasant are proud of that. Virginia militia against the Shawnee, and the Shawnee were led by a chief named Cornstalk.

Hold on to that name, friend. Cornstalk. Because Point Pleasant had a story about a curse long before it ever had a story about a monster, and I am going to give it to you when I get to the old stories.

Across the Ohio, on the other side, is the state of Ohio, and the town of Gallipolis a little way downstream.

And for a long time, if you wanted to get from one side to the other, you took a ferry. Then, in nineteen twenty-eight, they built a bridge.

It opened in May of that year. And it was something new. Most big suspension bridges hang from great cables of twisted wire. This one did not. This one hung from chains. Not chains like you would put on a dog, but long flat bars of steel, called eyebars, each one with a big round hole at each end, linked together with pins, the way you might link your fingers. Two of those chains ran across the river, up over two towers and down again, and the roadway hung from them.

It was the first bridge of its kind built in this country. And when it was finished, they painted it with aluminum paint, so that it shone in the sun. That was a new thing too, in those parts. And that is why everybody called it the Silver Bridge.

It started out, the way a good many river bridges did in those days, as a toll bridge, where you paid your nickels and dimes to cross. Later on, the state of West Virginia bought it, and in time the tolls came off. And for the folks on both sides of that river, it stopped being a wonder and became what every good bridge becomes. Something you do not think about. A man in Gallipolis could work in Point Pleasant. A woman in Point Pleasant could shop in Gallipolis. Children went to see their grandparents on the other side every Sunday. Thirty-nine years of that, friend. Two towns stitched together by one bridge.

Hold on to those chains, friend. Two chains of steel links. Everything in this story hangs on them.

Now the monster.

In November of nineteen sixty-six, a little more than a year before the bridge fell, two young married couples were out driving around late one night north of Point Pleasant. There is a place out there folks call the TNT area. During the Second World War, the government had built a plant out there to make explosives, and when the war was over they left it. Big concrete igloos for storing the stuff, half buried in the ground. An old power plant. Woods and ponds and dirt roads. It was where young people went to park, and to be out where nobody could see them.

Their names, as they have come down, were Roger and Linda Scarberry and Steve and Mary Mallette. It was the fifteenth of November, getting on toward midnight.

And they told the sheriff the next morning that they had seen something out there by the old power plant. Something big. Taller than a man. Shaped something like a man, with great wings folded on its back. And eyes. Red eyes, that glowed when the headlights caught them.

They said it chased their car. They said they were going fast, very fast, and it kept up with them.

Now, friend, I want to give this to you plainly. Those four young people were scared to death. Every account I ever heard says so. They did not go into the sheriff's office to play a joke. They went in shaking.

And once the newspaper printed it, other folks started saying they had seen it too. Over the next year, there were a good many reports around Point Pleasant. Big wings. Red eyes. Something standing by the road, or on a roof, or flying over a car. A newspaperman gave it a name, and the name stuck. Mothman.

Now, I have always found the name itself worth a word, friend. That was nineteen sixty-six. And that very year, there was a show on television that every child in America watched, about a fellow in a cape called Batman. And Batman had villains with names like that, the Penguin, the Riddler. I have heard it said that whoever first called the thing Mothman had that show in his head. I cannot prove it. But I notice that if those two couples had seen it ten years earlier, it would have had some other name altogether. A thing gets named by whatever the folks who see it already have in their heads. That is true of every creature in these mountains, and I do not see why it should not be true of this one.

Now, there was a man at the university up in Morgantown, a wildlife biologist named Robert Smith, who looked at those descriptions and said, I think I know what that is. He said it sounded like a sandhill crane. That is a big gray bird, friend, almost as tall as a man when it stands up, with wings that can spread out seven feet. And it has a patch of bare red skin on the top of its head, right up by the eyes. A crane like that is not supposed to be in West Virginia. It might have wandered off course from where it belongs, farther west. And if you had never seen one, and it came up out of the dark at you in your headlights, with that red around its eyes, you might think you had seen something nobody ever saw.

I give you that, friend, because I think it is the most honest explanation anybody ever offered. But I will tell you, it did not satisfy anybody in Point Pleasant. Nobody wanted it to be a crane.

And I know what some of you are saying, because men said it to me at my counter. A crane does not have eyes that glow red. A crane does not chase a car down the road at a hundred miles an hour.

Well, friend, let me give you what I know about that, which is only what any man who has ever driven a country road at night knows. Animals' eyes shine in headlights. A deer's eyes, a possum's, an owl's. They catch the light and throw it back at you, and depending on the creature, they can look green, or yellow, or red. Every hunter in these mountains has seen eyes shining at him out of the dark and not known what they belonged to until he got closer.

And as for the speed, I will only say this. I have known men who swore a bear ran alongside their truck for a mile, and men who swore a panther crossed the road in front of them twelve feet long. Fear does a thing to a man's sense of distance and speed. That does not make him a liar. It makes him a man who was scared, at night, on a lonely road. Those four young people saw something. I believe that with my whole heart. What it was, I could not tell you. And neither, I think, could they.

And then, a year later, the bridge fell.

And here is the part that turned a story about a big bird into a legend that is told all over the world. The folks who had been seeing the thing said that after the bridge fell, the sightings stopped. And people put those two things together. The creature had been a warning. It had come to tell them something terrible was going to happen. And when it happened, it left.

Now, there is a plainer way to look at the sightings stopping, and I would not be fair if I did not give it to you. After the bridge fell, nobody in Point Pleasant was out joyriding in the TNT area looking for monsters. They were burying their dead. The newspapers were not printing creature stories that winter. They were printing the names of the forty-six. When folks stop looking for a thing, and stop talking about a thing, the reports of it stop too. That is not a prophecy. That is grief.

But I will tell you, friend, that plain explanation never caught on half as well as the other one. And I will come back to why.

A writer from New York named John Keel had been coming down to Point Pleasant during that year, looking into the sightings and the lights in the sky that folks were reporting. And in nineteen seventy-five, he put out a book called The Mothman Prophecies, and he wove all of it together. The creature. The lights. Strange visitors. And the bridge. And after that book, Mothman belonged to the whole country.

I will come back to Mothman at the end, friend. Because the people of that town had a choice to make about him. And what they chose tells you a great deal.

But first, the bridge. Minute by minute.

Friday, the fifteenth of December, nineteen sixty-seven.

It was cold. It was the Christmas season. And the Silver Bridge, by then, was thirty-nine years old.

Now I want you to think about what had happened on that bridge in thirty-nine years. When it was built, in nineteen twenty-eight, the cars on the road were Model Ts and the like. A Model T weighed about fifteen hundred pounds. By nineteen sixty-seven, the ordinary family car weighed something like four thousand pounds. More than twice as heavy. And there were trucks on that bridge now, big ones, of a kind nobody had even imagined in nineteen twenty-eight.

The bridge had been built for one world. It was carrying another.

And that evening, at the West Virginia end, there was that traffic light. And when it was red, the cars backed up across the bridge. Folks who drove it every day said that at rush hour, the bridge would be stopped full, end to end, every evening. That evening, the accounts say, there were thirty-seven vehicles on it.

At about four fifty-eight in the evening, it happened.

The folks who were on it said there was a sound. Some said a crack, like a rifle. Some said a groan. Charlene Wood thought a boat had hit it.

And then the bridge came apart. Not one end, the whole thing. The chains let go, and the towers went, and the roadway, with the cars on it, dropped into the Ohio River. The accounts say it took less than a minute. Some say it was closer to twenty seconds.

Charlene Wood's car was right at the edge. She put it in reverse. The road in front of her was gone, and her front tires were sitting on the very lip where the roadway had broken off. And a man came, a volunteer named Robert Rimmey, and got her out of that car and walked her off what was left of the bridge.

That was how close it was, friend. The difference between Charlene Wood and the folks a car length ahead of her was a few feet of concrete.

Of the thirty-seven cars and trucks on that bridge, thirty-one went into the river.

It was December. The river was cold, and running high and fast, and dark coming on. Some of the cars went straight to the bottom. Some floated for a moment. Some of the people got out. A man named Frank Wamsley, twenty-eight years old, from Point Pleasant, was one of them. He told the newspaper afterward that he did not remember much. He said, I got hold of something and swam to the top. And then he said he got hold of something floating, he said it looked like a barrel of cotton, and he held on to it until the rescue men picked him up.

A barrel of cotton, friend. In the Ohio River, in the dark, in December. That is what was between Frank Wamsley and the bottom.

Others were not so lucky.

On both banks of that river, that evening, people came running. They stood along the water in the dark and the cold, looking for cars they knew. Wives whose husbands drove that bridge home from work every night. Mothers whose children had gone across to shop. The accounts say there were people standing on both sides of the Ohio, looking across at each other over the water where the bridge had been, and neither side able to get to the other anymore. Two towns that had been one for thirty-nine years, cut in half in less than a minute.

Forty-six people died. Men coming home from work. Women coming home from shopping. Folks from both sides of the river, from Point Pleasant and from Gallipolis and the country around. And Christmas presents, the accounts say, floating on the water, coming up from the cars below.

I have never been able to get that picture out of my head. Christmas presents, wrapped, floating on the Ohio River in the dark.

The search went on for weeks. Divers went down into that river, in the cold, in water so dark they could not see their own hands, feeling their way through a pile of broken steel and cars on the bottom. They brought the people up one at a time. And when it was done, two of the forty-six were never found. The river kept them. It has them still.

In the days after, they brought cranes and barges into that river, and they lifted the cars up off the bottom, one by one, and set them on the bank, full of mud and river water. And folks stood on the bank and watched, because every car that came up out of that river was somebody's car. Somebody who knew the color of it would see it rise up out of the water and know.

There were funerals all that week before Christmas, friend, on both sides of the river. Churches that had their Christmas greenery up already, and the wreaths on the doors, holding funerals in among them. I have always thought that must have been the hardest Christmas any town in this country ever had. The presents were bought. Some of them were still at the bottom of the river.

Now, here is where the story turns, friend, and I want you to pay close attention, because this is the part that does not make the legend.

When a bridge falls, the first thing everybody wants to know is why. And the first answers people reach for are always the big ones. Somebody did not do their job. Somebody cut corners. The bridge was old and nobody took care of it. Somebody is to blame.

And those things got said, believe me. They got said at my counter, plenty. That the state had not kept the bridge up. That it had not been inspected right. That everybody knew those old bridges were not built for modern trucks.

And some of that had truth in it. But it was not the answer.

The President of the United States, Lyndon Johnson, ordered an investigation. And the investigators did a thing I have always admired. They went down into the river, and they brought up the pieces of the bridge, the steel, the chains, the links, and they laid them out on the ground, and they put them back together, like a puzzle. They went looking for the very first thing that broke.

And they found it.

It was one link. One eyebar, out of all those links in those two chains. On the Ohio side of the river. The investigators gave it a number, number three thirty. And in the eye of that link, the round hole at the end where the pin went through, there was a crack.

Now, friend, I want you to understand how small that crack was. The accounts say the flaw that started it was about a tenth of an inch deep. A tenth of an inch. Hold up your finger and look at your fingernail. It was not much deeper than that is thick.

And it had been growing, slowly, for forty years. The investigators said it was caused by the steel rusting and straining at the same time, year after year, in that one spot. They have names for it, stress corrosion and corrosion fatigue. But what it means is this. A tiny flaw, in a place nobody could see, got a little bigger every year, for forty years, until one cold evening in December it was big enough. And that link broke.

And because of the way that bridge was built, friend, that was all it took. Remember the chains. Two chains of links. On most bridges, there are a great many pieces sharing the load, so if one goes, the others can hold. But on the Silver Bridge, each link carried its share alone. There was nothing to take up the slack. When one link broke, the chain broke. When the chain broke, the bridge fell.

Let me put it to you the way I came to understand it, behind my own counter. I had shelves along the back wall, heavy with canned goods, and each shelf sat on brackets. Now, if a shelf sits on four brackets, and one of them pulls loose, the other three hold it a while. It sags. Things slide. But you have time to see it and fix it. That is what the engineers call a second chance. But if you build a shelf so that each bracket holds its own piece, and nothing else can take up the weight, then the day one bracket goes, the whole shelf comes down, and every can on it hits the floor at once.

The Silver Bridge was that second kind of shelf, friend. Each link held its own piece. And there was no second chance anywhere in it. The engineers later had a name for a piece like that, a piece where if it fails, the whole thing fails. They called it fracture critical. Every link in those two chains was fracture critical. And nobody in nineteen twenty-eight had been thinking that way.

One link. A tenth of an inch. Forty-six people.

And here is the part that I have always found hardest to sit with. The investigators said that crack could not have been seen. Not by an inspector walking the bridge. Not by anybody looking at it with any method they had. It was hidden inside the joint, in the eye of that link, where the pin covered it. To find it, you would have had to take the bridge apart.

So you see, friend, there was nobody standing at the edge of that bridge with a clipboard who missed it. There was no man to hang. There was a flaw nobody could see, in a design that gave it no second chance.

Now I want to give you the other side, because I promised myself I would, with every one of these.

There are folks who will tell you that is too easy. That the state should have known that old chain bridges were dangerous. That the bridge was carrying twice the weight it was built for. That if it had been inspected properly, or replaced sooner, those people would be alive. And I will give them part of that. The bridge was old. It was carrying more than it was built for. It was not well kept. Those things are true, and they surely did not help.

But the investigators were clear about the thing that actually broke. And they said nobody could have seen it. I have always thought that if you want somebody to blame, you have to blame something bigger than one man. You have to blame the way the whole country built bridges back then, and then forgot about them.

Because here is what happened next, and it is the best thing in this whole story.

There was another bridge, upriver, at a town called Saint Marys, built on the same design. Same kind of chains. Same kind of links. And after the Silver Bridge fell, nobody could trust it anymore. It was closed, and later it was torn down. Nobody knew if there was a crack hiding in one of its links too, and nobody was willing to find out the hard way.

And the whole country woke up. Before the Silver Bridge fell, believe it or not, there was no national program in this country to inspect bridges. None. A bridge got built, and then it got driven on, and then folks mostly forgot it was there until something went wrong. After the Silver Bridge, Congress passed a law. And out of that law came the first national standards for inspecting bridges in this country. Every bridge on the federal highways had to be looked at, by somebody qualified, on a schedule. Over and over, for as long as it stood.

Think about that, friend, the next time you drive across a bridge. Every one you cross, somebody has been out there with a flashlight and a hammer, looking at it. And the reason they do it goes back to that Friday evening in Point Pleasant. Forty-six people paid for every one of those inspections.

They built a new bridge too, a little way downriver. It opened about two years after the old one fell. They called it the Silver Memorial Bridge. Folks drive across it every day.

Now I promised you the old stories too, and with this one, friend, the old stories are all about Mothman.

Folks said the creature was seen on the bridge itself, not long before it fell. Up on the towers. I have heard that more than once, and I have never found anybody who would say they saw it there with their own eyes. It was always somebody's cousin.

Folks said that after the bridge, the creature went on to other places where terrible things were about to happen. That it shows up before a disaster, the way a bird flies up before a storm. I have no way to know about that.

And folks said that some of the people who saw Mothman that year got telephone calls afterward, strange ones, and visits from strange men in dark suits. That one comes mostly out of John Keel's book, and I will leave it there.

Here is what I can tell you for sure. Point Pleasant had a choice to make about Mothman. And for a long time, it could not make it.

I had customers over the years with kin in that town, and I asked them about it more than once. And what they told me was that the town was split right down the middle. Some folks would not hear the word Mothman said in their house. They had lost a neighbor, a cousin, a man from church, in that river. And it made them sick to hear strangers from New York and California talk about their dead like they were a chapter in a book about flying saucers.

And other folks in that same town told the Mothman story every chance they got. To visitors. To reporters. To their grandchildren. And they told it, as near as I could make out, the way you would tell about an omen your granny saw before somebody passed. Not as a joke. As a warning that came, and that nobody understood in time.

And I have thought a good deal about why so many chose to keep telling it.

And there is one more piece to that, which I promised you. The curse.

Remember Cornstalk, the Shawnee chief who fought the Virginians at Point Pleasant in seventeen seventy-four. A few years after that battle, in seventeen seventy-seven, Cornstalk came back to Point Pleasant. Not to fight. He came to the fort there to talk, the history books say, to warn the Americans that the Shawnee might be pulled into the war on the British side. And the Americans held him there. And while he was being held, some American soldiers, angry over the killing of one of their own men nearby, broke in and murdered him. Cornstalk, and his son, and others with him. Men who had come in peace, killed while they were held.

The history of that is plain enough, and it is a shameful thing. And out of it, friend, grew a story that has been told in Point Pleasant for two hundred years. That Cornstalk, as he died, put a curse on that ground. That the land would never prosper, and that trouble would come back to it, over and over, for as long as anybody remembered what was done to him.

I cannot tell you whether Cornstalk ever said any such thing. Historians I have read about it doubt that he did. But I can tell you that every time something terrible happened in Point Pleasant, a flood, a fire, a bad accident, somebody would bring up the curse. And when the Silver Bridge fell, friend, they brought it up so many times that I heard it at my own counter, a long way from that river. A man buying shotgun shells said to me, well, that's Cornstalk's curse again. And another man said, no, that's the Mothman. And they stood there and argued about it.

And not one of them, that afternoon, said anything about a crack in a piece of steel.

So here is where I come down, friend. I promised you I would tell you which explanation the people of that town chose to believe, and why I think the true one is more frightening.

I believe that in their hearts, a good many folks chose the monster. Not because they were simple people. They were not. But because the monster means something. If a creature with red eyes came to warn them, then the bridge falling was part of some plan. There was a reason. There was a warning, and it was given, and folks just did not understand it in time. That is a terrible story. But it is a story with a shape to it.

And the truth has no shape at all.

The truth is that you got in your car on a Friday evening ten days before Christmas, with your packages on the seat beside you, and you sat at a red light on a bridge you had crossed a thousand times. And inside one piece of steel, where nobody could see it, where nobody had looked in forty years because nobody could, there was a crack about as deep as your fingernail is thick. And it had picked that evening. Not for any reason. Not as any warning. It just got there.

That is what frightens me about the Silver Bridge, friend. Not a creature with red eyes. That the thing that kills you can be that small, and that quiet, and that patient. Holding you up for forty years, right up until it doesn't.

There was a man who drove a truck, a regular of mine, who used to cross that bridge with loads now and then, before it fell. And after it fell, he told me he never once again stopped his truck on a bridge if he could help it. If he saw a red light ahead and a bridge between him and it, he would pull over before the bridge and wait for the light to turn, and let the cars behind him honk all they wanted. He did that for the rest of his driving life. He said, I know it's foolish, Otis. And I said, I don't think it's foolish at all.

I said a prayer every December after that, at my counter, when the parcels were stacked up for mailing and folks were in a hurry to get home to their families. I never told anybody what it was for. It was for the forty-six. And for whoever was sitting at a red light on a bridge somewhere that evening, not thinking about what was holding them up.

That's as far as the story goes, friend, and about as far as I'll take you tonight.
