In a hospital in eastern Kentucky, in the middle of the nineteen seventies, a baby boy was born blue.

Not a little blue around the lips, the way a newborn sometimes is for a minute. Blue. Dark blue, all over, the color of a bruise, the color of a plum. The doctors and nurses had never seen anything like it. They thought his heart was failing. They thought his blood was starved of air. They rushed him to a bigger hospital. They got ready to give him a transfusion, to change out every drop of blood in his little body.

And then, the way the story has been told, somebody in the family said, wait. Before you do that. Have you ever heard of the blue Fugates of Troublesome Creek?

The doctors had not. But there was an old woman back in the hills who could have told them. And a doctor in Lexington who had already figured it out, about ten years before. And a family that had been blue, on and off, for a hundred and fifty years.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I first heard about the blue people, friend, the way you hear about most things that sound made up. From a man who swore he was not making it up.

He was a drummer, a traveling salesman, who came through my store a couple of times a year selling notions and patent medicines. And he had been all over eastern Kentucky. And one afternoon he leaned on my counter and said, Otis, I'll tell you something you won't believe. There's a family back up in the hollows of Perry County that's blue. Blue as a jaybird. Whole family.

And I laughed at him. Everybody in the store laughed at him. We figured it was a drummer's story, the kind they told to pass the time.

But he did not laugh. He said, I saw a woman with my own eyes, hanging out wash in her yard, and her face and her arms were as blue as that can of bluing on your shelf. And he pointed right at it.

Now, if you are young enough not to know what bluing is, friend, let me tell you, because it matters to this story. Bluing was a little bottle or a block of blue powder that every household kept, and every store sold. You put a few drops in your rinse water on wash day, and it made white sheets and shirts look whiter, because a touch of blue covers up the yellow. My mother used it. Every woman in the hills used it. And that man was telling me there was a woman whose skin was the color of the stuff she was rinsing her sheets in.

I never forgot that. And years later, I found out every word he said was true.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to walk you down that family tree, one branch at a time, from the first man who came up Troublesome Creek to the baby in the hospital. Because the only way to understand the blue Fugates is the way the doctor finally understood them. By the family tree.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you what I believe finally cured the blue people of Troublesome Creek. And it was not the doctor's medicine. Not really. It was something a lot plainer, and I think a lot more interesting.

Let me start with the creek.

Troublesome Creek runs through the mountains of eastern Kentucky, through Knott County and Perry County and Breathitt, not far from the town of Hazard. And it got its name honest, friend. It is a crooked, hard creek in a crooked, hard country. Steep ridges. Narrow hollows. For most of the eighteen hundreds, there was no real road into that country. No railroad. You went in on foot, or on horseback, or not at all.

The county seat of Perry County is Hazard, and both the county and the town got their names from the same man, a navy commodore named Oliver Hazard Perry, who won a famous battle on Lake Erie in the War of Eighteen Twelve. Folks in that country always found it a little funny to live in a place named Hazard. They said it fit.

And up where Troublesome Creek forks, in Knott County, in nineteen hundred and two, two women came from the Bluegrass country and started a school. A settlement school, they called it. The Hindman Settlement School. It was one of the first places in that whole country where mountain children could get a real education, and folks came from all over to see it. I mention it because it tells you something. Even in nineteen hundred and two, people from outside looked at Troublesome Creek and saw a place that had been cut off from the world so long that somebody had to carry the world in to it on purpose.

And the families who settled up those hollows mostly stayed. They farmed the bottoms and the hillsides, and they married the folks who lived over the next ridge, because those were the folks there were to marry. And over a hundred years, in a country like that, nearly everybody ends up kin to nearly everybody else.

Hold on to that, friend. Nearly everybody kin to nearly everybody else. That is the whole of the science in this story, and I will explain it as plain as I can when we get there.

Now the first branch of the tree.

Sometime around eighteen twenty, the way the family story goes, a man named Martin Fugate came to Troublesome Creek and claimed land. The family tradition says he was an orphan, from France, though I have never seen that proved and I will not swear to it. Other tellings have him coming from back east, from Virginia or the Carolinas, the way most of the families up those creeks did. Families like to have a story about where they started, friend, and a French orphan is a better story than a farmer from Virginia. I will let you pick. Wherever he came from, he settled up the creek and he married a woman named Elizabeth Smith.

And the old story says that Elizabeth Smith was as pale and fair as the mountain laurel that blooms white along that creek in the spring.

And that Martin Fugate and Elizabeth Smith had seven children. And four of them were blue.

Now, friend, I want you to imagine being Elizabeth Smith in eighteen twenty-something, up a creek in Kentucky with no doctor for a day's ride in any direction. And you have a baby, and the baby is blue. Not sick. It cries, it eats, it grows. It is just blue. And then you have another, and that one is not. And then another, and that one is blue.

What would you think? You would not have any word for it. There was no word for it. You might think it was a curse. You might think it was something you did. You might think it was the Lord's will. And you would raise those children anyway, the blue ones and the pink ones together, and you would hope the neighbors did not talk too much.

And here is the first surprising thing about the blue Fugates, friend. The blue did not seem to hurt them much. The blue children grew up. They worked the farm. They married. They had children of their own. Some of them lived to be old. The blue was on the outside. Whatever it was, it did not seem to be killing them.

Now the second branch.

Martin Fugate's children grew up, and they married. And because of where they lived, they married into the families close by. The Smiths. The Combses. The Ritchies. The Stacys. Those names are all over that part of Kentucky to this day. And then those children's children married back and forth among those same families. Cousins married cousins, second cousins, third cousins. That was not anything shameful up there in those days, friend. It was just how it went, in a place where the next family over the ridge was the only family you were going to meet before you were grown.

And down through those branches, every so often, a blue baby would come.

Not every family. Not every generation. That was the strange part. A blue child might be born to two parents who were not blue at all. And a blue man might marry and have a whole houseful of children, and not one of them blue. It skipped around. It hid. It would disappear for a generation and then come right back, in a grandchild.

And the people of Troublesome Creek, being the people they were, made up their minds about it the only way they could. Some said it was bad blood. Some said it was a heart trouble that ran in the family. And some said it was the water. And some, I expect, did not say anything at all, because it is a hard thing to talk about when your own child is the color of a bruise.

I want to stop and tell you what that was like, friend, as near as I ever heard it. Because it was not all just a curiosity.

The blue Fugates were ashamed. That comes through every account I ever read or heard. They did not like to go into town. They did not like strangers staring at them. When folks came around, the blue ones would sometimes stay back in the house. Children at school, when there was a school, could be cruel, the way children are. And a blue grown man, going into Hazard to sell something or buy something, knew every eye on the street was on him.

There is a story in one account of a woman, one of the bluest of them all, who was so blue that folks said she was as blue as Lake Louise. And who, the story goes, would rarely go anywhere she might be seen. That is a hard way to live, friend. Healthy, strong, able to work as hard as anybody, and hiding in your own house because of the color of your skin.

Hold on to that shame. Because the doctor who finally came along saw it too, and it is a big part of why he did what he did.

And I want to tell you about another kind of blue, friend, because in those years, the word blue baby meant something else to most doctors, and it is part of why the Fugates confused everybody.

A blue baby, in those days, most often meant a baby born with something wrong in its heart. A hole, or the vessels hooked up wrong, so that the blood did not get to the lungs to pick up the air it needed. Those babies were blue because their blood was starved. And they were not healthy blue children who would grow up and work a farm. They were sick, and a great many of them died young.

Then, in nineteen forty-four, at Johns Hopkins hospital in Baltimore, a surgeon named Alfred Blalock and a children's heart doctor named Helen Taussig, working with a brilliant Black lab technician named Vivien Thomas, who had worked out how to do it on animals, performed an operation that rerouted the blood in a blue baby's heart. And the baby turned pink on the table. They called it the blue baby operation. It was in all the newspapers, and it saved thousands of children after.

So you can see, friend, why any doctor in America who saw a blue baby thought heart, right away. That is what blue meant. And that is exactly what nearly happened to that little boy in the hospital in Kentucky. The doctors thought heart. And it was not his heart at all. It was his great-great-great-great-grandmother.

And here, friend, is where this story turns. Because for a hundred and forty years, everything anybody knew about the blue Fugates came from guessing. Curses. Water. Bad blood. Hearts. And then one man decided to stop guessing and start counting.

Now the third branch. The one that put Troublesome Creek in the medical books.

By the nineteen sixties, eastern Kentucky was not as cut off as it had been. The coal companies had come in, and the railroad had come to Hazard back before the First World War. And there were clinics now, and doctors, though not many.

And there was a young doctor at the University of Kentucky in Lexington, a specialist in the blood, named Madison Cawein. And he started hearing a rumor. About blue people. Up in the hills.

Now, he was a scientist. He did not believe in bad blood or curses. But he believed there must be something. A family of blue people did not just happen. So he went looking.

And the way it has come down, he was at a clinic in Hazard when two people walked in. A brother and a sister. Their names were Patrick and Rachel Ritchie. And they were blue.

I have read that the two of them had been to doctors before, over the years, and had been told all manner of things. That it was their hearts. That there was nothing to be done. That they would just have to live with it. And I expect when they walked into that clinic, they were not hoping for much. Just one more doctor, one more stare.

Dr. Cawein, the accounts say, was polite and careful with them. He did not stare. He did not treat them like a sideshow. He asked them questions. He asked about their family. And they told him, as near as they could, about the blue ones going back. And he got the idea that the only way to figure this out was to do what nobody had ever done for the people of Troublesome Creek. Sit down and draw the whole family tree.

So he did. He and a nurse who worked with him, a woman named Ruth Pendergrass, who knew those hills and those people, went up and down the hollows, talking to the old folks, writing down who married who, who had children, which children were blue. And they took blood. And they drew it all out, branch by branch, back to Martin Fugate and Elizabeth Smith.

And when he had it all on paper, he could see it.

Now, friend, I am a storekeeper, not a doctor. But let me try to give you the science as plain as I can, because it is a beautiful thing when you understand it.

Every one of us carries the makings of our body in two copies. One from our mother, one from our father. Most of the time, that is a fine thing. If one copy is a little bit broken, the other one does the work, and you never know the difference.

Now, the blue Fugates carried a broken copy of one particular thing. A thing in the blood. Our blood carries air around the body, and to do that, the blood has to stay in a certain condition. And it has a little helper, a thing the doctors call an enzyme, that works all day long, keeping the blood fixed up the way it ought to be. The blue Fugates were short on that helper.

And without enough of it, part of their blood turned a different color. Not bright red, the way blood is supposed to be. A dark, chocolate brown. And dark brown blood, seen through white skin, makes a person look blue. Especially the lips. Especially the fingernails.

Now here is the key, friend. To be blue, you had to have two broken copies. One from your mother, and one from your father. If you only had one, the good copy covered for it, and you were as pink as anybody. But you could still pass the broken one on to your children, without ever knowing you had it.

So Martin Fugate carried it. And by the oddest luck in the world, the woman he married, Elizabeth Smith, carried it too. And when two people who each carry one broken copy have a child, there is about one chance in four that the child gets both broken copies. And that child is blue.

Seven children, friend. Four blue. That is a good deal more than one in four. But that is how the dice fall sometimes.

And then the children married their cousins. And their cousins very often carried the same broken copy, because they came from the same few families. So the blue babies kept coming. Skipping a generation, hiding in the pink ones, and then turning up again whenever two carriers happened to marry.

That is the whole secret, friend. That is what the doctor saw on his family tree. Not a curse. Not bad blood. A broken copy of one small thing, passed down quietly through a hundred years of cousins up a creek with no road out.

Hold on to that road. I am coming back to it.

Now here is the part I love best in this whole story.

Once Dr. Cawein knew what it was, he knew how to fix it. Or at least how to fix the color. Because there was a medicine that could do the work of that missing helper, at least for a while. And here is the joke, friend, and it is a joke the Lord himself might have played. The medicine was blue.

It was a blue dye. The doctors call it methylene blue. It is as blue as anything you ever saw. And Dr. Cawein, the accounts say, went to the blue Fugates and offered to give them a shot of this blue dye.

Now think about that from their side. A doctor from Lexington comes up the hollow and says, I can make you pink, and I am going to do it by putting something blue into your blood. I expect some of them looked at him like he had lost his mind.

But they let him. And the accounts say that within a few minutes of the shot, the blue began to fade. And then it was gone. Their skin turned pink. The lips, the fingernails. For the first time in their lives, some of those people looked in a mirror and saw a color they had never seen on themselves.

I have read that some of them cried, friend. I believe it. Imagine looking pink for the first time when you are forty years old.

The shot did not last forever. The body clears that medicine out. But the doctor left them pills, a little of that same blue medicine to take every day. And as long as they took the pills, they stayed pink. He did warn them of one thing, the accounts say, and I tell you because it is the kind of detail a man never forgets. The pills would turn their water blue. Their urine. And I expect that was a small price to pay for walking down the street in Hazard with nobody staring.

Dr. Cawein wrote it all up for the medical journals in the nineteen sixties. And after that, the blue Fugates were in the books.

Now, let me tell you about the baby in the hospital.

That was the fourth branch, friend, and it shows you how a thing like this can hide in a family. Around nineteen seventy-five, a baby boy was born into one of those families, a Stacy, the way it has been told. His mother and father were not blue. Nobody had told those young doctors at the hospital anything about Troublesome Creek. And there was this baby, as blue as a plum.

They did what any doctor would do. They thought his heart was failing, or his lungs. They took him to a bigger hospital. They ran tests. They got ready to change out his blood.

And then, the way the story goes, his grandmother asked them whether they had ever heard of the blue Fugates. And once the doctors knew to look, the tests showed it. The baby had the blue blood of his great-great-great-great-grandparents, from up the creek, from Martin Fugate and Elizabeth Smith.

And here is the happy end of that branch. The baby got pinker as the weeks went by. The accounts say that by the time he was a few weeks old, most of the blue had faded, and that afterward it only showed when he got cold, or when he was mad and crying, in his lips and his fingernails. He grew up fine.

A magazine writer told that baby's story in the early nineteen eighties, in an article about the blue people of Troublesome Creek. And after that, the whole country knew about them. Folks wrote songs about them. People made jokes. And people in Perry County, friend, I am told, got awful tired of strangers asking them if they were blue.

Now I want to give you the other side, as I always try to.

There are folks who will tell you this is not a story that ought to be told at all. That the blue Fugates were real people, with real feelings, who spent a lifetime being stared at, and that every time somebody tells their story for entertainment, it is one more stranger staring. And that the story makes eastern Kentucky look like a backward place full of cousins marrying cousins, which is an old and ugly way that folks from outside have always looked down on the mountains.

I take that seriously, friend. I do. That is why I have tried to tell it the way I have. Not as a freak show. As a family. And I will tell you plainly, cousins marrying cousins was not some mountain sin. It was what happened anywhere people were cut off. It happened in old villages in Europe and in the islands off the coast of New England and among the kings and queens of Europe, who were all kin to each other, and some of them had a lot worse than blue skin to show for it. The only difference with Troublesome Creek is that the result was something you could see.

And the other thing I will say is that the blue Fugates themselves, the ones who let the doctor take their blood and draw their family tree, did a fine thing. They let a stranger into their most private business, so that he could figure it out. And because they did, the next blue baby born in a hospital in Kentucky did not get his blood drained out by a doctor who did not know any better. That is not a freak show, friend. That is people helping people they would never meet.

Now I promised you the old stories too, and there are a few.

Folks said, before the doctor came, that the blue ones were blue because of what they drank. That something in the water up Troublesome Creek, some mineral, some iron or copper in the springs, turned them blue. You can see why folks would think that. Everybody up one creek, drinking from the same springs. But it was not the water. It was the family.

But here is a strange thing, friend, and I only learned it later. The old folks were not entirely foolish to suspect the water. Because there is a way water can turn a baby blue. Out in farm country, a well dug too close to a barnyard or a heavily fertilized field can get too much of a certain kind of salt in it, nitrate, the doctors call it, from the manure and the fertilizer. And when a young baby is fed formula mixed with that water, the nitrate does to the baby's blood something like what the Fugates' broken copy did to theirs. It turns the blood dark, and the baby turns blue. Doctors started finding cases of that in the nineteen forties, in farm country. They still warn about it.

So the old folks who said blue came from the water had the right idea about the wrong family. And I have always liked that. A wrong answer that is close to a right one, from people who had nothing but their own eyes to go on.

Folks said that a blue Fugate could not get married, that no one would have one. That was not so. A good many of them married, and had big families. The bluest woman in the stories, the accounts say, had thirteen children and lived to an old age.

And folks said that if you married a Fugate, your children would be blue. And there, friend, the old story was half right, which is about the best an old story usually does. If you married a Fugate who carried it, and you carried it too, your children might be. And the only way anybody up the creek had of knowing whether they carried it was by having children and seeing.

And there is one I heard at my own counter, years after that drummer told me about the woman hanging out the wash. A man from over in Kentucky, who had kin in Perry County, heard me telling the story and said, well, Otis, you know why they quit being blue, don't you? And I said, the doctor and his pills. And he shook his head and said, no. The road.

And I did not understand him right then. But I do now.

And there is one more I want to give you, because it came to me at my own counter and it has stayed with me. There was a woman who traded with me for years, a good bit younger than me, and one day when the blue Fugates came up, she told me she had been a blue baby herself. Not that kind. The heart kind. She had been born in the nineteen forties with a bad heart, blue as could be, and her folks had been told she would not live to go to school. And then they heard about the operation at Johns Hopkins, and somehow they got her up there, and the surgeons fixed her.

She said her mother told her that when they brought her out of the operation, she was pink, and that her mother did not recognize her at first. Did not know her own baby, because she had never seen her that color.

And then she said to me, so I know a little bit about how those Fugates felt, Otis, when they got that shot. Looking at yourself and not knowing who it is.

So I promised you I would tell you what I believe finally cured the blue people of Troublesome Creek.

Here is where I come down, friend.

It was not the pills. The pills were wonderful. They gave a good many people a pink face for the first time in their lives, and I would not take that away from Dr. Cawein for anything in the world. But the pills only covered it up. They did not stop the next blue baby from being born.

What stopped the blue babies was the road.

Think back to the family tree. The blue came from a broken copy of one little thing, and it only showed up when two people who both carried it married and had children. And the only reason that kept happening up Troublesome Creek, over and over for a hundred and fifty years, was that people up there could not get out to meet anybody else. They married the girl over the next ridge because she was the only girl over the next ridge.

And then the world came up the creek. First the railroad came to Hazard. Then the coal camps, with men from everywhere. Then the roads. Real roads, paved roads, that a school bus could run on and a young man could drive his first car down. And then the young people of Troublesome Creek started going to school in town, and working in town, and going off to the army, and going to Ohio and Michigan for factory work. And they met people. People from somewhere else. People who did not carry the same broken copy.

And they married them.

And the broken copy did not go away, friend. It is still out there, hiding in the pink ones, passed down quiet. But now it is almost never going to meet another broken copy coming from the other side. And so the blue babies almost stopped coming.

Now, I want to be fair to the science, friend, because a doctor would want me to be. That broken copy did not disappear from the world because a road came up the creek. Somewhere out there, today, there are a great many people walking around who carry one copy of it and will never know, because their husband or their wife does not carry the other. And every now and then, by the oddest luck, two of them will meet, the way Martin Fugate and Elizabeth Smith met, and a blue baby will come. It still happens. Only now, the doctors know what it is, and there is a pill for it, and nobody in that hospital is going to drain the baby's blood.

So that is what I believe cured the blue people of Troublesome Creek. Not a needle full of blue dye. A blacktop road and a school bus, and a whole generation of young people going out into the world and falling in love with somebody from somewhere else.

I have always thought there was something sweet in that. All those years, the thing that made them blue was being cut off. And the thing that made them pink was finally being part of everything else.
