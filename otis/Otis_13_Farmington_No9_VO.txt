On a cold morning in November, the women of a little West Virginia coal town stood in a company store and waited.

They waited all that day. They waited all that night. They waited for ten days. They slept on the floor and on the benches. They drank coffee out of paper cups. Every time a man in a company jacket came through the door, every head in that store turned toward him.

Seventy-eight of their men were somewhere under the ground beneath them. Husbands. Sons. Brothers. Fathers. And through the windows, the women could see the smoke and the fire still pouring up out of the shafts of the mine.

Up in the northern part of West Virginia, in Marion County, near the towns of Farmington and Mannington, there was a big underground coal mine. It belonged to the Consolidation Coal Company, which everybody called Consol, one of the biggest coal outfits in the country. The mine was called Consol Number Nine. Folks just said Number Nine.

And I want you to know something about Marion County before I go any further, because the people waiting in that store knew it in their bones. Farmington was not the first time.

Just a few miles away, near the town of Fairmont, there is a place called Monongah. And on the sixth of December, nineteen hundred and seven, two mines at Monongah exploded. The official count of the dead was three hundred and sixty-two, and a good many folks who have studied it believe the true number was higher, because nobody kept a careful list of who went down that morning. A great many of those men were immigrants, from Italy and from eastern Europe, who had come across the ocean to dig coal and could barely speak English. It is still counted as the worst mine disaster in the history of this country.

And here is the thing I want you to notice. After Monongah, and the other terrible mine explosions that same year, the country was shocked, the way it would be shocked again after Farmington. And within a few years, the federal government set up a Bureau of Mines, to study why mines blew up and how to stop it. A bureau to study it, friend. Not to make anybody do anything. Just to study.

Sixty-one years later, in the same county, under the same kind of hills, ninety-nine men went down into Number Nine. And the study was still going on.

So when those families gathered in that company store, some of them had grandparents who remembered Monongah. They knew what the country did after a mine blew up. It grieved for a week, and then it went back to buying coal. Hold on to that, friend, because this time, it went different.

It was a big mine, friend. Miles of tunnels under those hills, running out every direction from the shafts. And like every deep coal mine, it had a danger in it that nobody could see or smell. Methane gas. It seeps out of the coal itself. It is lighter than air, it has no color, and in the right amount, mixed with the air, a spark will set it off. And when it goes, it can stir up the fine coal dust that lies all over a mine, and the dust goes too. One explosion feeds the next, racing down the tunnels faster than a man can run.

The only thing that keeps a mine like that from blowing up is air. Great fans at the surface, pushing fresh air down and pulling the bad air out, day and night, never stopping. Every miner knew it. The fans were the mine's lungs.

Hold on to the fans, friend. I am going to come back to them.

On Wednesday, the twentieth of November, nineteen sixty-eight, the midnight shift, they called it the cat-eye shift, was down in Number Nine. Ninety-nine men.

Now, in a good many mines in those days, there was a little system for keeping track of who was underground. Each man had a small brass tag with his number stamped on it, and when he went down, he hung his tag on a board, or handed it in at the lamp house. When he came up, he took it back. That way, if anything happened, the company could look at the board and know exactly who was down there.

I have always thought about that board, friend. A plain wooden board with brass tags hanging on it. Because on the morning of the twentieth of November, nineteen sixty-eight, somebody at Number Nine looked at the tags, and counted them. And the tags that were still hanging there, when the last man who was coming up had come up, were seventy-eight.

At about half past five in the morning, near the end of that shift, Number Nine exploded.

The folks who lived nearby said it shook their houses. Windows rattled for miles. And then they saw it. Fire and smoke shooting up out of the shafts and the openings of the mine, high into the dark sky. Some said the flames went up a hundred feet and more. One explosion, and then another, and then more, over the hours that followed.

Down below, ninety-nine men were in the dark.

And here is the first thing I want you to know, friend, because it is the only good news in this whole story. Twenty-one of them got out.

Some of them found their way to the surface through the smoke, feeling along the walls, following the air. And eight of them were working near a new shaft that had just been dug, called the Mahan shaft. They made it to the bottom of that shaft, and they waited there. For about four hours, the accounts say, they stood at the bottom of a hole in the ground, with the mine burning behind them, looking up at a little circle of sky.

And then a crane came. A mobile crane, at the top of the shaft, with a long steel cable and a bucket on the end, big enough to hold three men. They lowered it down. I have thought about those men more than once, friend. Standing at the bottom of that shaft, taking turns, deciding who would go up first. And the last ones waiting down there alone after the others had gone up, listening for the cable to come back.

Twenty-one came out. Seventy-eight did not.

Now the waiting.

And the news did not come.

The fires kept burning. There were more explosions, the accounts say, over the days that followed, deep down in the mine. Rescue teams wanted to go in, and they could not. The air in that mine was poison, and the fire was moving, and any man who went down there would have been the seventy-ninth.

I want you to understand what those rescue men were up against, friend, because folks who have never been near a mine sometimes ask why nobody went in. They kept testing the air at the openings, the accounts say, and the air kept coming back deadly. And every time they thought about going in, the mine answered with another explosion down below.

Seventy-eight men were already down there. Nobody in that county wanted to send down seventy-eight more.

The second thing that happened was the miners themselves.

Now you have to understand something, friend. The men who died at Farmington died in an explosion. That is the kind of mine death that makes the news, because it is sudden and it is many at once. Black lung.

Years and years of breathing coal dust, and the lungs go black and stiff, and the man cannot get his breath. He gets so he cannot walk to the mailbox. He sits up in a chair at night because he cannot breathe lying down. And he dies that way, a little at a time. I knew a good many of those men, friend. They came into my store. You could hear them coming before they got to the door. And for most of them, there was not one penny of compensation. The companies and the state said black lung was not a proper disease. That it was just the way old miners were. That you could not prove the mine did it.

And in the winter after Farmington, the miners of West Virginia decided they had heard enough.

On the eighteenth of February, nineteen sixty-nine, miners in the southern part of the state walked off the job. Not because their union told them to. Their union told them not to. They walked out on their own, a wildcat strike. And it spread from mine to mine, until something like forty thousand miners in West Virginia had stopped work. They went to the state capitol in Charleston. They filled the place up. And they said, we are not going back until you make black lung a disease that a miner can be paid for.

And they won, friend. That spring, the state of West Virginia passed a law that said black lung was a disease a miner could be compensated for. Forty thousand men, walking out against their own union's wishes, and they made the state of West Virginia say out loud what every miner's wife had known for a hundred years. The dust killed them.

The third thing that happened was in Washington.

With the widows in the hallways, and the miners on strike, and the pictures from Farmington still in everybody's head, Congress went to work on a law. And it was not a small one. It was the biggest law about coal mine safety this country had ever seen.

It said the government would inspect the mines. Not once in a while. Regularly, and often, and every underground mine. It said inspectors could shut a mine down if it was not safe. It set limits on how much coal dust could be in the air a miner breathed. It set rules for the air, and for the roofs, and for checking for gas. And it said that miners with black lung would get benefits. Federal money, from the national government, for the men coughing in their chairs.

It was called the Federal Coal Mine Health and Safety Act. And on the thirtieth of December, nineteen sixty-nine, the President of the United States, Richard Nixon, signed it into law.

He did not much want to, the accounts say. He did not care for the black lung part.
