For more than thirty years, if you drove down Route Sixteen outside of Fayetteville, West Virginia, you passed a billboard.

It was not selling anything. It had five photographs on it, of five children. Three boys and two girls, the youngest about five years old, the oldest about fourteen. And above them, in big letters, it asked a question.

Who were they? And it asked, what happened to them? And it said there was a reward.

The man who put it up was their father. He had built it with his own hands, on his own land, by the road, where every car going by would see it. And every year, the paint faded, and he painted it again. And he kept painting it until he died. And then his wife kept it up. And when she died, more than forty years after the children disappeared, it finally came down.

The government had said, from the very first week, that those five children had burned to death in a house fire on Christmas Eve, nineteen forty-five.

Their mother and father never believed it. Not for one day of the rest of their lives.

I saw that billboard, friend. More than once. I had a reason to drive through Fayetteville now and again, over the years, and every time I came down that road, there it was. Five children, looking out at the traffic. And every time, I slowed down. I could not help it. Nobody could. And every time, I thought about a father standing out there in the summer heat with a paintbrush, painting his children's faces over again so they would not fade.

And the story came across my counter more times than I can count. Usually around Christmas. And then everybody in the store would start in. Every one of them had a theory. Every one of them had heard something.

Let me start with the family.

The father's name was George Sodder. He was born in Italy, on the island of Sardinia, and his name there was Giorgio Soddu. And I will tell you a thing that folks always mention, friend, and I give it to you for what it is worth. George Sodder never talked about why he left Italy. Not to his children, the accounts say, not to anybody. A boy of thirteen does not usually cross an ocean alone without some reason. He never said what it was. And some folks have wondered, ever since, whether something followed him from the old country. He came to this country as a boy, thirteen years old, the accounts say, alone, in nineteen hundred and eight. And he went to work the way immigrant boys went to work in those days. Hauling water for the men building the railroads in Pennsylvania. And later he came down to Fayetteville, in the coal country of southern West Virginia, and he started a business. A trucking business. Hauling dirt, and then coal. And he did well. He was a hard worker and a smart man, and by nineteen forty-five, he was a respected businessman in that town.

He married a woman named Jennie, who had come over from Italy as a little girl too. And they had ten children. Ten, friend. And they lived in a two-story wooden house a couple of miles outside of Fayetteville.

And I want you to know that the Sodders were not the only Italians in that country, friend. Not by a long way. In the years around nineteen hundred, the coal companies of West Virginia brought in immigrants by the thousands, from Italy and Hungary and Poland and everywhere else, to dig the coal. Whole towns in those coalfields had Italian sections, with Italian groceries and Italian bakeries and Italian churches, where the old folks spoke nothing but Italian. Fayetteville had its share. And those communities were close, and proud, and they had their own politics, carried over from the old country. When Mussolini came to power in Italy, some of the Italians in this country admired him, at least at first, for making the trains run on time, as folks used to say. And some, like George Sodder, despised him. And in a small town, friend, those were not small differences.

And here is the first strange thing. Hold it in your hand.

George Sodder had strong opinions. And he did not keep them to himself. In particular, he had opinions about Mussolini, the dictator who ruled Italy in those years. George hated him. And he said so, loud, to anybody who would listen, including to other Italian men in Fayetteville, some of whom did not see it the same way. Folks said that in the Italian community of that town, there were hard feelings over it.

Hold on to that, friend. A man who spoke his mind about Mussolini, in a town where not everybody agreed.

Now the second strange thing.

In the fall of nineteen forty-five, a few months before Christmas, the accounts say a man came by the Sodder house looking for work. And while he was there, he went around to the back of the house, and he looked at the fuse box. And he said, the story goes, that the wiring was going to cause a fire someday.

George Sodder thought that was odd. Because the power company had just been out and checked the wiring, and told him it was fine.

And then, around that same time, the accounts say, an insurance salesman came to the house. And George would not buy any insurance from him. And the salesman got angry. And he said, and these are the words that have come down to us, that the house was going to go up in smoke, and the children were going to be destroyed. And he said it was payback, for the dirty remarks George had been making about Mussolini.

Your house is going to go up in smoke, and your children are going to be destroyed.

Hold that one in your hand too, friend. Hold it tight.

And there was one more thing that fall. The older Sodder boys noticed, the accounts say, a car parked along the highway near the house, more than once. Somebody sitting in it. Watching the children come home from school.

Now Christmas Eve.

On Christmas Eve of nineteen forty-five, the Sodder house was full. One of the older boys was away in the Army. But the other nine children were home. And the older girl, Marion, who worked at a store in town, had brought home presents for the younger ones, new toys. And the little ones were so excited that they begged to stay up late and play with them. And their mother said they could, as long as the older boys remembered to shut the cows in and put out the lights.

And Jennie Sodder took the littlest one, Sylvia, who was two years old, up to bed. And she went to sleep.

And five of the children stayed up downstairs, playing with their Christmas toys. Maurice, who was fourteen. Martha, who was twelve. Louis, who was nine. Jennie, who was eight. And Betty, who was five.

Say their names with me, friend. Maurice. Martha. Louis. Jennie. Betty. Because in a few hours, nobody would ever see them again.

And think about what kind of Christmas it was, friend. Christmas of nineteen forty-five. The first Christmas after the war. The fighting had ended that summer. Boys were coming home from Europe and the Pacific by the trainload, and families all over this country were having the first Christmas in four years without a gold star or a telegram hanging over it. The Sodders had a son in the Army himself. It was supposed to be the happiest Christmas in years. That is what makes the rest of this so hard to think about.

Now the third strange thing.

At about half past twelve that night, the telephone rang. Jennie Sodder got up and went downstairs to answer it. And on the other end was a woman's voice she did not know. And the woman asked for a name Jennie had never heard of. And Jennie said she had the wrong number. And the woman laughed. A strange laugh, Jennie said later. And hung up.

And when Jennie went back through the house to go to bed, she noticed some things. The lights were still on downstairs. The curtains were not drawn. The front door was not locked. The children who had stayed up were not in the front room. She figured they had gone up to bed and forgotten to close up. So she closed up herself, and turned off the lights, and went back to bed.

Now the fourth strange thing.

A little later, Jennie heard a sound. A loud bang, the accounts say, on the roof. Like something hit it. And then a rolling noise, like something rolling across the roof. And then nothing.

She went back to sleep.

And around one o'clock in the morning, she woke up again. And this time the house was full of smoke.

Now the fire.

George and Jennie Sodder, and four of their children, got out of that house. Marion, the older girl, and the baby Sylvia, and two of the older boys, John and George Junior. They got out alive. And they stood in the yard, in the cold, in their nightclothes. And they screamed for the other five children. The five who had been downstairs playing, who must have gone up to bed in the rooms upstairs. They screamed their names up at the burning house.

And nobody answered.

Now the fifth strange thing. And here they start coming fast, friend.

George Sodder tried to get into the house to get to his children. And he could not. The stairs were on fire. So he ran for his ladder. He always kept a ladder leaning against the side of the house. And the ladder was gone. It was not where it always was.

So he ran for his trucks. He had two coal trucks parked by the house. He meant to drive one of them up against the wall of the house and climb up on it to the second floor windows. And neither truck would start. Both of them, which had run fine the day before, the accounts say, would not turn over.

So the daughter, Marion, ran to a neighbor's house to call the fire department. Because the Sodders' own telephone did not work. And they found out later why. The telephone line had been cut.

A ladder gone. Two trucks that would not start. A telephone line cut. On the same night.

And there were two more things about that night, friend, that the family told and retold. A man who drove a bus through there in the dark, late that night, said afterward that he had seen something strange as he passed the Sodder house. He said he saw people throwing what looked like balls of fire up onto the roof. And Jennie remembered the bang on the roof, and the rolling sound. And later on, the accounts say, little Sylvia, the baby, playing in the yard where the house had been, found a strange object in the brush. A dark, hard rubber thing, shaped something like a pineapple, the family said, like some kind of firebomb or the shell of one. George Sodder believed somebody had thrown it onto the roof that night.

I cannot tell you what that object was, friend. Nobody ever proved what it was. But I can tell you that the family kept it, and believed in it, the rest of their lives.

And the family said one more thing, which I have always thought was worth thinking about. They said that when the fire started, the Christmas lights downstairs were still burning. If the fire had been the wiring, the way the coroner's jury said, the lights ought to have gone out first. They did not.

Now the sixth strange thing.

The neighbor called the fire department in Fayetteville. And nobody answered. They tried and tried. And another neighbor drove into town to find the fire chief. The fire department in Fayetteville was all volunteers in those days, and a good many of the men had been away at the war, and it was Christmas Eve. You have to understand how a little town's fire department worked in those days, friend. There was no station full of men sleeping in bunks waiting for the bell. There was a truck in a garage, and a list of men who would come running when the siren went off, if they heard it, if they were home, if they were sober. On Christmas Eve, with the war barely over and some of the men not back yet, that list was short. And the fire chief, the accounts say, did not drive the truck. He waited for somebody who could. And the accounts say the fire truck did not come until eight o'clock the next morning.

Eight o'clock in the morning, friend. Seven hours after the fire started. By then, the Sodder house was nothing but ashes in the basement.

The fire chief, a man named Morris, the accounts say, looked through those ashes. Not for very long. And he said he found no bodies. No bones. Nothing. And he told the family that the fire had been so hot it had burned the children up completely, bones and all.

Now the seventh strange thing. The one that started it all for George and Jennie.

No bones.

Five children in a house fire, and not one bone, not one tooth, not one scrap of anything. The family did not believe it. And as the years went by, George and Jennie Sodder learned things that made them believe it less and less. Jennie, the accounts say, did a test of her own. She burned the bones of animals, chickens and beef and pork, in a hot fire, to see what would be left. And the bones were still there when the fire was out. Bones do not burn up in a house fire, friend. Even in a crematory, at much higher heat, for much longer, there are bones left. Everybody who knows about such things will tell you that.

And there were other things. Household things. The appliances in that house, they said, were found in the ashes, still recognizable. And it was a wooden house that burned in less than an hour. The family said the whole house was gone, collapsed into its own basement, in something like forty-five minutes. Forty-five minutes, friend. A big wooden house goes fast. But it does not burn long enough, or hot enough, to leave nothing at all of five children. Not a furnace.

And then there was the heart. I give you this one as it was told, friend, because it is the strangest of all, and I cannot swear to every part of it. Years later, a private detective the family hired, a man from over at Gauley Bridge named Tinsley, heard a rumor around Fayetteville. The rumor was that the fire chief had found something in the ashes after all. A heart. And that he had put it in a box and buried it, secretly, and told the family there was nothing. So the detective went to the fire chief and confronted him. And the fire chief, the accounts say, admitted it, and took them to the place, and they dug up a box. And inside the box was a piece of beef liver. Fresh, the accounts say, untouched by any fire. The fire chief, by some tellings, said he had put it there hoping it would satisfy the family and make them stop looking.

Think about that, friend. A man burying a piece of meat in a box, to make a grieving mother think she had found her child's heart, so she would stop asking questions. Whatever else happened in this story, that happened. And it tells you something about how hard some people in Fayetteville wanted the Sodders to stop.
