A young woman is riding a mule up a frozen creek bed in the mountains of eastern Kentucky, in the middle of winter, in the nineteen thirties.

There is no road. The creek is the road. The ice cracks under the mule's feet, and the water underneath is cold enough to stop a man's heart. She has been riding since before daylight. She will ride till after dark. And she is not carrying the mail, or medicine, or groceries.

She is carrying books.

Two big saddlebags full of them, slung over that mule. Old books, worn books, books with the covers coming off. Magazines. A Bible storybook for a family with five children. A tattered copy of a novel for an old woman who cannot get out of bed anymore. And a homemade book, pasted together out of newspaper clippings and recipes, that somebody up the next hollow has been waiting two weeks to see.

She is paid, the accounts say, about twenty-eight dollars a month. She has to furnish her own mule.

And at the end of that frozen creek, there is a cabin with a child standing in the door, who has been watching for her since sunup.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I have told you a good many hard stories on these nights, friend. Murders and floods and mine explosions and men who should have been hanged and were not. And I will tell you more of them. But every now and then, a man needs to tell one where folks did right by each other. And this is one of those.

I first heard about the book women, as the mountain folks called them, from a lady who traded at my store for years. She was a schoolteacher, retired, a sharp old woman who read everything she could get her hands on. And one day I asked her where she had learned to love books so much, growing up poor the way she had, up a hollow with no library within fifty miles.

And she said, the book woman, Otis. Every two weeks. On a mule.

And she told me about it. And I have been collecting it ever since.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to take you along on the route. One day, from before sunup to after dark, the way those women described it. And at every stop, I am going to tell you a piece of the story.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you what folks up those hollows asked the book women for most. It was not what you would think. And I believe it tells you more about the mountain people than anything else in this whole story.

Let me start before daylight, friend, with the saddlebags.

To understand why a woman would ride a mule up a frozen creek with a load of books, you have to understand eastern Kentucky in the nineteen thirties. It was about as poor and as cut off as any place in this country. The Depression had come down on it hard. The coal mines had cut back or closed. Families who had been poor before were hungry now. A great many of them lived up hollows where there was no road at all, only a creek bed and a footpath. No electricity. No telephone. And in a good many of those counties, no library. Not one, in the whole county.

And a great many of the people could not read. Some of the older folks had never been to school a day in their lives. Children went to school when there was a school, and when they could be spared from the work, and when the creek was low enough to cross. Most of the schools up there were one-room schoolhouses, with one teacher for every grade, and a woodstove in the middle, and a term that might only run a few months of the year, because the children were needed for planting and harvest. The teacher often boarded with a family in the hollow. And she might have a handful of schoolbooks for forty children, passed around and shared until they fell apart. And even the ones who had learned to read had nothing to read. A family might own a Bible, and an almanac, and a catalog from a mail-order company, and that was the whole library.

Hold on to that, friend. A family that could read, and had nothing to read. It is the whole reason for what came next.

Now the first stop. The government office.

In nineteen thirty-five, President Roosevelt started something called the Works Progress Administration. The WPA. It was a great big government program to put people to work, in the worst of the Depression, doing all kinds of jobs. Building roads and bridges and schools. Painting murals in post offices. Writing down the old stories and songs. Millions of people, the accounts say, worked for the WPA at one time or another.

And it was not the only one of those programs that reached into these mountains, friend. There was another one, the Civilian Conservation Corps, the CCC, that took young men off the streets and out of the hollows and put them in camps, planting trees and building trails and fighting fires. A great many of the trails and stone shelters in the Great Smoky Mountains park were built by those CCC boys. And in those same years, the government started building a long road along the top of the Blue Ridge, the Blue Ridge Parkway, that folks still drive today to see the leaves turn. And there was a part of the WPA that sent writers out to talk to old people and write down their stories, their songs, their memories of slavery days and pioneer days. Some of what we know about how folks lived in these mountains a hundred and fifty years ago, we know because some WPA writer sat on a porch in nineteen thirty-seven with a pencil and let an old woman talk.

I have always felt a kinship with those writers, friend. That is about what I do at my counter.

And most of those jobs went to men. But there was a woman in Washington, named Ellen Woodward, who ran the part of the WPA that found work for women. And she and the people working with her, with the backing of Eleanor Roosevelt, who cared about this sort of thing a great deal, and who had already been to the mountains herself, more than once, put together an idea for eastern Kentucky.

They would hire local women. Mountain women, who knew the country and knew the people. And give them books. And send them out on horseback and muleback, up the hollows and the creek beds, to carry the books to the families who could not get to a library. Every week or two, the same route, the same families, so folks would know when to watch for them.

They called it the Pack Horse Library Project. And it started in nineteen thirty-five, and it ran until nineteen forty-three.

I want to tell you about Eleanor Roosevelt and these mountains, friend, because folks forget. In nineteen thirty-three, the very first year her husband was President, she went down into the coalfields of West Virginia, to see for herself how the miners' families were living. She went into the camps. She went into the houses. She saw children sleeping on piles of rags, and families living in shacks along creeks that ran black with coal water. And she went home to Washington and would not let anybody forget it. She got the government to build a whole new community for some of those families, a place called Arthurdale, with real houses and a school. She went back to visit it again and again.

A President's wife, friend, sitting in a coal miner's kitchen in West Virginia. Folks in these hills did not forget that. When the book women came, a good many mountain people knew that she was somewhere behind it, and it made them more willing to open the door.

And the women who did it, friend, came to be called the pack horse librarians. But up the hollows, the folks just called them the book women.

Now the second stop. The creek.

The routes were long. The accounts say a book woman might ride a hundred miles or more in a week, or close to it. Up one creek and down the next, over the ridges, along footpaths so steep that she would have to get off and lead the mule.

And I wish you could hear the names of some of those creeks, friend. Eastern Kentucky has the best creek names in the world. There is a creek over there called Hell for Certain. Another one called Kingdom Come. Troublesome Creek, which I have told you about before. Cutshin. Greasy. Lower Devil. Bad Branch. The people who named them, a hundred and fifty years before, were not being poetic. They were telling you what it was like to get up them. And the book women rode every one. In the summer, there were snakes and heat and flies. In the spring, the creeks came up and you had to wait or swim. In the winter, there was ice and snow and cold that went right through your coat.

And they went anyway. The accounts are full of it. Women who rode through blizzards. Women whose mules slipped on the ice and went down. Women who had to walk the last miles leading their animal, when the path got too steep or the mule got too tired. One of them, the story goes, when her horse died, kept going on foot, carrying the books on her back, until she could get another.

And they had to bring their own horse or mule, friend, out of that twenty-eight dollars a month. And I will tell you, as a man who sold a good deal of harness and feed in his time, a mule was the better choice for that work, and most mountain people knew it. A horse is faster on a good road. But there were no good roads. A mule is surer on its feet. It will pick its way down a rocky creek bed in the dark, testing every step, where a horse might panic and fall. A good mule will not go where it is not safe, no matter how hard you kick. Folks call that stubborn. I call it sense. I expect more than one book woman owed her life to a mule that would not step out on bad ice. Feed it themselves. Doctor it themselves. If it went lame, they found another way.

I have tried to imagine it. A young woman, maybe twenty years old, maybe married with children of her own at home, out on a mule in the dark at five in the morning in January, with a hundred miles of creek beds ahead of her that week. For twenty-eight dollars a month. In a time when twenty-eight dollars a month was the difference between a family eating and not eating.

Hold on to that, friend. It was a job. It was a paycheck. But I do not believe any of them did it only for the money. You do not ride up a frozen creek for twenty-eight dollars. You ride up it because there is a child in a doorway at the end of it.

Now the third stop. The center.

The book women did not just ride out from nowhere. Each route had a home base. Somewhere the books were kept, and sorted, and mended, and handed out to the riders. And those centers, friend, were set up wherever there was a place. In a schoolhouse. In a church. In a courthouse, if there was one.

And in a good many places, the accounts say, in the post office and the general store.

When I heard that, friend, I sat down on the stool behind my own counter. Because I thought about my store, and the post office window in it. And I thought, if I had been keeping store up a creek in eastern Kentucky in nineteen thirty-six, the book women would have come to me. My store would have been one of the places. I would have had a shelf in the back with library books on it, and folks would have come in for their coffee and their kerosene and gone out with a book under their arm.

I have always been a little jealous of those storekeepers, friend. They got to be a library.

Now the fourth stop. The books themselves.

Where did the books come from? Not from the government, mostly. The WPA paid the women, but it did not have much money for books. So the books were donated. People and churches and women's clubs and schools all over the country, when they heard about the pack horse libraries, sent books. Old books. Used books. Schoolbooks that a city school was done with. Magazines that somebody had already read.

And a great many of those books came to Kentucky worn out. Pages loose. Covers gone. So the book women and the women at the centers mended them. They glued in the pages. They made new covers out of cardboard and cloth. Some of them, the accounts say, sat up at night at their kitchen tables with a pot of homemade paste, flour and water, mending the books for the next day's ride. Old cereal boxes and wallpaper scraps for covers. Whatever they had. A book that came in falling apart went back out holding together. They took magazines that were falling apart and cut out the good stories and pasted them into new books.

And they made scrapbooks.

Hold on to those scrapbooks, friend. They are the answer to the question I promised you.

When there were not enough books to go around, which was always, the women made their own. They took old newspapers and magazines, and they cut out things folks would want. Stories. Poems. Pictures. And recipes, and household hints, and patterns for quilts. And they pasted them into books, and those homemade books went out in the saddlebags along with the printed ones.

Now the fifth stop. The cabin.

So let me take you to the end of that frozen creek.

When the book woman came up the hollow, the accounts say, the children saw her first. They had been watching for her. They knew her day. And they would run down the path to meet her. Because she was not only bringing books. She was the only person from outside that hollow some of those children would see all week. She brought news. She knew who had a new baby and who was sick and what was happening in town. She was a visitor, in a place that hardly ever had one. Remember, friend, in most of those hollows there was no radio yet. A few families had a battery set, and they would gather the whole neighborhood on Saturday night to listen to the Grand Ole Opry out of Nashville, if the battery held out. But most folks heard about the world from whoever came up the creek. The preacher, once a month. The peddler, once in a while. And now the book woman, every week or two, like clockwork.

And at each house, she would take back the books she had left the time before, and leave new ones. And the folks would tell her what they wanted next. A book about farming. A book with pictures for the little ones. More of that one story. Something from the Bible.

And for the old folks who could not read, or whose eyes had gone bad, the book women sometimes sat down on the porch and read to them. Out loud. A chapter, or a story, or a few psalms. Before they got back on the mule and rode on to the next house.

I think about that, friend. A young woman on a porch in the mountains, reading out loud to an old man who never learned how. With the mule tied to the fence, and the next house two miles up the creek, and the light going.

Not every house was glad to see them at first, I will tell you that. Mountain folks are careful of strangers, and careful of anything that comes from the government. The accounts say some families would not take the books. Some thought it was a trick. Some thought it was charity, and would not take charity. Some were suspicious of what was in the books. And the book women, being mountain women themselves, knew how to handle that. They did not push. They came back next time, and the time after. They talked about the weather and the crops. They left a book for the children, if the parents would allow it. And little by little, the doors opened.

And the accounts say, friend, that by the time it was going full, the pack horse libraries were reaching something like a hundred thousand people in eastern Kentucky. A hundred thousand. Up the creek beds, one saddlebag at a time.

Now the sixth stop. The night school.

I want to take a little side trip here, friend, because the book women did not come out of nowhere. Kentucky had already tried something like it, years before, and I think it belongs in this story.

Back in nineteen eleven, in a county in eastern Kentucky, there was a woman named Cora Wilson Stewart, who was the school superintendent there. And she was bothered by how many grown people in her county could not read or write. Grown men who had to sign their names with an X. Mothers who could not read the letters their sons sent from somewhere far away.

So she started schools for grown-ups. At night. And she held them on nights when the moon was bright, so that folks could see to walk the paths and creek beds to the schoolhouse after their day's work. She called them the moonlight schools.

And the first night, she hoped a hundred and fifty people might come. And the accounts say more than a thousand came. Old men and old women, walking down out of the hollows in the moonlight, to sit at children's desks and learn their letters.

Imagine that, friend. A grandmother of seventy, learning to write her name for the first time, by the light of a lamp, in a schoolhouse she walked to by moonlight.

So when the book women came along, twenty-some years later, there were people up those hollows who had learned to read at the moonlight schools, and had been waiting all that time for something to read.

And the women of Kentucky had been at this longer than that, friend. Back in the eighteen nineties, the women's clubs of the state had started sending out boxes of books, traveling libraries they called them, to the mountain counties. A box of fifty books or so, shipped out to a schoolhouse or a store, kept for a while, then shipped on to the next place. Not enough, never enough. But a start. So by the time the government got around to it in nineteen thirty-five, there had been women in Kentucky trying to get books up those creeks for forty years. The WPA just gave them a paycheck and a mule to do it with.

Now the last stop. The ride home.

The pack horse libraries lasted until nineteen forty-three. And then the war came, and the war took the money, and the jobs, and the young people. And a good many of the book women, I expect, had their own husbands and brothers and sons go off to that war. The same women who had carried books up the creeks spent the next years waiting on letters from Europe and the Pacific. And some of those letters, friend, were written by boys who had learned to read out of a saddlebag. The men went into the army and the women went into the factories, and the WPA was shut down. And the book women put away their saddlebags.

But it did not end there, friend. Because once a place has had books coming up its creeks, it does not forget. After the war, Kentucky started running bookmobiles. Trucks and vans fitted out with shelves, driving out to the country to bring books to the people. And Kentucky took to it like nowhere else. I have heard it said that Kentucky came to have more bookmobiles on the road than any other state in the nation. That is the book women's doing, friend, as far as I am concerned. They just traded the mule for a truck.

And here, friend, I have to turn this story around a little. Because up to now, I have told it like a fairy tale, and it was not. Not to everybody. Now I want to give you the other side, because I always try to.

There are folks who will tell you that the whole thing was the government meddling. That mountain people did not need Washington sending women up their creeks with books, and that there was something a little insulting in it. As if the mountain people were children who needed to be taught. And I have heard that said at my counter, by proud men who did not like to think their families had needed anything from anybody.

And I take that seriously. I am a mountain man myself. I do not like to be talked down to any more than anybody else does.

But here is what I would say back, friend. The book women were not outsiders. They were mountain women. Hired from right there, in those same counties. They were somebody's daughter and somebody's wife, riding up the creeks to their own neighbors. The government paid them. But the government did not ride the mules. And the people up those hollows did not take those books because they were told to. They took them because they wanted them. Because they had been hungry for them for a long time.

There was a fellow at my counter once, friend, who laughed when this came up and told me about his granny. He said his granny up a creek in Kentucky took the books from the book woman for years. Took every one. And then one day, the book woman left a novel that had some kissing in it. Nothing much, he said, by the standards of today. But his granny read about two chapters and was so scandalized that she marched it back down to the road the next time the book woman came and handed it to her and said, I'll thank you to keep that kind of thing out of my house.

And then, he said, she asked for the next one in the same series.

He said, Otis, Granny never did let on she read the rest of it. But the book woman told my mama she had.

There are photographs of them, friend, that the government took at the time, to show what the money was being spent on. Maybe you have seen one. A young woman on a mule, in a long coat and a hat, with saddlebags full of books, stopped in a creek bed with the water running around the mule's legs. Or a book woman on a porch, handing a book to a barefoot child. Or two of them riding single file up a mountain path through the bare trees. I have seen a good many of those pictures. And the thing I always notice is that the women are not smiling for the camera, the way folks do now. They are looking at it plain and steady, like they have somewhere to be and you are holding them up.

And a few of the scrapbooks, I am told, still survive, in libraries and archives, falling apart, with the recipes and the quilt patterns still pasted in, in the handwriting of women who have been dead for fifty years.

Now I promised you the old stories, and there are some good ones.

Folks said there was a book woman who was so faithful to her route that when the creek was too high for her mule, she took the books in a sack on her shoulder and crossed on a footlog, and went on afoot. I believe that one, friend. I believe there were a dozen of them who did it.

Folks said that one family up a hollow had a boy who read every book the book woman brought, every single one, and asked for more, and that the book woman started saving the best ones back just for him. And that the boy grew up to be a teacher. I have heard that told about more than one boy, and I hope it is true of every one of them.

And folks said that the most worn-out books in all the saddlebags, the ones that came back with the pages falling out and had to be mended over and over, were not the novels or the schoolbooks. They were the scrapbooks.

And that brings me to what I promised you.

What did the folks up those hollows ask the book women for most?

Here is where I come down, friend.

They asked for the Bible stories, a good deal. And the magazines. And the children asked for anything with pictures. All of that is so.

But the thing that came back the most worn, the thing folks kept the longest and passed around the hollow and did not want to give back, the accounts say, were the scrapbooks. The homemade books. The recipes, and the quilt patterns, and the household hints, and the poems clipped from the papers.

And here is the part I love most. As the years went on, folks up those hollows started adding to the scrapbooks themselves. A woman would give the book woman her own recipe, for her stack cake or her apple butter, written out on a scrap of paper, and it would get pasted in. A quilt pattern that her grandmother had made up. A cure for the croup. A song. A story. And that scrapbook would go on to the next hollow, with her recipe in it, and the next woman would add hers.

So the book women thought they were carrying the world into the mountains. And they were. But they were also carrying the mountains back out. One hollow to the next. Every recipe and quilt pattern and old remedy that had been kept in somebody's head for a hundred years, written down and pasted in and passed along.

That is what folks wanted most, friend. Not just to read the world's books. To be in a book themselves.

I asked that old schoolteacher, at the end of the day she told me all this, what she remembered best about the book woman. And I expected her to say some book, some story she had loved as a little girl.

She said, her coming up the creek, Otis. She said, I would stand in the door and watch for her from first light. And when I saw that mule come around the bend, I felt like somebody out there knew I was alive.

That's as far as the story goes, friend, and about as far as I'll take you tonight.

Before you head out, do two things for me. First, subscribe, if you want another one of these waiting on you next time the lamp's lit. Second, answer me this one down in the comments: who was the person who first put a book in your hands and made you love reading? Most folks say a teacher or a mother, but the best answers are always about somebody nobody else would remember.

And if there's a story sitting on a shelf somewhere in your own family, one nobody ever bothered writing down proper, leave it there too. I read every single one that comes through.

Name's Otis Greer. Mind the road on your way out.
