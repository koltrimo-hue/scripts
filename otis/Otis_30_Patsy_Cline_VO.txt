On a stormy evening in March of nineteen sixty-three, a little four-seat airplane took off from an airport in west Tennessee, headed home to Nashville in the rain and the dark.

In the back sat a young woman of thirty. She had a scar across her forehead from a car wreck two years before, and she covered it with her hair. She had two little children waiting for her at home. She was the biggest woman singer in country music, and maybe in all of American music. And she had told a friend, not long before, the way the story has come down, that she had already been through two bad ones in her life. And that the third one would either be a charm, or it would kill her.

The airport manager had told the pilot to wait. The weather was bad, and getting worse. Stay the night, he said. Go in the morning.

The pilot wanted to get home. They took off.

About fifteen minutes later, in the woods outside a little town called Camden, Tennessee, the airplane came down out of the clouds and hit the trees. Everybody on board was killed. And when they found her, the accounts say, her wristwatch had stopped at twenty minutes past six.

Her name was Patsy Cline.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I had a jukebox in my store for a good many years, friend. A big glowing thing in the corner, by the drink box, that a vending company kept stocked and came around to empty of nickels and dimes. And I will tell you, from the summer of nineteen sixty-one to the summer of nineteen sixty-three, there was one name on that jukebox that wore out more records than any other.

Folks would come in from the mines on a Friday evening, dirty and tired, and put a dime in, and stand there with a bottle of pop, listening to Patsy Cline sing Crazy, or I Fall to Pieces. Big rough men, friend, with coal dust in the creases of their faces, standing in front of that jukebox not saying a word. And when it was over, one of them would put in another dime and play it again.

And then, one evening in March, the radio said she was dead. And for a week after that, the jukebox played nothing else.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to tell you about Patsy Cline by the three times. The three times something terrible happened to her. Because the friend she said it to was right, friend. Patsy Cline did have three bad ones. And each one changed her.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you what I believe the third one did. Not to her. To the rest of us. And why I believe that voice of hers is going to outlast every one of us who ever heard it on a jukebox.

Let me start in the valley.

Up in the northern end of Virginia, there is a long beautiful valley between the Blue Ridge and the Allegheny Mountains, called the Shenandoah. Apple orchards and farms and little towns. And the biggest town at the north end of it is Winchester. And in September of nineteen thirty-two, in the Depression, a baby girl was born there named Virginia Patterson Hensley.

Everybody called her Ginny, at first. Later on, she would call herself Patsy.

Her mother was named Hilda, and Hilda was only sixteen years old when Patsy was born. So the two of them grew up nearly together. Folks said they were more like sisters than mother and daughter, and they stayed close as anything the rest of Patsy's life. Her daddy was a blacksmith, a much older man, a hard man by the accounts, and the family moved around the valley a good deal, and they did not have much.

And when Patsy was about fifteen, her daddy left. Just left the family. And Patsy, as the oldest, quit school and went to work to help her mother put food on the table for the younger children.

She went to work at a drugstore in Winchester, friend. At the soda fountain. Making milkshakes and fountain cokes and banana splits for the high school kids and the shoppers. And while she did it, she sang.

Now the first time.

When Patsy was about thirteen, before her daddy left, she got sick. Terribly sick. A bad infection in her throat, the accounts say, and a high fever, rheumatic fever by her own telling, that put her in the hospital and nearly killed her.

And she lived. And here is the thing she always said about it, friend. She said that when she got over that fever, her voice was different. She said that sickness did something to her throat, and when she came out of it, she had a big, booming voice, a woman's voice, a voice like a great singer she admired on the radio. She said the fever gave her that voice.

Now, friend, I do not know if a fever can do that. The doctors I have asked do not think so. They think a girl's voice gets bigger and deeper as she grows up, and that it happened to happen around the time she was sick. But Patsy Cline believed it, all her life. She believed the thing that almost killed her was the thing that made her who she was.

Hold on to that, friend. The first bad one gave her the voice.

And from then on, she sang every chance she got. In church. At the soda fountain. On the little radio station in Winchester. At dances and fairs and clubs all through the valley. She would go down to the radio station and walk right in and ask to sing, and they let her. She sang with local bands in places with sawdust on the floor. She sewed her own cowgirl outfits, with fringe, with her mother helping. And folks all over the northern Shenandoah knew the girl from the drugstore with the big voice. And I have heard it said, friend, that not everybody in Winchester was proud of her. That some of the better-off folks in town looked down on the girl from the wrong side of things, with the daddy who ran off and the homemade outfits and the honky-tonks. That they did not think she was quite respectable. I expect that hurt her. I expect it also lit a fire under her. A good many of the people I have told you about on these nights had somebody looking down on them. It is a powerful thing to want to prove somebody wrong.

She married, young, in nineteen fifty-three, a man named Gerald Cline. That is where the name came from. The marriage did not last. But the name did. Patsy Cline.

And in the middle of the nineteen fifties, she got on a television show out of Washington, D.C., a country music show, with a young singer named Jimmy Dean as the host. And a good many people around Washington and Virginia started to know her name.

There was a woman who traded at my store who had grown up over in Virginia, and she told me she saw Patsy Cline sing at a fair, around that time, before anybody outside the valley had heard of her. She said it was a hot night, and the stage was a flatbed wagon, and Patsy came out in a cowgirl outfit with fringe on it, red and white, that you could tell somebody had sewn at home. And she said Patsy put one hand on her hip and opened her mouth, and the whole crowd at that fair went quiet, even the men at the shooting gallery. She said, Otis, I was fourteen years old, and I thought, that girl is going to be famous, and she does not care one bit what any of us think of her.

Now the big break.

In January of nineteen fifty-seven, Patsy Cline went up to New York City, to be on a television show called Arthur Godfrey's Talent Scouts. That was one of the biggest shows on television in those years, friend. A talent contest. Every week, unknown singers and performers got up, and at the end, the studio audience voted with their applause, and a meter showed who won. Millions of people watched.

And Patsy Cline got up there and sang a song called Walkin' After Midnight. And she won.

And you have to understand, friend, how rare that was for a woman in those years. Country music was a man's music, mostly. The big stars were men. Hank Williams, Ernest Tubb, Roy Acuff. A woman might sing harmony, or play the fiddle, or do a comic routine. Only a few years before, a woman named Kitty Wells had become the first woman ever to have a number one country record by herself. And the record companies did not think women singers sold. Patsy Cline was going to change their minds about that.

And that song went up the charts. Not just the country charts. The popular charts too. Folks all over the country who would never have listened to a hillbilly singer heard that girl from Virginia on Arthur Godfrey and went out and bought her record.

And then, friend, for a while, nothing much happened. She was tied up in a record contract that did not do right by her, and the next records did not sell, and she went back to playing clubs and dances. She married again, in nineteen fifty-seven, a man named Charlie Dick, and they had a little girl, Julie, and later a little boy, Randy. And she kept singing. And waiting.

And in nineteen sixty, she got to Nashville. To the Grand Ole Opry. She became a member of the Opry, the biggest honor in country music. And she got a new record company, and a new producer, a man named Owen Bradley, who knew exactly what to do with a voice like hers. He put strings behind her, and soft piano, and smooth background singers. Not fiddles and steel guitars, like the old country records. Something new. Something that sounded like country music in a fine dress. He did it in a little studio he had built on a side street in Nashville, friend, in a building made out of a surplus Army hut, a big half-round metal thing like a giant tin can cut in half, that folks called the Quonset Hut. And out of that tin can came a great many of the records that made Nashville the music city. Folks called it the Nashville Sound.

And in nineteen sixty-one, she recorded a song called I Fall to Pieces. And it went to number one on the country charts. And way up the popular charts too.

Patsy Cline was a star, friend. A real one. After all those years.

And then came the second time.

On the fourteenth of June, nineteen sixty-one, Patsy Cline was riding in a car in Nashville with her brother. And another car came at them head on. And she was thrown into the windshield.

She was terribly hurt. Her face was cut open. Her ribs were broken. Her hip was dislocated. The accounts say she nearly died. And the story that has been told, friend, and I believe it, is that when she was lying there after the wreck, hurt as bad as she was, she told the folks who came to help her to see to the people in the other car first.

And another young singer, a friend of hers named Dottie West, came to the hospital, and the story goes that she sat by Patsy and picked the little pieces of glass out of her hair.

Patsy Cline was in the hospital for a long while. And she came out with a big scar on her forehead that never went away. She wore her hair a certain way to cover it, and wore wigs sometimes, and some headbands. And she came out on crutches. And she went back to work almost right away.

And the accounts say that the first time she walked back out onto the stage of the Grand Ole Opry after that wreck, on her crutches, the whole audience stood up and cheered for her. For a long time.

Hold on to that, friend. The second bad one gave her the scar. And the crowd that stood up.

Now the song.

That same summer, still hurting from the wreck, she was supposed to record a new song. It had been written by a young songwriter from Texas, who was not famous yet, named Willie Nelson. He wrote it as a slow, talky sort of song. And it was called Crazy.

And the accounts say Patsy did not like it at first. She had heard Willie Nelson's own way of singing it, half speaking, with his odd phrasing, and she did not think she could sing it that way. And they worked on it in the studio for hours, and it would not come right. Her ribs still hurt from the wreck, the accounts say, so that she could not hold the long notes.

So they set it aside, and came back to it. And when she came back, she sang it her own way. Slow, and full, and aching. And the story is that she did it all in one take. One time through. And when it was over, everybody in the studio knew.

Crazy, friend. I expect you can hear it in your head right now. That is the song those coal miners kept putting dimes in my jukebox for. It went up the charts, both kinds. And it has been played on more jukeboxes, the folks who count such things say, than just about any song ever recorded.

And she did not stop there. The next year, nineteen sixty-two, she had another number one, She's Got You. And she sang at Carnegie Hall, in New York, with a whole show of Opry stars. And she went to Las Vegas and played a big hotel there. A girl from the soda fountain in Winchester, Virginia, in Carnegie Hall and Las Vegas.

And here is a thing about her that every person who knew her says, friend, and I want you to know it. She was good to people. Especially to other young women trying to make it in that business, which was a hard business for a woman in those days. She helped a young singer from Kentucky named Loretta Lynn, who had just come to Nashville and did not know anybody, and took her under her wing, and gave her clothes off her own back, the story goes, and stood up for her. She helped Dottie West. She helped Brenda Lee. And Loretta Lynn never forgot it, friend. Years later, when Loretta was a great star herself, she made a whole record album of Patsy's songs, and called it I Remember Patsy. And she said, in every interview she ever gave about her, that Patsy Cline was the best friend she ever had in Nashville. She called everybody hoss, men and women both, and she cussed like a sailor, and she would give you the last dollar out of her pocketbook if you needed it.

Now the third time.

In March of nineteen sixty-three, a disc jockey in Kansas City, a man folks called Cactus Jack, had been killed in a car wreck, and left a family without much. And a group of country stars went out to Kansas City to put on a benefit show to raise money for his widow and children. Patsy Cline went. She was not feeling well, the accounts say. She had a cold. She went anyway, and sang, three shows in one day, on the third of March.

And the next day, she meant to fly home. On a little private airplane, a four-seat plane, flown by her manager, a man named Randy Hughes. And with them were two other singers from the Opry. A man named Cowboy Copas, who had been a star in country music for a long time. And a man named Hawkshaw Hawkins, a tall handsome singer, whose wife was a country singer too, and was home in Nashville, expecting a baby. His wife was Jean Shepard, a fine singer herself. And about a month after the crash, the accounts say, she gave birth to their second son. A boy who never met his father.

And Randy Hughes, the pilot, was not only Patsy's manager. He was married to Cowboy Copas's daughter. So that family lost a father and a husband on the same airplane.

The weather was bad. They could not fly that day. So they waited. And on the fifth of March, they took off.

They stopped once, for fuel. And then at a little airport in Dyersburg, Tennessee, to fuel again. And it was raining there, and windy, and getting dark. And the man who ran that airport, the accounts say, told Randy Hughes that the weather ahead was bad. That he ought to stay the night, and fly in the morning. He even offered, by some accounts, to put them up.

And Randy Hughes, the accounts say, was not a pilot who was trained to fly by his instruments alone, in clouds, where you cannot see the ground. He was the kind of pilot who needs to see where he is going. And it was getting dark, and the clouds were coming down.

And he decided to go anyway. The accounts say he said something like, we've come this far, we'll go on.

They took off from Dyersburg a little after six in the evening.

And about fifteen minutes later, flying low through the rain and the clouds, somewhere over the woods near Camden, Tennessee, the airplane went down. The investigators said afterward that the pilot had most likely lost his sense of which way was up, in the clouds, the way a pilot can when he cannot see, and that the plane had come down out of the sky nose first. It hit the trees in a forest, in rough country, near a little creek.

And all four of them died. Patsy Cline. Cowboy Copas. Hawkshaw Hawkins. And Randy Hughes.

When the plane did not come into Nashville that night, friend, the word went around. Folks at the Opry, friends, family, sat up by the telephone. And the accounts say a number of them, country singers and musicians among them, drove out toward Camden in the rain in the middle of the night to help look, stumbling around the back roads and the woods with flashlights, calling out. Some of the biggest names in country music, the story goes, out in the mud of west Tennessee in the dark, hoping. It was rough country, thick woods and ravines, and the rain did not let up.

The searchers did not find the wreck until the next morning. And when they found Patsy Cline, the accounts say, her watch had stopped at twenty minutes past six.

And there is one more thing, friend, and I almost cannot tell it. Two days later, a country singer named Jack Anglin, who had been a friend of theirs, was driving to a memorial service for Patsy Cline. And he was killed in a car wreck on the way.

The third bad one, friend. It was not a charm.

Nashville was stunned, friend. The whole country music business in that town in those years was not much bigger than a family, and everybody knew everybody, and in one evening it lost four of its own. The Opry went on that Saturday night, the way it always did, and the accounts say the singers could hardly get through their songs. And thousands of people came to the funerals. And for weeks, every country radio station in the country played Patsy Cline.

And there was one more record, friend. A month before she died, in February of nineteen sixty-three, Patsy had gone into the studio for what turned out to be her last sessions. And one of the songs she recorded was called Sweet Dreams. A slow, sad song about a woman who cannot stop dreaming about a man who does not love her. And it came out after she was gone. And folks who bought it said they could not listen to it all the way through. Her voice, the last time, singing about sweet dreams.

Now I want to give you the other side, as I always try to, though with Patsy Cline there is not much of one.

There are folks who will tell you she was hard to get along with. That she had a temper, and a mouth on her, and did not take anything off anybody, and that she and her husband Charlie fought like cats and dogs and there was more than one ugly night. And all of that is so, by the accounts. She was not a saint, friend. She was a strong-headed woman in a man's business who had come up from nothing and had learned to fight for every inch.

And there are folks who will tell you that a good part of her sound was Owen Bradley's doing, the strings and the smooth background, and that a great many folks who loved the old fiddle and steel guitar country did not like what Nashville did to country music in those years, making it sound like the radio in a department store. And I understand that. I like a fiddle myself.

But I will tell you, friend, there is no producer in the world who can put that voice into somebody who does not have it.

Now I promised you the old stories, and there are some.

Folks said she knew she was going to die. That she had told people, more than once that last year, that she did not think she would live long. That she gave away some of her things. That she wrote out her will, by some accounts, on a piece of airline stationery, and gave it to a friend to keep. I cannot tell you how much of that is so. But I have heard it from too many people to say none of it is.

Folks said that the people of Camden, Tennessee, went out into those woods the morning after the crash, and that some of them took things from the wreck. Souvenirs. Scraps of the airplane. Things of hers. And I am sorry to say I believe that, friend, because I have told you on these nights about the crowds at hangings and the raisins off a dead girl's cake. We have always come to watch, and some of us have always come to take.

And folks said, and this is the one that came true, that Patsy Cline would sell more records after she died than she ever did alive. And she did. A few years after the crash, they put out a record of her greatest hits. And folks are still buying it. It has sold more copies, the folks who count say, than nearly any country record ever made. In nineteen eighty, they made a picture show about her friend Loretta Lynn, called Coal Miner's Daughter, and an actress played Patsy in it, and a whole new generation of young people found her. And a few years after that, they made a picture about Patsy herself, called Sweet Dreams, after one of her last songs.

She is buried back home in Winchester, friend, in the Shenandoah Valley, where she started. And on her grave marker, the accounts say, there are a few words. Death cannot kill what never dies. Love.

So I promised you I would tell you what I believe the third one did.

Here is where I come down, friend.

The first bad one, the fever, gave her the voice. Or she believed it did, which may be the same thing.

The second bad one, the car wreck, gave her the scar. And the ribs that hurt when she tried to sing Crazy, so that she slowed it down, and sang it her own way, and made it the saddest, most beautiful thing that ever came out of a jukebox.

And the third one, friend, the one in the woods outside of Camden, did a strange thing. It stopped the clock. Not just her wristwatch. All of it.

Patsy Cline never got old. She never had a record that did not sell. She never lost her voice the way singers do as they get older. She never had a bad comeback, or a sad last tour in a half-empty hall, or a scandal, or a fall. She is thirty years old forever, friend. In a cowgirl outfit her mother sewed, or a fine dress with her hair just so over the scar. Standing at a microphone in nineteen sixty-two, singing, with that voice at the very top of what it could ever do.

That is a terrible price, friend. Two children grew up without their mother for it. And I would give it back in a minute if I could.

But it is why, when you put that record on today, it does not sound old. It sounds like she is in the next room.

I had that jukebox for years after she died. And I noticed something. The young folks who came in, the teenagers, the ones who were not even born yet in nineteen sixty-three, would play Crazy too. Not because their parents liked it. Because they did.

Death cannot kill what never dies, friend. Whoever chose those words for her grave, I believe, was right.
