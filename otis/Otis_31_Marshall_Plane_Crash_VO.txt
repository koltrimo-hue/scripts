On a hillside above a river town in West Virginia, on a Saturday night in November, a whole town lost its football team.

The game was over. They had lost, by three points, in North Carolina, in a hard game. And the team got on a chartered jet airplane to fly home. The players. The coaches. The team doctor. The athletic director. And a good many of the town's leading citizens, who had gone along to cheer. Doctors, businessmen, a city councilman, a state legislator, husbands and wives together. Seventy-five people in all, counting the crew.

It was raining, and foggy, and dark. And as the airplane came down toward the airport, it came down too soon. It hit the trees on a hillside, a little short of the runway. And it burst into flames.

Nobody lived. Not one.

Back in town, the families who had come out to the airport to meet the team, and the folks waiting at home by the radio, heard the news that night. Thirty-seven young men of the football team, gone. Their coaches, gone. Their neighbors, gone. And the next morning, the town woke up, and had to decide whether it would ever play football again.

The town was Huntington, West Virginia. The team was the Thundering Herd of Marshall University. And the date was the fourteenth of November, nineteen seventy.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I had the radio on that night, friend. It was a Saturday, and Saturday nights I kept the store open a little late, for folks coming home from town, and the radio was on behind the counter, the way it always was. And somewhere around eight o'clock, the music stopped.

A good many of the folks who came into my store had boys at Marshall. Or had gone there. Or had a cousin or a neighbor's boy who did. It was the big school in that end of West Virginia, friend, the one a coal miner's son or a farmer's daughter could get to, and come home from with a teacher's certificate or a nursing degree, and make something of themselves. Folks in our country were proud of Marshall.

And that night, as the news came in, the store filled up. Folks heard it at home and did not want to be alone with it, so they came to the store. Like they did when the Buffalo Creek dam broke, a little over a year later. Standing around the counter and the stove, listening to the radio, not saying much.

I remember a man, a coal miner, standing by the window with his hat in his hand, saying over and over, the whole team. The whole team.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to tell you this story in two halves. The first half is the night. The second half is the season after. Because the night is the part everybody knows, friend, and the season after is the part I believe matters most.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you about two things. A fountain, that they turn off once a year. And a few words the folks in Huntington say to each other. And I am going to tell you what I believe those words really mean.

Let me start with the town.

Huntington sits on the Ohio River, in the far western corner of West Virginia, right where West Virginia and Kentucky and Ohio all come together. It is a railroad town, a river town, a working town, built by the railroad that ran coal out of the mountains to the river. It was named for the man who built that railroad, friend, a rich man from California named Collis Huntington, who laid out the town in the eighteen seventies as the place where his railroad would meet the Ohio River. A railroad baron's town, full of railroad men and mill men and river men. And in nineteen seventy, it had about seventy thousand people in it, and a college at the heart of it. Marshall University.

And Marshall football, friend, was not a powerhouse. I want to be honest about that. Marshall had not had a great deal of success on the football field in those years. They had had losing seasons. There had even been some trouble with recruiting rules a year or two before. But it did not matter. It was the town's team. On a Saturday afternoon in the fall, half of Huntington was in the stands at the old stadium, Fairfield Stadium, cheering for those boys whether they won or not.

And the coach that year was a young man named Rick Tolley. He was only about thirty years old himself. And the team that fall of nineteen seventy was young and scrappy and coming along.

And I want you to remember what nineteen seventy was like, friend, in this country. It was a hard year. The war in Vietnam was going on, and boys from every town in these mountains were over there, and some were coming home in boxes. That spring, at a college in Ohio called Kent State, a few hours' drive up the road from Huntington, soldiers of the National Guard had fired on students during a protest, and four young people were killed. Colleges all over the country were in an uproar. Young folks and old folks were shouting at each other on the television every night.

And in the middle of all of that, on a Saturday afternoon in the fall, a college football game was one of the few things left that a whole town could sit down together and agree about.

Now the game.

On the fourteenth of November, Marshall played East Carolina University, down in Greenville, North Carolina. And it was a close game, a hard game. And Marshall lost, seventeen to fourteen. Three points.

Now, the team had chartered an airplane for the trip. A jet. A Southern Airways DC-nine, with a crew of five. The team had not flown much before, the accounts say. A lot of trips they went by bus. But this one was a long way, and they had gotten the airplane.

And on that airplane, after the game, were thirty-seven players of the Marshall football team. And the head coach, Rick Tolley. And his assistant coaches. And the athletic director, a man named Charles Kautz. And the team doctor. And the team trainer and the sports information man and the radio man who called the games. And twenty-some boosters, folks from Huntington who loved that team, who had flown down to see the game. A city councilman. A member of the state legislature. Several doctors. Husbands and wives, a good many of them, who had left their children at home with a sitter for the weekend.

Seventy-five people, friend. Hold that number.

And here is a thing that makes it harder still. That same fall, just six weeks before, another college football team had gone down in an airplane. On the second of October, nineteen seventy, one of the two planes carrying the team from Wichita State University, out in Kansas, crashed in the mountains of Colorado, and something like thirty people were killed, a good many of them players. It was in all the newspapers. Every college team in the country that flew that fall flew with that in the back of their minds. I expect some of those Marshall boys thought about it, friend, getting on that airplane in North Carolina. I expect some of their mothers did.

Now the night.

The airplane took off from North Carolina in the evening and flew north toward home. And the weather around Huntington that night was bad. Rain. Fog. Low clouds over the hills.

The airport that served Huntington is up on a ridge, outside of town, near a place called Ceredo, a little way from the river. And to get to it in bad weather, a pilot has to come down through the clouds over the hills, following his instruments, and find the runway at the top.

And at about seven thirty-five that evening, coming in through the rain and the fog, the airplane came down too low, too soon. It hit the tops of the trees on a hillside, about a mile short of the runway. It tore through the trees. And it went down into a hollow, and burst into flames.

The accounts say everybody on board was killed. All seventy-five.

And back in Huntington, friend, a good many of the families had gone out to the airport that evening to meet the team. Wives and children and parents, waiting in the little terminal up on the ridge, the way you do, watching the windows for the lights of the plane coming in out of the clouds. And they saw a glow, the accounts say, off over the hill. And then the fire trucks went screaming past. And then somebody came into that terminal and told them.

Volunteer firemen from the little towns nearby, from Ceredo and Kenova, were the first ones up that hillside, in the rain and the dark and the mud. They went up toward the fire as fast as they could go, hoping to find somebody alive. They did not. And I have known men who were volunteer firemen in towns like those, friend, and the ones who were on that hill did not talk about it much for the rest of their lives.

And among the dead was the man who called the games on the radio. The voice that every Marshall fan in that country had heard every Saturday for years, telling them about the game. He was on the plane with the team, the way he always was. So the folks listening to the radio that night, waiting to hear about the game, were waiting for a voice that was never going to come on again.

The investigators who looked into it afterward said the airplane had come down below the height it was supposed to stay at, before the pilots could see the runway. They talked about the instruments, and the altimeter, and the crew. They never could say for certain why. And I will not pretend I can tell you more than they could.

Now the people who were not on it.

There were a few, friend. And I want to tell you about them, because they are part of the story.

There were some players who were not on that airplane. Some had been hurt, and had stayed home. Some had not made the traveling squad. One of them, the accounts say, a young man named Nate Ruffin, had a hurt arm and stayed in Huntington that weekend. And he and the handful of others who were left became, overnight, the whole Marshall football team.

And there was one coach who was not on the plane. An assistant coach named Red Dawson. He had gone off on a recruiting trip that weekend, the accounts say, to look at high school players, and was driving back instead of flying. And he heard on the car radio, on the road home, what had happened.

Think about that, friend. Driving home through the dark, alone, and hearing on the radio that your whole team, and every coach you worked with, and your friends, are gone. And that you are the one who is left.

He was never the same, the accounts say. How could he be.

Now the morning.

When the sun came up in Huntington on Sunday, the fifteenth of November, nineteen seventy, the town woke up to find out how many of its own it had lost. And it was not just the football team. It was the doctors and the business people and the neighbors. There were children in Huntington that morning, the accounts say, who had lost both their mother and their father, who had gone down to see the game together.

And there were funerals. Day after day after day. All over West Virginia and Ohio and Kentucky and wherever those young men had come from. And there was a memorial service at the stadium, the accounts say, with thousands of people in the stands, in the cold.

There was a woman who traded at my store who had been a nurse in a hospital in Huntington that night. And she told me, years later, that they had gotten word to get ready, that there had been a crash, that they should expect a great many injured. And every doctor and nurse they could reach came in. And they set up the emergency room, and got the beds ready, and laid out the bandages and the blood. And they waited. All night.

And nobody came, friend. Not one patient. Because there was nobody left alive to bring.

She said, Otis, that was the worst night of my life. Not because of what we saw. Because of what we didn't. All those beds made up, and nobody to put in them.

And some of the dead, friend, could not be identified. The fire had been too terrible. And so, in a cemetery in Huntington, on a hillside, six of the young men of that team were buried together, side by side, because nobody could say which one was which. Somebody's sons. Buried together, the way they had played together.

Now here is where the story turns, friend. Because here is where it becomes the second half. The season after.

After the funerals, the people who ran Marshall University had to decide something. Should they keep playing football?

And a good many people thought they should not. The team was gone. The coaches were gone. How could you put a team on the field? And even if you could, how could anybody bear to watch? Wouldn't it be better to let it go? To end it, out of respect for the dead?

And the accounts say the president of the university was leaning toward ending it.

And then the students spoke up. And the few players who were left. And the people of Huntington. The story goes that the students gathered, and the players who had not been on the plane, and they said, the dead boys would not want it to end. That if Marshall quit playing football, then the crash had taken that too. And they would not let it. And the university decided to go on.

But they did not have a team, friend. They did not have a coach. They had a handful of players who had been hurt or left home. And a season to play the next fall.

They needed a coach. And it was not easy, the accounts say, to find one. Who would want that job? To walk into a town in mourning, with no team, and try to make one out of nothing, knowing that whatever you did, folks would be measuring it against the dead?

And a young coach named Jack Lengyel, who had been coaching at a little college in Ohio, said he would do it.

He came to Huntington. And the accounts say that when he got there, he did not try to make anybody forget the old team. He did the opposite. He talked about them. He told the new boys they were playing for the ones who were gone, and that every time they went out on that field, the boys on the hillside were going out with them. Some coaches would have tried to put the crash behind them, friend. He put it in front of them, and told them to carry it. And the first thing he did, the accounts say, was go and find Red Dawson, the assistant coach who had not been on the plane, and ask him to stay. To help. To be the bridge between the old team and the new one. And Red Dawson, who was carrying more than any man should have to carry, said he would, for that one season.

And then they had to find players.

They took the few who were left. They went around the campus and asked boys who played other sports, basketball and baseball, to come out for football. They found walk-ons, boys who had never played college football in their lives. And there was a rule in college football in those days, friend, that freshmen could not play on the varsity team. The first-year boys had to wait. And the organization that ran college sports, the NCAA, made an exception for Marshall. It said, for this one time, Marshall could play its freshmen.

So the team that took the field for Marshall in the fall of nineteen seventy-one was made up of a handful of survivors, a bunch of eighteen-year-old freshmen, some basketball players, and some walk-ons. Folks called them the Young Thundering Herd.

Now the game.

They lost their first game, friend, on the road. Nobody expected anything else.

And then, on the twenty-fifth of September, nineteen seventy-one, the Young Thundering Herd played its first home game. At the stadium in Huntington. Against a school from Cincinnati called Xavier.

And the stands were full. The whole town came. Folks who had been at the funerals. Families of the boys who had died. Folks who had not been to a football game in years. They came and they sat in those stands, the accounts say, in a quiet you could hardly believe, and watched a bunch of kids who were not really a team yet go out on the field where the dead boys had played.

And it was close, friend. All day long. And with the clock running down, at the very end, Marshall was behind, thirteen to nine. Four points. They needed a touchdown, and they had time for one more play.

The quarterback was a young man named Reggie Oliver. And he took the snap. And he threw a short pass to a running back named Terry Gardner. And Terry Gardner caught it, and ran into the end zone.

And the clock ran out.

Marshall fifteen, Xavier thirteen.

I was not there, friend. But I had it on the radio in my store. And I will tell you what happened in my store when that pass was caught. A man who had been sitting on a feed sack by the stove, who had a nephew on that airplane, stood up and did not say a word, and put his hand over his face. And the rest of us did not cheer. We could not. We just stood there. And somebody, I do not remember who, said, they did it. They did it.

And the accounts say that in the stadium in Huntington, when that pass was caught, folks did not know whether to cheer or cry, and most of them did both. People who were there said it was the loudest and the quietest they ever heard a crowd be, all at once.

They did not win many more that year, friend. The Young Thundering Herd won two games that season, and lost the rest. But nobody in Huntington cared about the record. They had played. They had won one at home. They had kept it going. And the other win that year, friend, the accounts say, came on homecoming, against a good team from Ohio called Bowling Green, by two points, twelve to ten. Two wins, both by a whisker, both in front of the home crowd. I have always thought the Lord has a sense of how to tell a story.

And when that season was over, Red Dawson, the assistant coach who had not been on the plane, did what he had told Jack Lengyel he would do. He stayed one season, to get the new boys started. And then, the accounts say, he left coaching, and did not go back to it. He had done what he could stand to do. And I do not believe there is a man alive who could fault him for it. He held that team together for one year with his own two hands, carrying seventy-five people on his back, and then he set them down.

Now I want to give you the other side, because I always try to, though with this story, it is a small one.

There were folks, then and later, who thought the university should not have kept on. That it was too soon. That putting a team of children on the field so quickly, in front of all that grief, was asking too much of those boys, and of the families. That a town ought to be allowed to mourn without being asked to cheer. And I understand that, friend. There were families, I am told, who never could bring themselves to go to another Marshall game. Who could not hear a football game on the radio again for the rest of their lives without leaving the room.

And there is a harder question, which folks asked about the airplane, and about the airport on the ridge, and whether it should have had better equipment to guide planes down in bad weather. That question was asked, and argued, and in the end the airport and the airlines did make changes. But that does not bring anybody back.

Now I promised you the old stories, and I have a few, and they are gentle ones.

Folks said that on the hillside where the airplane went down, nothing grew right for years. I do not know about that. I have heard it said of a great many places where something terrible happened.

Folks said that some of the families of the boys who died went to every single home game for the rest of their lives, and sat in the same seats, and that the people around them always knew who they were, and always made room. That one I believe. That is the kind of town Huntington is.

And folks said, and this one I know is so, that it took Marshall a long time to win again. Years. More losing seasons than anybody wants to count. And that the town stuck with them the whole time. And then, in the nineteen nineties, it all came back. Marshall started winning, and winning big. They won the national championship in their division, more than once. And a young man named Randy Moss came out of the coalfields of West Virginia and played for them, and went on to be one of the greatest players the professional game ever saw. And folks in Huntington who had been in those stands in nineteen seventy-one, watching a bunch of freshmen and basketball players try to play football, lived to see Marshall on top of the world.

Now I promised you two things. A fountain, and a few words.

On the campus of Marshall University, the accounts say, a couple of years after the crash, they put up a memorial. A fountain. A tall bronze sculpture, with water rising up and falling down. And every year since, on the fourteenth of November, the people of Marshall gather around that fountain for a memorial service. They read the names, all seventy-five. And at the end of the service, they turn the fountain off.

And it stays off, friend, all through the winter. Until the spring. When they turn it back on.

I have always thought that was the finest memorial I ever heard of. Not a stone that stays the same forever. A fountain that stops every November, in the cold and the dark, and then starts again in the spring. Because that is exactly what that town did.

And the words.

At Marshall football games, for many years now, the folks in the stands do a thing. One side of the stadium shouts, we are. And the other side shouts back, Marshall. We are. Marshall. Back and forth, over and over, until the whole place is shaking.

Here is where I come down, friend, on what I believe that really means.

When the folks in Huntington shout we are Marshall, I do not believe they are just cheering for a football team. I believe they are saying something they decided on the morning after the crash, when the university was thinking of letting football go, and the students and the few boys who were left said no.

They are saying, we are still here.

Seventy-five people went down on that hillside in the rain. The whole team. The coaches. The neighbors. And the town could have let it all go with them. Quit. Buried the team with the boys. And nobody would have blamed them. Not one person in this country.

They did not quit. They found a coach nobody else wanted to be, and a coach who could barely stand to stay, and a bunch of freshmen and basketball players, and they put them on the field in front of the families of the dead. And they lost almost every game. And it did not matter. Because the first time they won, on the last play, everybody in that stadium knew what it meant.

It meant the crash did not get the last word. They did.

We are Marshall, friend. We are still here.
