In the spring of nineteen thirty-five, in a valley in East Tennessee, men were digging up the dead.

Not grave robbers. Families. Sons digging up their fathers. Daughters standing by while men with shovels opened their mothers' graves. Little country churchyards, on the hillsides above the river, emptied out one grave at a time, the bones laid in new boxes and carried off in wagons and trucks to new cemeteries on higher ground.

Because in a little while, the water was coming. And it was going to cover everything. The churches. The schools. The general stores. The farms that families had worked for a hundred and fifty years. Whole towns. And the government had decided that the graves, at least, should not be left down there under the lake.

Thousands of graves, friend. In just that one valley. And it was only the beginning.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I want to tell you about the day the lights came on at my store, friend.

I was a young fellow then, working for the man who had the store before me. And for years, that store had been lit by kerosene lamps. In the winter, it got dark at five o'clock, and we would light the lamps and trim the wicks and the whole place smelled of coal oil. And then the power line came up the road. And one evening, a man threw a switch, and there were electric bulbs hanging from the ceiling. And the whole store lit up brighter than any of us had ever seen it at night.

And folks came in just to look at it. Grown men stood in the middle of the floor and looked up at those bulbs like children at a Christmas tree.

I will never forget that, friend. And it was years later before I understood where that light came from. It came from a dam. And behind that dam was a lake. And under that lake was somebody's town.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to tell you about the towns under the lakes. The ones the government flooded, all up and down these mountains, in the nineteen thirties and forties, to make the lights come on. I am going to tell you about four dams, and four valleys, and the people who had to leave them.

And at the very end, I am going to answer the question every one of those people asked, and every one of their children and grandchildren has asked since. Was it worth it? And I am going to give you an honest answer, which is not a simple one.

Let me start with the valley as it was.

In the early nineteen thirties, the valley of the Tennessee River and all the rivers that run into it, through Tennessee and North Carolina and Kentucky and Virginia and Georgia and Alabama and Mississippi, was about as poor as any part of this country. The Depression had come down on it like a rock. Farms were worn out. The hillsides had been cut over and plowed till the soil washed down into the rivers. The rivers flooded every spring, and took what was left. And most folks did not have electricity. The accounts say that in those years, only a few farms out of every hundred in that whole valley had electric power. A few out of a hundred, friend. The rest of the country had been using electric lights and electric irons and electric radios for twenty years, and folks in the Tennessee Valley were still reading by coal oil and hauling water from the spring.

And in nineteen thirty-three, a new president came in. Franklin Roosevelt. And he had an idea that was as big as anything any American government had ever tried.

He was going to fix the whole valley at once.

And the idea had a history, friend, that I always found interesting. Down in north Alabama, on the Tennessee River, at a place called Muscle Shoals, the government had started building a dam and some plants back during the First World War, to make nitrates for explosives. The war ended before they were finished, and the whole thing sat there half done. And in the early nineteen twenties, none other than Henry Ford, the automobile man, tried to buy it. He offered to take it off the government's hands and build a great industrial city there, seventy-five miles long, he said. A good many folks in the valley were thrilled. And a senator from Nebraska named George Norris fought him tooth and nail.

Norris said, in effect, that the power of that river belonged to the people, not to Henry Ford. And he fought for more than ten years to have the government develop the river itself, for the folks who lived along it. Presidents vetoed his bills. And he kept at it. And when Roosevelt came in, Norris finally got his way.

He got Congress to create something called the Tennessee Valley Authority. The TVA, everybody called it. And the TVA was going to build dams. Great big dams, all up and down the rivers. The dams would stop the floods. They would make the rivers deep enough for barges. And they would turn the falling water into electricity, and run power lines up every hollow, and bring the lights to the people of the valley. Cheap power, for farmers who had never had any.

And friend, I want you to understand. A great many people in these mountains loved Franklin Roosevelt for it. Loved him. I knew men who kept his picture on the wall next to Jesus, and did not see anything strange about it.

And it was not only the TVA. A couple of years later, Roosevelt started something else, called the Rural Electrification Administration. Because building a dam is one thing, friend, but getting the power from the dam up a hollow to a farmhouse is another. The power companies had never wanted to run lines out into the country. Too many miles of wire for too few customers. So the government helped farmers form their own electric cooperatives. A farmer would pay a few dollars to join, and the co-op would borrow money from the government and string the lines, and buy the power from the TVA dams, and sell it to its own members.

I remember men coming into my store arguing about whether to join. Some of them were suspicious. A few dollars was real money, and they did not trust anything that came from Washington. And then their neighbor joined, and got the lights, and his wife got an electric iron, and that was the end of the argument.

But a dam makes a lake. And a lake has to go somewhere.

And it goes where the people are.

Now the first dam. Norris.

The very first big dam the TVA built was on the Clinch River, up in East Tennessee, north of Knoxville. They named it after a senator from Nebraska, George Norris, who had fought for years in Washington to get it done. Work started in nineteen thirty-three. And the lake behind it was going to cover a great deal of country. Bottomland along the Clinch and Powell rivers, the best farmland in those counties, the land families had been working since before Tennessee was a state.

And it was going to cover a town called Loyston.

Loyston was not a big place, friend. It was a little farm town in Union County. The accounts say it had a post office, two general stores, a filling station, a café, a mill, a barbershop. Churches. A school. Houses. The kind of place where everybody knew everybody, and the general store was where you heard the news, the same as my store. I have always felt a kinship with those two general stores in Loyston. I expect the men who ran them were a good deal like me. They knew who owed what, and whose baby was sick, and whose boy had gone off to look for work.

And one day the government men came, and told them all that in a year or two, Loyston would be at the bottom of a lake.

The TVA had a whole program for it. They called it family removal. Removal, friend. That was the word. They sent caseworkers out, men and women, who went to every farm and every house in the land the lake would cover, and wrote down everything about every family. How many children. What they owned. What they farmed. Whether they owned their land or rented it. Where they meant to go. And the caseworkers wrote down, the accounts say, what each family thought about having to leave. Some were glad of the money. Some were angry. A good many were simply bewildered. People who had never been more than twenty miles from home, being asked by a stranger with a clipboard where they intended to live for the rest of their lives. And the government appraised every farm and every house and offered a price for it.

And then the families had to go.

Not everybody thought the price was fair, friend. I want to tell you that plainly. The government said it paid what the land was worth. But land in a poor valley in the middle of the Depression was not worth much on paper, no matter what it was worth to the family on it. Some folks took the government to court over the price. Some won a little more. Most did not. And a good many families, the accounts say, could not find another farm as good for the money they were paid, because everybody else from the valley was out looking for farms at the same time, and prices in the next county went up.

The accounts say that for Norris alone, something like three thousand families were moved off their land. Three thousand families, friend. And their graves. Something over five thousand graves, the accounts say, were dug up and moved out of the way of that one lake.

I want you to sit with that a minute. Five thousand graves. Every one of them somebody's mother or father or baby. Folks had to decide whether to let the government move their dead or to leave them under the water. And most chose to move them. And so for months, up and down that valley, there were men with shovels in the churchyards, and families standing by, watching their people come up out of the ground.

There was a famous photographer, friend, a man named Lewis Hine, who had spent his life taking pictures of working people and poor children. And the TVA hired him to go and photograph the families in the Norris basin before they were moved. And his pictures are still around. Old men on their porches. Women in front of their cabins. Children in the doorways. Families who had no idea how they would look to anybody a hundred years later, looking straight into the camera on the last days they would ever spend in that valley.

I have seen some of those pictures. They are not sad, exactly, friend. They are proud. That is what makes them hard to look at.

In nineteen thirty-six, the gates on Norris Dam were closed. It was a great wall of concrete, friend, more than two hundred and fifty feet high, across the Clinch River gorge. Folks drove from all over to see it, the way folks drive to see a mountain. I went myself, as a young man, and stood on top of it and looked down, and could not believe men had built it. And the water came up. And it came up over the fields, and over the roads, and over the mill and the barbershop and the two general stores, and over the churchyards where the graves had been dug up, and over Loyston. And Loyston was gone.

And not far away, the TVA built a brand new town, a model town, with nice houses and paved streets, for the folks who worked on the dam. They called it Norris. And it is still there.

And here is a thing I heard at my counter from a woman who came from that country. Her daddy had been a young man when the Norris lake came. And when it came time to move his mother's grave, the government had men to do it, with their own boxes and their own trucks. And he would not let them. He said no stranger was going to dig up his mother. So he went up to that churchyard himself, with his brother and a shovel, on a cold morning, and did it with his own hands. And carried her in the back of his own truck to the new cemetery up the hill, and buried her again.

The woman said he never talked about it after. Not once. She only knew because her uncle told her, years later, when her daddy was gone. And she said, Otis, I think that was the hardest day's work my daddy ever did, and he farmed his whole life.

Now the second dam. Hiwassee.

I will not tell you about every dam, friend, because there were a great many of them. Over the next few years, the TVA built dam after dam, on river after river. And everywhere it was the same story. The appraisers. The caseworkers. The price. The moving. The graves.

On the Hiwassee River, down in the far western tip of North Carolina, the accounts say a couple of hundred families were moved, and some four or five hundred graves. That is a small number next to Norris. But I want you to understand something, friend. To the families in that valley, it was not a small number. It was everybody they knew.

And here is the thing about it that people from outside these mountains do not always understand. When the government bought your farm, it paid you. And a good many folks took that money and bought another farm somewhere, and did all right. Some of them did better than they had before. I will not pretend they did not.

But you cannot buy your granddaddy's farm somewhere else. You cannot buy the spring your great-grandmother carried water from, or the big oak in the yard your children played under, or the church where your parents were married. You cannot buy your neighbors. When a whole valley is moved, the people do not all land in the same place. They scatter. One family goes to the next county, another goes to Knoxville, another goes off to Ohio. And the community that had been there for a hundred and fifty years is gone, the same as if the water had drowned it.

I heard that from more than one person at my counter, friend. They did not mind the money so much. It was the scattering.

Now the third dam. Fontana. And the road to nowhere.

In nineteen forty-one, the country went into the Second World War. And the war needed electricity. A great deal of it. Airplanes were made of aluminum, and making aluminum takes more power than almost anything. And over in East Tennessee, there was a big aluminum company that needed all the power it could get. And there was something else going on in East Tennessee that very few people knew about at the time. At a place called Oak Ridge, the government was building a secret city, working on something it would not talk about. And that needed power too. More than anybody outside could imagine. We found out later, friend, it was the atomic bomb.

And Oak Ridge took land too. That is part of this same story, though it was not the TVA that did it. In nineteen forty-two, the army came into a stretch of valley west of Knoxville, a farming country with little communities in it, with names like Wheat and Robertsville and Scarboro. And the army told about a thousand families there that they had to leave. Not in a year or two, like the TVA lakes. In weeks. Some of them, the accounts say, had only a couple of weeks' notice. Some came home and found a notice tacked to their door. They were not told why. It was a secret. They were not told until after the war that their farms had been taken to build the atomic bomb.

Imagine that, friend. You plant your corn in the spring, and in the fall a soldier tacks a paper to your door and says you have to be gone before Christmas, and he cannot tell you why.

So the TVA built a dam in North Carolina, on the Little Tennessee River, at a place called Fontana. They built it fast, in the war, working night and day. And it was big. Taller than any dam in the eastern part of this country.

And the lake behind it was going to cover a whole stretch of the Smoky Mountains country. Little towns, like one called Proctor, that had been a busy lumber town. And farms up the side valleys. And the accounts say something like thirteen hundred families had to leave.

And here is the part, friend, that the folks in Swain County, North Carolina, have never forgotten.

When the lake came up, a good many of the old family cemeteries in those valleys were not moved. They were on high enough ground that the water would not reach them. But the roads that went to them would be under the lake. So those graves would be cut off. Up on the far side of the water, in what was going to become part of the national park, with no way to get to them except by boat and a long walk.

And the government made a promise. The accounts say that in nineteen forty-three, the government agreed that after the war, it would build a new road along the north shore of that lake, through the park, so the families could get back to their dead. A road to the old cemeteries. So folks could go and put flowers on the graves and clean them off on Decoration Day, the way mountain people always have.

If you did not grow up in these mountains, friend, you may not know about Decoration Day. Once a year, usually in the late spring or summer, every family goes back to the old family graveyard. They clean it off. They pull the weeds and rake the leaves and straighten the stones. They put new flowers on every grave, paper ones in the old days, and real ones when they could. And then they have dinner on the grounds, spread out on tablecloths right there in the churchyard, and the preacher says a few words, and the old folks tell about the ones who are buried there. It is one of the most important days of the year in a mountain family. Folks who have moved away to Ohio or Detroit come home for it.

So when the government told the people of those valleys that their graves would be cut off by the lake, it was not only the dead it was cutting them off from. It was Decoration Day. It was the one day a year a scattered family came back together.

They started that road, years later. They built a few miles of it, and a tunnel. And then they stopped. Over money, and over the damage to the park, and over a great many arguments in Washington. And the road just ends. A nice paved road, through the woods, into a tunnel, and then out the other side of the tunnel, into nothing.

Folks in Swain County call it the road to nowhere.

I have been on it, friend. I drove over there once, years ago, because I had heard about it so many times at my counter. And I drove out to the end of the pavement, and walked through that tunnel, and stood on the far side looking at the woods. And an old man who was there, from Swain County, told me his people were buried up the lake somewhere, and that he had been waiting on that road since he was a boy. And then he said, well, I reckon I'll get there one way or another.

He meant when he died, friend. I knew what he meant.

Now the fourth dam. Watauga. And the town that would not drown.

Up in the far northeast corner of Tennessee, near the Virginia and North Carolina lines, there is a river called the Watauga. And there was a town on it called Butler. And after the war, in the late nineteen forties, the TVA finished a dam on the Watauga. And the lake behind it was going to cover Butler.

And Butler was different from Loyston, friend, in one way. Butler was an incorporated town. A real town, with a mayor and a council. And the accounts say it is the only incorporated town the TVA ever flooded.

And the folks of Butler did a thing I have always admired. They did not just scatter. A good many of them moved their town. They picked up houses, the accounts say, and moved them up the hill to higher ground. They built a new Butler, up above where the water would come. They took their churches. They took their names. They took each other. And they kept on being Butler.

The lake came up, in nineteen forty-eight, and covered the old town. And before it did, the accounts say, folks held last services in some of the old churches, and a last homecoming, so that everybody who had been baptized and married there could come and sit in those pews one more time. I have heard of that being done in more than one valley where the water came. A last hymn, in a church that would be at the bottom of a lake by summer. But the people did not let it go. And to this day, I am told, the folks up there call it the town that wouldn't drown. And there is a little museum, and they get together and remember old Butler.

And here is the strange thing, friend. Every so often, when the TVA lets the water down low in the lake, in a dry year or for work on the dam, the old town comes back up. The foundations of the houses. The old roads. The steps that led up to the front doors of houses that are not there anymore. And people go down and walk around in it. Old folks who were children in that town go and find where their house was, and stand on the front steps.

I cannot think of anything sadder or finer than that, friend. Standing on your own front steps in the bottom of a lake.

And here, friend, is where I have to turn this story around. Because up to now, I have told it from the bottom of the lake. And there is a whole other side, a big one, and it would be wrong of me not to give it.

The TVA did what it said it would do. It stopped the floods. The great floods that used to sweep down the Tennessee River every few years, drowning towns and farms, mostly stopped. It made the rivers so barges could go up them. And it brought the lights.

I told you about the day the lights came on in my store. That happened in farmhouses all over these mountains, friend. Women who had cooked on a wood stove their whole lives got an electric stove. Women who had done the wash by hand on a board got a washing machine. Families got a refrigerator, so the milk would not spoil. Children could read at night by a bulb instead of a lamp. A man could pump water from his well with an electric pump instead of hauling it by the bucket. Folks got radios. They could hear the news, and the Grand Ole Opry, and the President talking to them by the fireside.

It changed everything, friend. Everything. I do not believe anybody who did not live through it can understand how much. Before the power came, life in these mountains was not very different from how it was in eighteen fifty. After the power came, it was the twentieth century.

And the TVA did something else too. It went up on the worn-out hillsides and planted trees. Millions of them. And it taught farmers how to keep their soil from washing away. And it made fertilizer. And the lakes it made became places where people came to fish and swim and boat, and that brought money into counties that had never seen much.

And the power from those dams helped win the war. The aluminum for the airplanes. And whatever you think about the atomic bomb, friend, and I have my own thoughts, the men who fought in the Pacific were glad when the war ended.

So when somebody tells you the TVA was nothing but the government taking people's land, I want you to know that is not the whole story. A great many people in these mountains got a better life out of it. More people got a better life than lost their land. That is just the arithmetic.

But arithmetic is not the whole story either, friend.

Now I promised you the old stories too, and there are some.

Folks say that on still nights on some of those lakes, if you are out in a boat, you can hear church bells ringing under the water. From the drowned churches. I have heard that said about more than one lake. I have never heard it myself, and I expect I never will, since most of those church bells were taken out and moved before the water came. But I understand why people say it.

Folks say there were some who would not leave. Old people, mostly, who sat on their porches and told the government men they were going to die on that land, and the government would have to drown them. And there were some like that. There always are. And in the end, the accounts say, the law moved them, one way or another. There was a picture show made about that, friend, in nineteen sixty, called Wild River. About an old woman on an island in a TVA river who would not leave her land, and a young government man who had to move her. It was made up, but it was made up out of a great many true stories. I saw it at the picture house, and I knew folks who had been through it, and they said it got a good deal of it right.

And folks say that the fish in those lakes grow big because they swim through the old houses and the old barns. That one I give you with a grin, the way it was given to me, by a fisherman who wanted me to buy some of his bait.

So I promised you I would answer the question. Was it worth it?

Here is where I come down, friend.

I stood in my store the night the lights came on, and I looked up at those bulbs, and I was as happy as I have ever been in my life. I will not pretend otherwise. I would not give that back. Nobody I knew would have given it back.

But I have also stood at the end of the road to nowhere, next to an old man who was waiting to go to his family's graves. And I have heard women at my counter tell me about the scattering. About going to the bottom of a lake to stand on their own front steps.

So here is my honest answer. It was worth it. And it cost a great deal. And the folks who paid the cost were not, for the most part, the folks who got the most out of it. The people of Loyston and Proctor and old Butler paid for the lights in Knoxville and Chattanooga and Nashville, and in my store too. They paid with their land and their towns and their churchyards and their neighbors. They were paid money for it, and a good many did all right with the money. But money is not what they lost.

And I believe the least the rest of us can do, friend, every time we flip on a light switch in these mountains, is to remember that somebody's town is under the water so we could do it.

I think about it sometimes, still. I turn on the light in the evening, and I think, Loyston. And I think about two general stores, a lot like mine, sitting down there in the dark at the bottom of Norris Lake.

That's as far as the story goes, friend, and about as far as I'll take you tonight.
