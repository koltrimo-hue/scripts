On a gray September afternoon in nineteen sixteen, in the railroad yard of a little mountain town in Tennessee, they put a chain around the neck of an elephant.

The chain ran up to the arm of a railroad crane, the big kind they used to lift wrecked boxcars back onto the tracks. Thousands of people had come to watch. Men in their work clothes. Women with their pocketbooks. Children, a great many children, sitting on their fathers' shoulders to see over the crowd.

And when the crane began to lift, the elephant's feet came up off the ground. Five tons of her, hanging in the air.

And then the chain snapped.

Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Every town in these mountains has a story it would rather you did not bring up, friend. And over the years, I learned which ones not to bring up at my counter, depending on who was standing there.

But there was one story that every town in East Tennessee loved to bring up. Because it was not theirs. It belonged to one little railroad town called Erwin, down in Unicoi County. And folks from every other town in that end of the state never got tired of saying it. Erwin? Isn't that the place that hung the elephant?

I heard it said as a joke a hundred times, friend. And I laughed along with it, the first few times. And then one day an old man from Erwin was standing at my counter when somebody said it. And he did not laugh. He set his jaw and said, I was there. I was nine years old. My daddy put me up on his shoulders so I could see. And then he picked up his sack and walked out.

I never laughed at it again.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to tell you the whole thing, from the circus coming to town to the chain in the railroad yard. And I am going to tell it like a trial, because that is what it was, though there was no judge and no lawyer and the one on trial could not speak a word in her own defense.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you who I believe really ought to have been on trial in Tennessee that September. Because I do not believe it was the elephant.

Let me start with the circus.

In nineteen sixteen, there was a traveling show called the Sparks World Famous Shows. It was not one of the giant circuses, not Ringling Brothers or Barnum and Bailey. It was a middle-sized show that traveled on its own train, from small town to small town, all over the South and the East, the kind of circus that came to places too little for the big ones to bother with. It was run by a man named Charlie Sparks.

And for a little mountain town in nineteen sixteen, friend, you cannot imagine what it meant when the circus came. There were no picture shows in most of those towns. There was no radio yet. A child might go a whole year and not see anything stranger than a two-headed calf at the county fair. And then one morning the circus train would pull in, and there would be a parade down Main Street. Wagons painted red and gold. A brass band. Men on stilts. Horses with feathers on their heads. And elephants.

Elephants, friend. Walking down Main Street in a town where most folks had never seen anything bigger than a mule.

I remember circus day myself, friend, from when I was a boy, years after this. A smaller show than the Sparks show, by then, and the country was changing. But I remember it like it was yesterday. They let school out. Not officially, but nobody went. Farm families came into town before daylight in their wagons to get a good spot along the parade route. Men who had not taken a day off since Christmas took one. And the boys, me among them, ran down to the rail siding at dawn to watch them unload. The elephants came down the ramp out of the stock cars first, and then they put the elephants to work, pushing the heavy wagons and pulling the tent poles up, like the biggest, smartest mules you ever saw.

And there was always a man walking beside them with a hook. I did not think anything about it then. I do now.

And the star of the Sparks show was an elephant named Mary.

She was an Asian elephant, and she was big. Five tons, the accounts say. The circus advertised her as the largest living land animal on earth, which I expect was a circus man's way of talking. But she was big, and she was smart, and she was famous on that circuit. The posters said she could play tunes on horns. She could pitch a baseball, they said. She was the one the children came to see.

And I want to tell you a little about elephants, friend, because most folks in those mountains knew nothing about them, and I expect that is part of what went wrong. An elephant is not a big dumb beast. It is one of the smartest animals God ever made. It remembers. Folks who worked with them said an elephant would remember a man who was kind to it for years, and a man who hurt it for longer. They live in families, the females especially, and they look after each other, and they grieve when one of them dies. A circus elephant like Mary had been taken from all that, and trained, and the training was not gentle in those days. The hook was how the keepers made them mind. A good keeper, an old hand, hardly ever had to use it hard, because the elephant knew him and trusted him. A green man with a hook, friend, was another matter.

Hold on to Mary, friend. Five tons, and the children's favorite.

Now the man.

His name was Walter Eldridge. Folks called him Red. He was a drifter, by most accounts, a young fellow down on his luck, who had been working as a janitor at a hotel in a little town in southwest Virginia when the circus came through. And the circus needed hands. Circuses always needed hands. And they hired Red Eldridge on the spot as an under-keeper for the elephants.

He had never worked with elephants in his life.

I knew a man, years later, who had worked circuses as a young fellow, in the twenties, before he settled down and came back to the hills and drove a truck. He traded at my store. And one day when Mary came up, he got very quiet, and then he said, they never should have let that boy near her. He said, you do not give a green hand the big elephant. You give him the water bucket and the shovel, and you let him follow an old keeper around for a season, and you let the elephants get to know him. He said, an elephant has to decide she trusts you. You cannot just climb up on her and start giving orders. He said, I'd have bet money on what happened. And then he said, God rest that boy, he never had a chance either.

Hold on to that, friend. He had never worked with elephants. And the circus put him in charge of the biggest one they had.

On the twelfth of September, nineteen sixteen, the way most of the accounts have it, the Sparks show came to Kingsport, Tennessee. Kingsport was a young town then, just getting built up, a new factory town on the Holston River. A group of businessmen had laid it out on paper as a model industrial city, with straight streets and room for factories, and they were busy bringing in industry. Not long after this, the Eastman Kodak company would build a big plant there, and Kingsport would grow into one of the biggest industrial towns in that end of the state. But in September of nineteen sixteen, it was still a raw new place, with more mud than pavement, and the circus coming to town was the biggest thing to happen there in a good while. And the circus did what it always did. It paraded.

And Red Eldridge, who had been on the job a day or two, rode on Mary's back, or walked beside her, leading her down the street in the parade, with the whole town lined up to watch.

And along the way, Mary saw something on the ground. The way it has been told, it was a piece of watermelon rind. Somebody had thrown it down by the side of the road. And Mary, being an elephant, and elephants being fond of a good watermelon, stopped, and reached her trunk down to pick it up.

And Red Eldridge, who did not know anything about elephants, did not want her to stop. So he hit her. With the elephant hook, the bull hook they called it, a stick with a metal hook on the end that keepers used to steer the animals. He struck her with it, hard, the accounts say on the head, behind the ear.

Now I have heard it said, and I want to give it to you because it may matter, that Mary may have had something wrong with her mouth or her head that day. An infected tooth, some say. A sore place. I cannot prove that. But if it was so, friend, then a man who did not know what he was doing had just jammed a metal hook into the one spot on her that already hurt.

And Mary turned on him.

She reached up with her trunk and pulled Red Eldridge down off her back, or wrapped it around him, the tellings differ. And she threw him. Hard, the accounts say, against a drink stand by the side of the street. And then she walked over to where he lay, and she put her foot down on his head.

In front of the whole town. In front of the children who had come to see her.

Red Eldridge died right there in the street.

And I want to stop a minute on Red Eldridge, friend, because in all the years this story has been told, he has mostly been a name. The man the elephant killed. He was a young fellow without much, working as a janitor, sweeping floors in a hotel, who saw a chance to join the circus and see something of the world. That is about as old a dream as there is. And he got about two days of it. Whatever you think about Mary, and I know where I come down, Red Eldridge did not deserve what he got either. He was a poor man the circus used as cheap help, and he paid for their hurry with his life. I have never heard of anybody putting up a stone for him. I hope somebody did.

And somebody started shooting.

There was a blacksmith in that crowd, a man named Hench Cox, and he had a pistol on him. And the accounts say he pulled it and fired at Mary. Five times. And it did not do a thing to her. The bullets just went into all that hide and muscle and she hardly seemed to notice. And I expect that made it worse, friend. Because now the crowd had seen an elephant kill a man, and then they had seen a man shoot that elephant five times, and she was still standing there.

And somebody in that crowd started to holler, kill the elephant. Kill her. And it spread.

Now, let me tell you what happened next, because this is where the town of Erwin comes into it.

The circus got Mary calmed down and back to the train. And they moved on, the way circuses do. The show had a date the next day in another town. But the news did not stay in Kingsport. It went out over the telegraph and into the newspapers, all through that part of the country, and the newspapers did what newspapers did in those days with a story like that. They made it as big as they could. Murderous Mary, they called her. They printed things about her that were not true. That she had killed other men before. That she was a known killer. The circus went into each new town with its posters, and the towns already knew.

And then towns started saying, do not bring that elephant here.

That was the thing that sealed it, friend. Not the law. There was no law that said an elephant had to die for killing a man. It was money. The towns along the route sent word, the accounts say, that if the Sparks show came with Mary, they would not let the show in. Or folks would not come. And a circus that cannot play is a circus that cannot pay its hands or feed its animals. Charlie Sparks had a whole show to keep alive.

So Charlie Sparks decided Mary would have to die.

And then he had a problem, friend. How do you kill an elephant?

You could not shoot her. They had tried that. Pistol bullets would not do it, and nobody had a big enough gun handy. To kill an elephant with a gun, friend, you need the kind of heavy rifle that big game hunters carried in Africa and India, a gun that could knock down a wall, and a man who knew exactly where to put the bullet. There was no such gun in Unicoi County, and no such man. There was talk of other ways, the accounts say. Talk of electrocuting her. Talk of running two locomotives in opposite directions with her chained between them, which I will not describe further, and thank God nobody did it.

And then somebody thought of the railroad.

Down the line from Kingsport was the town of Erwin. And Erwin was a railroad town. It was the shop town for a railroad called the Clinchfield, which ran up through those mountains carrying coal. And in the rail yard in Erwin, there was a derrick car. Now, the Clinchfield was something, friend. It had been built not many years before, through some of the roughest mountains in the East, from the coalfields of Virginia down through Tennessee and into the Carolinas, with more tunnels and high bridges than a man could count. It was a coal railroad, built to haul coal, and Erwin was where it kept its shops, where the engines were fixed and the cars were mended. A good part of the men in Erwin worked for the railroad one way or another. And a railroad that runs through mountains like that has wrecks. That is why they kept a wrecking crane. A big steam crane, mounted on a railroad car, built to lift a derailed locomotive or a wrecked boxcar back up onto the track. The accounts say it was rated to lift a hundred tons.

A hundred tons, friend. More than enough to lift five.

So on the thirteenth of September, nineteen sixteen, the day after Red Eldridge died, the circus brought Mary to Erwin.

And the people came.

It had gotten around, the way things did, that the elephant was going to be hanged. And the accounts say something like twenty-five hundred people came to the rail yard to watch. Some say more. Men off their shifts at the railroad shop. Farmers in from the hills. Women. And children. A great many children. The old man who stood at my counter was one of them.

And the way it has been told, the circus brought the other elephants down too, and kept them nearby. Some say so the crowd could see them. Some say it was to keep Mary calm, because elephants are herd animals and she would go easier with her own kind near.

They led Mary under the derrick. They put a chain around her neck. A railroad man ran the crane. And they began to lift.

I have always wondered about that railroad man, friend. The one at the controls of the crane. He was not a circus man. He was not a hangman. He was a fellow who went to work that morning expecting to fix a boxcar, and somebody told him he was going to lift an elephant by the neck in front of half the county. I do not know his name for certain, and I will not guess at it. But I would bet you he went home that night and did not eat much supper. Now here is where the story turns, and I want to give you the other side, the way I always try to.

In nineteen sixteen, nobody in that crowd thought they were doing anything wrong. That is the plain truth. A man had been killed, in front of the whole town of Kingsport. Killed by an animal. And in those days, if a dog killed a man, or a bull gored a farmer, the animal was put down. Everybody knew that. That was not cruelty. That was common sense. An animal that has killed once might kill again, and there were children around.

And an elephant was not a pet. It was a wild animal, five tons, that could kill a man as easy as you or I could step on a beetle. The circus people knew that. That is why they carried the hooks.

So a good many folks, then and since, have said, what else were they supposed to do? Turn her loose? Keep her on the road and hope she never did it again? Put her in a zoo, when there was no zoo within a thousand miles that could take her?
