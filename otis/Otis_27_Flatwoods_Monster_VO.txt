On a Friday evening in September, nineteen fifty-two, a group of boys were playing in a schoolyard in a little town in the middle of West Virginia, when something came across the sky.

A bright light. Moving fast, low, trailing fire. And it came down, the boys said, right on top of a hill on a farm at the edge of town. And it stayed there, glowing.

So they ran to get somebody. And a woman came with them, and a young man from the National Guard, and a dog. And seven of them climbed that hill in the dark, with one flashlight, to see what had come down out of the sky.

They smelled it before they saw it. A sharp, burning, metal smell, in a mist that stung their eyes and their throats. And at the top of the hill, there was a big round thing, pulsing with light. And off to one side, under a tree, the young man raised his flashlight.

And something looked back at them.

Something taller than a man. Taller than two men, some of them said. With a face that glowed, and eyes that shone, and a great dark hood rising up behind its head like the ace of spades. And it made a hissing sound. And it started to move toward them.

They ran. Every one of them. And by morning, newspapers all over the country were writing about the monster of Braxton County.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I remember that fall, friend. I was a young man then, behind the counter, and I remember the arguing.

For weeks, every man who came into my store had an opinion about the Flatwoods monster. Some of them laughed at it. Some of them were half scared of it. One old fellow swore it was a Russian spy in a suit, sent to scare us. A preacher told me it was a sign of the last days. And a young fellow who had been in the Air Force said it was a weather balloon, and everybody told him to hush, because nobody wanted it to be a weather balloon.

And I noticed something, listening to all of it. Every one of those men, whatever they said, glanced up at the sky on the way out to their trucks. Every one.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to take you up that hill with those seven people and that dog, step by step, the way they told it. And then I am going to take you back down, and tell you everything that happened after. The newspapermen. The television. The fellow from Braxton County who made a living out of it the rest of his life.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you what I believe those people saw on that hill. And I will tell you right now, I believe they saw something. I have never once thought they were lying. But I believe the real monster that fall was a good deal bigger than anything on a farm in Braxton County.

Let me start with the summer.

You have to understand what nineteen fifty-two was like, friend. The big war had been over for seven years. And a new one had come right behind it. Not a shooting war, mostly. A cold one. The Russians had the atomic bomb now. They had set off their first one in nineteen forty-nine. And every American knew that somewhere over the North Pole, there might be Russian bombers coming. Schoolchildren were practicing hiding under their desks. There was a cartoon they showed in the schools, friend, a turtle named Bert, who would pull his head into his shell when the bomb came, and the children were supposed to duck and cover the same way. Duck and cover. Every child in America knew it. My own customers' children came into the store singing that little song. Folks were building bomb shelters in their back yards. And American boys were fighting and dying in Korea that very year.

And folks were watching the sky.

Five years before, in nineteen forty-seven, a pilot out west had reported seeing a string of strange shining objects flying over the mountains in Washington State, faster than any airplane. And he said they moved like saucers skipping across water. And the newspapers called them flying saucers. And after that, friend, folks saw flying saucers everywhere. That same summer, out in New Mexico, the Army put out a story that it had recovered a crashed flying disc on a ranch near a town called Roswell, and then the next day took it back and said it was a weather balloon. Folks have been arguing about that one ever since.

And the picture shows got hold of it too. In nineteen fifty-one, there was a picture called The Day the Earth Stood Still, about a spaceman who lands his saucer in Washington with a great tall silent robot. And the year after Flatwoods, there was The War of the Worlds, with Martian machines burning up cities. Folks in little towns all over these mountains went to those pictures on Saturday night. I went. And I will tell you, friend, you came out of that picture house and looked up at the stars in a different way than you had going in.

And in the summer of nineteen fifty-two, it got bigger than it had ever been. In July, two weekends in a row, the radar operators at the airports around Washington, D.C., saw strange blips on their screens, moving over the capital itself, over the White House. Pilots in the air saw lights. The Air Force sent jet fighters up to chase them. The jets saw lights and could not catch them. It was in every newspaper in the country. The Air Force held the biggest press conference it had held since the war, to tell everybody it was nothing to worry about. Most folks did not believe them. The Air Force had a whole program by then, friend, with a name you may have heard. Project Blue Book. They collected reports of strange things in the sky from all over the country, thousands of them over the years, and sent men out to look into some of them. And most of what they found, they said, was airplanes and stars and weather balloons and the planet Venus. But some of them, they said themselves, they could not explain.

That was the summer, friend. The summer the government itself was chasing lights over the White House. And two months later, a light came down on a hill in Braxton County, West Virginia.

Hold on to that, friend. It is the whole key to the story.

Now the town.

Flatwoods is a little place, right about in the middle of West Virginia, in Braxton County. Farm country and hill country, a few houses and stores and a school, a long way from any city. The kind of town where everybody knows everybody, and most nights the biggest thing that happens is a car going by. The county seat is a little town called Sutton, on the Elk River, a few miles off. Folks farmed, and cut timber, and worked in the small mines and the gas fields. On a Friday night in September, the young folks might go into Sutton to the picture show, and the older folks sat on their porches and listened to the crickets. Nobody in Flatwoods, friend, had ever expected anything from the sky but rain.

And on the evening of the twelfth of September, nineteen fifty-two, a Friday, a little after seven o'clock, some boys were out on the playground at the school. Among them were two brothers, Eddie and Fred May, who were about twelve and thirteen. And a friend of theirs named Tommy Hyer.

And they saw the light in the sky. They said it came over, bright, and seemed to slow down, and came down on top of a hill on a farm that belonged to a man named Bailey Fisher. And it glowed there on the hilltop.

Now, you or I might have gone home and told our mother. Which is exactly what those boys did. They ran to the May house and told their mother, Kathleen May, that a flying saucer had landed on the Fisher farm.

Kathleen May was a beautician. A sensible woman, by every account. And I expect her first thought was that the boys had seen a shooting star. But they were so worked up, and so sure, that she said she would go and look.

And a few more boys came along. A boy named Neil Nunley, about fourteen. Another named Ronnie Shaver. And a young man named Eugene Lemon, seventeen years old, who was in the West Virginia National Guard, and who brought his flashlight. And Eugene Lemon brought his dog.

Seven people and a dog, friend. And they started up the hill in the dark. A beautician. A seventeen-year-old soldier. Five boys between about ten and fourteen years old. One flashlight. And not one of them, I would bet you, had any idea what they would do if they found something.

Now the climb.

It was getting dark. The hill was steep, and there was a fence, and brush, and tall grass. And as they climbed, the dog ran on ahead of them, up into the dark. And they heard it start barking, up ahead, and then the barking stopped. And the dog came running back down past them, with its tail between its legs. Running away.

Hold on to that dog, friend. Dogs do not make up stories.

And as they got near the top, a mist came over them. A thin, low mist, lying on the ground. And it had a smell. A terrible smell, they said. Sharp. Like burning metal. Like something hot and sulfurous. It stung their eyes and burned their noses and throats. Some of them said it made them sick to their stomachs.

And at the top of the hill, off to one side, they saw a glow. Something big, and round, pulsing with a light that went bright and dim, bright and dim. Some of them said it was red. Some said it was like a ball of fire. Some said it was as big as a house.

And then, off to the left, under a big oak tree, two of them saw what they thought were eyes. A pair of shining points of light, up high, higher than a man's head. And Eugene Lemon swung his flashlight around and pointed it right at them.

And here is what they said they saw, friend. All of them who looked, more or less the same thing.

A figure. Very tall. Ten feet tall, some of them said, though I expect in the dark and in the fear that was hard to judge. It had a face, round and red, or glowing, with those two eyes shining out of it. And behind its head, rising up over it, was a great dark shape, pointed at the top, like a hood, or a cowl, or the ace of spades on a playing card. Its body was dark, maybe dark green, and below it seemed to be something like a skirt, or folds, and some of them said it did not seem to have legs at all. It seemed to float.

And it made a sound. A hissing sound. Some of them said a high hiss, like steam.

And it came toward them. Not walking. Gliding. Slowly, out from under that tree, toward the light.

And Eugene Lemon, the National Guard boy, the accounts say, dropped his flashlight, or fell over backward. And all of them turned and ran. Down that hill, through the brush, over the fence, in the dark, as fast as they could go. And they did not stop until they got back to the May house.

Now the night.

When they got back to town, the accounts say, they were in terrible shape. Scared nearly out of their minds. And some of them were sick. Eugene Lemon, the accounts say, was vomiting much of that night. Some of the boys had swollen, sore throats, and their noses and eyes were running, for days afterward, as if they had breathed in something they should not have.

And somebody called the sheriff. The sheriff of Braxton County, a man named Robert Carr, and a deputy, were off on another call, the accounts say, following up on a report of a plane crash somewhere in the county, because folks had seen the same bright light come down and thought it was an airplane. And when they got back, they went up on that hill. And they found nothing. Nothing on the hill, nothing under the tree.

But the editor of the local newspaper went up there too. A young man named A. Lee Stewart. And he went up that same night with Eugene Lemon, and later he said that he could still smell that smell, in the grass. And the next morning, when he went back, he said he found two tracks, or marks, in the grass where something had been, as if something heavy had been dragged, or had sat there. And he said there was an oily, gummy stuff on the ground.

Hold on to that smell, friend. Every one of them mentioned it. The editor mentioned it. It is the one thing in this whole story that does not change from telling to telling.

Now the newspapers.

Within a day or two, friend, the story of the Flatwoods monster was in newspapers all over the United States. Reporters came from the cities. And a television show up in New York City, the accounts say, brought Kathleen May and Eugene Lemon up to tell their story in front of the cameras. A beautician and a National Guard boy from Braxton County, West Virginia, on network television, in nineteen fifty-two, when most folks in Braxton County did not have a television set.

And an artist made a drawing of the monster for the show, the accounts say, and it was a good deal more frightening than what they had described. He gave it claws, and a long flowing skirt, and made it look like something out of a horror picture. And that drawing is the picture most folks have had in their heads ever since. Kathleen May, the accounts say, did not think it looked much like what she had seen at all.

And here is something interesting, friend. That same bright light in the sky, that the boys saw come down, was seen by other people that night too. A good many of them. Not just in Flatwoods. In Maryland and Pennsylvania and other parts of West Virginia. Newspapers in other places reported a bright fireball streaking across the sky that same evening. And the general opinion of the folks who know about such things was that it had been a meteor. A shooting star, a big bright one, burning up as it came through the air, the kind that looks like it lands just over the next hill, though it is really miles and miles away, or burns up long before it gets to the ground.

So that part of it, friend, a great many people saw. The light was real. The question was what was on the hill.

Now I want to tell you about a man from Braxton County who did more with the Flatwoods monster than anybody.

His name was Gray Barker. He grew up right there, in Braxton County, in a little place not far from Flatwoods. And he was a theater manager, and a writer. And when the monster story broke, he went out and talked to everybody. Kathleen May, the boys, Eugene Lemon, the editor. He wrote it up for a magazine that printed stories about strange things. And that started him on a whole life of it. He became one of the best-known writers about flying saucers in this country. He wrote books. And in one of them, a few years later, he wrote about men in dark suits who would come to visit people who had seen flying saucers, and warn them to keep quiet.

Men in Black, friend. That idea, the strange men in dark suits who show up after a sighting, a great deal of it goes back to Gray Barker of Braxton County. And you may remember, if you heard me tell about the Mothman over in Point Pleasant, that the men in dark suits showed up in that story too, years later. It is all one long thread, friend, running through these mountains. And it started on a hill in Flatwoods.

I will tell you plainly, though. A good many folks who knew Gray Barker said he loved a good story more than he loved the truth, and that he had a sense of humor about it all that his readers did not always share. Some said he made up a good part of what he wrote. I cannot tell you which parts.

Now here is where the story turns, friend. Because I have told it to you the way the folks on that hill told it. And now I want to give you the other side, the plain side, the way a good many careful people have looked at it since.

There are folks who have gone back over every detail of that night, and here is what they believe.

The light in the sky was the meteor. The one seen over three states. It did not land on the Fisher farm at all. It only looked like it did, from a schoolyard, to some excited boys. I have seen a big meteor myself, friend, once in my life, a fireball, coming home late from Bluefield on a fall night. It came across the sky green and white, with a tail of sparks behind it, and it went down behind a ridge not a mile from the road, I would have sworn. I nearly pulled over to go look for it. And I read in the paper the next week that folks had seen the same thing in Ohio and Kentucky, and that it had probably burned up somewhere over the ocean. A meteor always lands just over the next hill, friend. That is what makes them so convincing.

The pulsing red light on the hilltop, the round glowing thing, was most likely an airplane beacon. There were beacons on hills in that part of the country in those days, lights that flashed red, to warn airplanes away from the hills at night. And folks have said there were beacons in sight of that hill.

And the creature under the tree, friend. The tall shape with the shining eyes and the hood like the ace of spades, that hissed and seemed to glide.

They say it was an owl.

A barn owl, sitting up on a branch of that oak tree. A barn owl has a big round pale face, shaped like a heart, that shines white when a light hits it. Its eyes shine in a flashlight beam. When it is frightened, it hunches up and spreads itself out, so the shape of its head and shoulders can look something like a hood. And a barn owl, friend, when it is scared or angry, does not hoot. It hisses. A loud, long, rasping hiss, like steam.

And if you are a frightened boy on a dark hill, in a stinging mist, and somebody swings a flashlight up into a tree and catches a barn owl, eyes shining, face glowing, hunched up and hissing at you, with the leaves and branches of the tree behind it making a great dark shape, and the flashlight beam jumping around because the boy holding it is shaking, then you might very well see a ten-foot monster. And when it flies down at you, which an owl will do, you might think it is gliding toward you with no legs.

And as for being sick, they say that seven people who climbed a steep hill in the dark, and got the scare of their lives, and ran down that hill as hard as they could go, and maybe breathed in some smoke or some ground fog full of something from a field, might well throw up, and have sore throats, without any monster at all. Fear will do that.

I take all of that seriously, friend. I do. I have heard a barn owl hiss in the dark myself, at the back of my store, at night, and it raised every hair on my neck, and I knew perfectly well what it was.

There was a farmer who traded with me who had a barn owl living up in the loft of his barn for years. And he told me that the first time it came at him, in the dark, when he climbed up there with a lantern, he fell halfway down the ladder. He said, Otis, that bird spread out its wings and came at my face, white as a ghost, hissing like a busted steam pipe, and I'd have sworn on the Bible it was six feet across. And then he laughed and said, it weighs about as much as a can of beans.

He said, I'll tell you something. If I'd had a flying saucer in my head when I climbed that ladder, I'd still be telling people about the monster in my barn.

But I will tell you the one thing the plain answer has never quite answered for me.

The smell.

Every one of them talked about it. The newspaper editor smelled it, hours later, when nobody was scared anymore. A burning, metal, sickening smell. And an owl does not smell like that, friend. A meteor that burned up miles away does not leave a smell on a hillside. An airplane beacon does not smell like anything. Something on that hill that night was burning, or had burned, or had left something behind. I do not know what. Nobody does. But that smell is the one piece of the Flatwoods story that I have never heard anybody explain to my satisfaction.

Now I promised you the old stories, and there are some good ones.

Folks said that the monster was seen again, here and there, in the weeks after, around Braxton County and the counties next to it. A woman and her daughter, by one account, said they had seen something like it on a road some days before, and smelled the same smell. I give you those as they were given to me. Once a story like that gets loose, friend, everybody in three counties sees something.

Folks said the dog died. That Eugene Lemon's dog, the one that ran down the hill with its tail between its legs, got sick and died not long after. I have heard that told, and I have heard it told the other way, that the dog was fine. I will not choose.

Folks said Kathleen May never changed her story. That one I believe. She told it the same way, plain and steady, for the rest of her life, whether people laughed at her or not. And a good many people laughed at her, friend. A woman from a little mountain town on television, talking about a monster. You can imagine what some of the city papers did with that. She did not care. She said what she saw. She lived a long life, by the accounts, there in West Virginia. And folks who knew her said she would talk about it if you asked, and would not if you did not, and never once tried to make money off of it or make it bigger than it was. I have always respected that about her, friend. There are a good many people in this world who would have.

And folks said, and this is the one that has lasted, that Braxton County never got rid of its monster. That children there grew up with it the way other children grow up with the boogeyman. Do not go up on the Fisher farm hill at night, or the Braxton County monster will get you. I have heard grown men from that county say it to their grandchildren with a straight face.

So I promised you I would tell you what I believe those people saw on that hill.

Here is where I come down, friend.

I believe the light in the sky was a meteor. A big bright one, that a great many people saw. I believe the boys saw it come down behind the hill and thought it had landed. Anybody would have.

I believe the round pulsing light on the hilltop was something ordinary. A beacon, or something burning, I could not say.

And I believe, though I would not say it to Kathleen May, God rest her, that the thing under the tree was most likely a barn owl. Caught in a flashlight beam, hunched up and hissing, on the worst night it ever had.

But I do not believe for one minute those people were lying, or foolish. They were frightened. Truly frightened. And I want you to think about why.

It was nineteen fifty-two, friend. The summer the radar screens over Washington lit up with things nobody could explain, and the Air Force sent up jets to chase them. The Russians had the bomb. American boys were dying in Korea. The newspapers were full of flying saucers every week. Every grown person in this country, and every child, had been taught, for years, to watch the sky and be afraid of what might come out of it.

So when a light came down out of that sky, onto a hill in Braxton County, a mother and six boys and a National Guardsman climbed up to meet it with all of that in their heads. Every newspaper headline. Every air raid drill. Every picture show about invaders from space. And when the flashlight caught two shining eyes in a tree, they saw what the whole country had been waiting, all that summer, to see.

That is the real monster in the Flatwoods story, friend, the way I see it. Not something from another world. Something from this one. A whole country, scared to death of the sky.

And there is one thing more. Whatever it was, those people walked up that hill. In the dark. Toward it. With one flashlight and a dog. Because a boy said something had come down, and somebody ought to go see if anybody needed help.

That is the part of the Flatwoods story I always tell first, when folks ask me. Not the monster. The walking up.

That's as far as the story goes, friend, and about as far as I'll take you tonight.
