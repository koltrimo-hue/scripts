In the first gray light of New Year's morning, nineteen fifty-three, a powder-blue Cadillac convertible pulled into a gas station in a little town in the mountains of West Virginia.

The young man driving it was seventeen years old. He had been driving most of the night, through ice and sleet, over the mountains from Tennessee. And in the back seat, under a blanket, his passenger had been asleep for hours. Or so the young man thought.

The young man got out. He looked in the back seat. He reached back and touched his passenger's hand.

And it was cold.

The man in the back seat was twenty-nine years old. He was the biggest star in country music. He had written songs that every person in America knew by heart. Songs that are still sung in honky-tonks and churches and kitchens all over the world to this day. And he had died, sometime in the dark, somewhere on those mountain roads, alone, in the back of his own Cadillac.

His name was Hank Williams.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I heard it on the radio, friend. New Year's Day, nineteen fifty-three. I had the store open for half a day, the way I did on holidays, for folks who had run out of something. And I had the radio going behind the counter. And the music stopped, and a man came on and said Hank Williams was dead.

There were three or four people in the store. And nobody said anything. A woman put her hand over her mouth. And a young fellow, a coal miner's boy, maybe nineteen, who had been leaning on the drink box, turned around and walked out without buying anything. And I saw him standing out by the gas pump in the cold with his back to the window for a long time.

I want you to understand what Hank Williams meant to folks like that, friend. He was not a star the way movie stars were stars. He was one of us. A poor boy from the country, who sang about being lonesome and broke and heartsick and drunk and sorry, and about the Lord, all in the same voice. Every man and woman in those hills who had ever been lonesome heard him and thought, he knows. He has been where I am.

And he died in the back of a car in West Virginia, on a mountain road, a couple of hours' drive from my store.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to take you on that last ride. Mile by mile, town by town, from Alabama to that gas station in Oak Hill. And at every stop along the way, I am going to tell you a piece of the life of the man in the back seat.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you what killed Hank Williams. Not what the doctors wrote down. I will give you that too. But what I believe killed him. And I am going to tell you about the last song of his that came out while he was alive, and the name of it. Because I do not believe there has ever been a stranger name for a last song.

Let me start in Montgomery.

On the thirtieth of December, nineteen fifty-two, Hank Williams was at home in Montgomery, Alabama. And he had shows to play. One on New Year's Eve, in Charleston, West Virginia. And one on New Year's Day, in Canton, Ohio. Big shows. Good money. And he needed the money, friend, because by the end of nineteen fifty-two, Hank Williams was in a bad way.

He was too sick to drive himself. So he hired a driver. A young man named Charles Carr, seventeen years old, a college boy home for Christmas, whose daddy ran a taxi company in Montgomery. And the plan was for young Charles Carr to drive Hank Williams in his Cadillac, a beautiful powder-blue convertible, all the way to West Virginia and Ohio, and get him to his shows.

Now, before we leave Montgomery, let me tell you where that man in the back seat came from.

He was born in September of nineteen twenty-three, in a little place in south Alabama called Mount Olive. His name was Hiram, Hiram King Williams, though nobody ever called him that for long. His daddy worked on the railroad and in the logging camps, and got sick, and spent most of Hank's boyhood in a veterans' hospital, far away. His mama, a big strong woman named Lillie, raised the children mostly by herself, in the hard years, taking in boarders and doing whatever it took.

And Hank was born with something wrong with his back. A defect in his spine, that the doctors call spina bifida. A mild form of it, that most folks would never have known was there. But it gave him pain. Back pain, all his life, that got worse as he got older. Hold on to that pain, friend. It is the first thing that killed him, though it took twenty-nine years.

And when he was a boy, selling peanuts and shining shoes on the streets of a little town called Georgiana, Alabama, he met a man who changed his life. A Black street singer named Rufus Payne. Folks called him Tee-Tot. And Tee-Tot played the guitar on the street corners for nickels, and sang the blues. And the little white boy with the bad back followed him around and begged him to teach him. And Tee-Tot did. The accounts say Hank paid him in nickels and dimes and food his mama sent along.

Hank Williams said, later in his life, that everything he knew about music, he learned from Tee-Tot. I have always wanted folks to remember that, friend. The greatest white country singer who ever lived learned it from a Black man on a street corner in Alabama.

Now the road north.

Charles Carr and Hank Williams left Montgomery and headed north, toward Tennessee. And it was winter, and the weather was terrible. Ice and sleet and snow all across the South that week. They stopped in Birmingham, and in Chattanooga, and they kept going.

And let me tell you about the man in the back seat in his best years.

By the time he was a teenager, Hank Williams had his own show on the radio in Montgomery. He had a band, the Drifting Cowboys. He played every honky-tonk and schoolhouse and barn dance in south Alabama. And he drank. He started young, the accounts say, and he never really stopped. The drinking and the back pain went together, friend, from the very beginning. Whiskey was the only medicine a poor country boy could get.

He married a woman named Audrey Sheppard, in nineteen forty-four. She wanted to be a singer too. She pushed him. They fought like cats and dogs. They broke up and made up and broke up again. And a great many of the most heartbroken songs Hank Williams ever wrote, the accounts say, were written about Audrey. They had a boy together, in nineteen forty-nine. Randall Hank. Folks called him Bocephus. He grew up to be Hank Williams Junior. And that little boy grew up and became a big star himself, years later, singing about his daddy and about the family tradition of drinking and hard living. I have sold a good many of his records too.

And before the Opry, friend, there was a man in Nashville named Fred Rose. A songwriter and a music publisher, a city fellow who knew the business. And in nineteen forty-six, Hank and Audrey went to Nashville and found Fred Rose and asked him to listen to Hank's songs. And the story that has been told is that Fred Rose did not believe a country boy like that could have written them himself. So he tested him. He told Hank to go into a room and write a song, right then, on a subject Fred gave him. And Hank came back out in a little while with a song. And Fred Rose believed him after that, and signed him, and worked with him for the rest of his life, helping him polish the songs and get them recorded.

I cannot swear to every word of that story, friend. But I believe the heart of it. Hank Williams could write a song the way other men could split wood.

And in nineteen forty-nine, Hank Williams got his chance. The Grand Ole Opry, in Nashville. The biggest stage in country music. The show that every family in the South listened to on the radio on Saturday night. It came out of an old red brick church building in downtown Nashville called the Ryman Auditorium, with hard wooden pews, where the audience fanned themselves with paper fans in the summer. Folks called it the mother church of country music. To stand on that stage, friend, for a country singer, was like a ballplayer walking out onto the field at Yankee Stadium. And the story that has been told a thousand times, and I believe it, is that on his first night on the Opry, in June of nineteen forty-nine, he sang a song called Lovesick Blues, and the crowd would not let him off the stage. They called him back, and called him back, and called him back. Six times, by most accounts. Nobody had ever seen anything like it.

From that night on, friend, he was the biggest star in country music.

And over the next three years, he wrote and sang the songs that every one of us knows. I will just say the names, and I expect you can hear them. Your Cheatin' Heart. Hey, Good Lookin'. Cold, Cold Heart. Jambalaya. I'm So Lonesome I Could Cry. I Saw the Light.

I'm So Lonesome I Could Cry, friend. I have heard folks say it is the saddest song ever written in this country. I will not argue with them.

And his songs went past country music too. A pop singer from New York named Tony Bennett recorded Cold, Cold Heart, in nineteen fifty-one, with a big orchestra behind him, and it went to number one on the pop charts. Folks in the cities who would never have listened to a hillbilly singer on the Opry were humming Hank Williams songs without knowing it. Other singers did the same with Jambalaya and Hey, Good Lookin'. Hank used to say, the accounts tell, that he liked hearing the money come in from those city singers just fine.

And he had another side, friend, that a good many folks did not know about. He recorded a set of records under a different name. Luke the Drifter, he called himself. And on those records, he did not sing so much as talk. Little sermons, little stories, recited over a slow guitar. About the poor, and the lonely, and men who had done wrong. Hymns of a kind. He did not want them sold as Hank Williams records, the story goes, because he did not want the jukebox operators to think he had gone soft and stop buying his dance records. So he made up Luke the Drifter. And I have always thought Luke was closer to the real man than Hank was.

Now Knoxville.

On the thirty-first of December, New Year's Eve, Charles Carr and Hank Williams got to Knoxville, Tennessee. And the weather was so bad, the accounts say, with ice on the roads all through the mountains, that there was no way they were going to drive to Charleston, West Virginia, in time for the New Year's Eve show. They tried to fly out of Knoxville, and the plane turned back because of the weather. The Charleston show was canceled.

So now they had to get to Canton, Ohio, for the New Year's Day show. And the only way was to drive. All night. Through the mountains. In the ice.

They checked into a hotel in Knoxville, the Andrew Johnson Hotel, to rest a few hours before the long drive. And Hank Williams was sick. Hurting. Hiccupping, the accounts say, and could not stop. And a doctor was called to the hotel. And the doctor gave him shots. Vitamins, the accounts say, and morphine. Morphine, friend, for a man who had been drinking.

And late that night, around eleven o'clock, the hotel porters had to carry Hank Williams down and put him in the back seat of the Cadillac. He could not walk on his own. And the accounts say one of the porters noticed that he did not seem to be awake. But they laid him down back there, with a blanket, and Charles Carr got behind the wheel, and they drove off into the night.

Now let me tell you about the last year of that man's life, friend, because it was not a happy one.

By nineteen fifty-two, it was all coming apart. His back had gotten so bad that he had an operation on it, at the end of nineteen fifty-one, and it did not help much, and he was in pain all the time. He drank more. He took pills. A man who called himself a doctor, and was not one, the accounts say, gave him a drug called chloral hydrate, a powerful sedative, to help him sleep, and he took a great deal of it. Audrey divorced him. He missed shows. He showed up drunk to shows. And in August of nineteen fifty-two, the Grand Ole Opry fired him.

The Grand Ole Opry, friend. Fired Hank Williams. The biggest star they ever had. For being too drunk to count on.

He went home to Alabama and down to Louisiana and played on another big radio show down there, the Louisiana Hayride, where he had played on his way up.

And I want to tell you about one tour he went on, back in his good years, because it came right through stores like mine. There was a patent medicine in those days called Hadacol. A tonic, a dark sweet syrup in a bottle, that was supposed to cure just about anything that ailed you. Tired blood. Bad nerves. Whatever you had. I sold it, friend. Every store in the South sold it. And a good part of what made folks feel better after a spoonful, the accounts say, was that it was something like twelve parts in a hundred alcohol.

And the man who made Hadacol put together a traveling show, the Hadacol Caravan, with movie stars and comedians and singers, going from town to town across the South in nineteen fifty-one, and folks got in by bringing a Hadacol box top. And Hank Williams was the star of it. Bob Hope was on some of those shows, the accounts say, and the stories go that Hank was the one the crowds came for.

I always found that a little sad, friend, looking back. A man with a drinking problem, headlining a traveling show for a medicine that was mostly liquor. He married again, a young woman named Billie Jean, in the fall of nineteen fifty-two. They had the wedding in New Orleans, on a stage, in front of a paying crowd. Twice, the accounts say, so they could sell tickets to both. And there was another woman, too, who was carrying his child that winter. The baby, a little girl, would be born just a few days after he died.

He was twenty-nine years old, friend. And he looked sixty. I have seen the pictures from that last year. A thin, worn man in a white cowboy hat and a fine suit, with eyes that looked like they had seen the end of something.

Now the long road east, through the night.

Charles Carr drove out of Knoxville and up into the mountains of East Tennessee, headed for Virginia. And somewhere along there, the accounts say, a highway patrolman stopped him for driving too fast. And the patrolman looked into the back seat, the story goes, at the man under the blanket, and remarked that he did not look well. Some say the patrolman said he looked dead. The young driver said he was Hank Williams, and he was asleep, and he had been given something to help him sleep. And the patrolman, the accounts say, wrote the ticket and let them go.

I do not know if Hank Williams was alive then, friend. Nobody does. It may be that he died before they ever left Tennessee.

Then on into Virginia. And they came through Bristol. Bristol, friend, the town where the state line runs down the middle of the main street. The town where the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers made their first records, twenty-five years before, in a room above a hat company, and started the whole thing that Hank Williams became the king of. And the accounts say Charles Carr stopped there, at a little all-night diner, and went to get something to eat, and asked Hank in the back seat if he wanted anything. And Hank, the story goes, said no. That he did not want anything.

That may have been the last thing he ever said, friend. I do not want anything. In Bristol. The birthplace of country music.

I cannot swear to that. Some accounts say he spoke later. Some say he never spoke at all after Knoxville. But I have always believed it was Bristol, because if the Lord was writing it, that is where he would have put it.

Then north, over the mountains, into West Virginia. Through the dark and the ice, up through Bluefield, where the young man, worn out from driving, the accounts say, hired another man to spell him at the wheel for a while. And on up the long road through the coal country, through towns like the ones up and down from my store. Little mining towns, dark and asleep on New Year's Eve, with the coal tipples standing up against the sky and the creeks running beside the road.

I know those roads, friend. I have driven them a good many times. In nineteen fifty-three, there were no interstate highways through those mountains. The road was two lanes, winding up one side of a mountain and down the other, through every little town, around curve after curve, with the creek on one side and a rock wall on the other, and coal trucks coming at you in the dark. In ice and sleet, at night, a man driving that road had to hold the wheel with both hands and not take his eyes off the road for a second. That is what that young man was doing. Holding on, through the mountains, all night. He had no reason in the world to turn around and look at the back seat.

And somewhere in there, in the dark, in the cold, in the back seat of a powder-blue Cadillac, Hank Williams died.

And nobody knew. The driver kept driving. The car kept going. The mountains went by. And the man who had written I'm So Lonesome I Could Cry was alone in the back seat, the most lonesome a man can be.

Now Oak Hill.

In the early morning of New Year's Day, nineteen fifty-three, the car pulled into a gas station in the town of Oak Hill, West Virginia. And Charles Carr, the accounts say, had begun to worry. Hank had been too still for too long. And he looked in the back seat, and reached back, and touched him. And he was cold. And his body was stiff.

They took him to the hospital there in Oak Hill. And the doctor pronounced him dead.

The death certificate, friend, when it was written up, gave the causes as a heart condition and alcohol. Something wrong with the right side of his heart. And acute alcoholism. That is what the doctors wrote down.

Hank Williams was twenty-nine years old.

Up in Canton, Ohio, that afternoon, there was a crowd in the auditorium, thousands of people, waiting for the show. And somebody came out on the stage and told them that Hank Williams was dead. And the story, and I have heard it told so many times I believe it, is that the crowd laughed at first. They thought it was a joke. They thought it was the kind of thing Hank might pull, after all the shows he had missed. And then they understood it was not a joke. And somebody started singing I Saw the Light. And the whole crowd sang it.

And some say a spotlight was put on the empty place at the microphone where he should have been standing, while they sang. I cannot tell you if that part is so, friend. But I hope it is.

They took him home to Montgomery. And they held the funeral on the fourth of January, in the city auditorium. And the accounts say there were something like twenty thousand people. Some say more. Inside the auditorium and filling up the streets all around it, people who had come from all over the South. Farm people. Factory people. Black and white. The biggest crowd Montgomery had ever seen, for anything. The great stars of the Opry came and sang over his coffin. The same Opry that had fired him four months before.

And Charles Carr, the young man who had been driving, went back to college, the accounts say, and lived out his life. And for the rest of it, folks asked him about that night. Over and over. Reporters and writers and fans, wanting to know what Hank said, and when, and what he looked like, and whether he knew. He told it as best he could. I have always felt for him, friend. He was seventeen. He took a job driving a famous man to a show, and he spent the rest of his days being the boy who was there.

And here is a strange thing, and I give it to you because it is true and it has always given me a chill. Hank's young widow, Billie Jean, married again, a few years later. She married another country singer, a tall fellow named Johnny Horton, who had big hits in the late fifties, songs about history, like one about the battle of New Orleans that every schoolchild knew. And in nineteen sixty, Johnny Horton was killed in a car wreck, coming home from a show. And the accounts say the last place Johnny Horton played was the same little club in Texas where Hank Williams played one of his last shows, back in December of nineteen fifty-two.

The same woman, friend. Two husbands. Both country singers. Both dead in automobiles, coming from the same stage. I do not know what to make of that, and I do not suppose anybody does.

And here, friend, is where I have to turn this story around a little. Because I have told it the way the fans tell it, and the fans loved him. Now I want to give you the other side, as I always try to.

There are folks who will tell you that Hank Williams was no hero, and should not be made into one. That he was a drunk who let down his band and his wife and his fans, over and over. That he was mean when he drank, and he drank all the time. That he missed shows people had paid for, and showed up to others too drunk to stand. That he left a trail of hurt people behind him. And that the young man driving that car, the porters, the doctor, all the people around him, were caught up in the mess he made of his own life.

And all of that is true, friend. I will not pretend it is not. I knew men like Hank Williams, in my store, and they were hard to love up close. Their wives could tell you.

But I will tell you what else is true. He was in pain, every day of his grown life, from a back that God gave him crooked. He was a poor boy who never had any medicine but whiskey, until he got rich enough to have worse medicine than whiskey, from men who called themselves doctors and were not. He was twenty-nine. A boy, really. And everybody around him, the managers, the record people, the show promoters, wanted one more show out of him, one more record, one more drive through an ice storm to Ohio. Nobody made him stop. Nobody could have, maybe. But nobody much tried.

Now I promised you the old stories, and with Hank, there are a thousand of them, and I will give you just a few.

Folks said that among his things in the car, there was a piece of paper with words on it. A song, or the start of one. His last song. I have heard different words given for it, and I do not know which, if any, are really his. So I will not give them to you. But I will tell you that it is just like Hank Williams to have died with a song in his pocket.

Folks said he knew he was going to die. That he told people, that last year, that he would not live much longer. I believe that, friend. I believe a man that sick knows.

Folks said that Hollywood would make a picture of his life, and they did, in nineteen sixty-four. They called it Your Cheatin' Heart, and a handsome young actor named George Hamilton played Hank, and when you heard Hank singing in that picture, it was not Hank and it was not the actor. It was his boy, Hank Junior, a teenager then, singing his daddy's songs for the soundtrack. I saw it at the picture house. And I will tell you, friend, when that boy's voice came out of the speakers, sounding so much like his father, there were grown men in that theater wiping their eyes.

And folks said, and this is the one I will leave you with, that his ghost rides in that Cadillac still. That on New Year's Eve, on the old highway through the mountains of West Virginia, people have seen a powder-blue Cadillac go by in the dark, with a young man at the wheel, and somebody under a blanket in the back. I have never seen it. I do not expect to. But I have thought about it, closing up my store on a cold New Year's Eve, and looking up the road.

So I promised you I would tell you what killed Hank Williams.

The doctors wrote down his heart and the alcohol. And I expect the doctors were right, as far as it goes. And there was the morphine in Knoxville, and the pills, and the chloral hydrate, all together in a body that had nothing left. Any one of those could have done it. All of them together surely did.

But here is where I come down, friend.

I believe what killed Hank Williams was the thing he sang about. Loneliness. A kind of lonesome that no amount of fame or money or women or whiskey could reach. He had more people loving him than any man in the country. Twenty thousand at his funeral. And he died alone, in the back seat of his own car, under a blanket, on a mountain road in the dark, with a seventeen-year-old boy who did not know he was gone, and nobody else.

He spent his whole life telling everybody exactly how lonesome he was. In every song. And everybody listened, and everybody loved him for it, and nobody really heard.

And I promised you the name of the last song of his that came out while he was alive.

It was on the radio that very winter. It was climbing the charts when he died, and after he died, it went all the way to the top. Folks were buying it in the record stores the week of his funeral.

It was called, I'll Never Get Out of This World Alive.

He wrote that, friend. He sang it. A few weeks before he got into that car.

I have always thought he knew exactly what he was singing about.

That's as far as the story goes, friend, and about as far as I'll take you tonight.
