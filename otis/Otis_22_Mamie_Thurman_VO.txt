On a hot morning in June of nineteen thirty-two, a boy was picking blackberries along a mountain road above a coal town in West Virginia.

He could not hear, and he could not speak. He had been deaf all his life. So he did not hear anything strange that morning. He just saw it, in the brush below the road, among the blackberry canes.

A woman's body. In a good dress. Her throat had been cut, from one ear to the other. And she had been shot, twice, in the head.

Her name was Mamie Thurman. She was the wife of a city policeman. She was well known in that town, and well liked, and she went to church. And she kept a little book. And in that book, the story goes, were the names of more than a dozen of the most powerful men in Logan County.

Within a few weeks, a man was arrested for her murder. And within a few months, a jury sent him to prison for the rest of his life. And for ninety years since, a great many people in Logan County have believed they sent the wrong man.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I have told you about Logan County before, friend. In the story of the mine wars, the march over Blair Mountain, and the sheriff named Don Chafin who ran that county like his own farm, with the coal companies paying him to do it. Logan County in those years was a place where a few men had a great deal of power, and everybody knew who they were, and nobody said their names too loud. Don Chafin himself was long gone by nineteen thirty-two. You may remember he went to federal prison for moonshining, of all things, and then moved off to Huntington and died rich. But the way of doing things that he built in Logan did not leave with him. A county that has been run by a few men for long enough, friend, keeps on being run that way, whoever is sitting in the sheriff's chair.

And when the Mamie Thurman story came across my counter, which it did more than once, it always came from somebody with kin in Logan. And it always came the same way. Low. With a look over the shoulder. Even forty and fifty years later. Because in Logan County, the story of Mamie Thurman was never really about who killed her. It was about who did not want anybody to find out.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to lay this out like a lawman would. I am going to give you the woman, and then I am going to give you the suspects, one at a time. Every one of them that folks in Logan have pointed at, over the years. And you can weigh them.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you who I believe killed Mamie Thurman. And I am going to tell you why I believe the man who died in prison for it did not.

Let me start with the woman.

Her name had been Mamie Morrison. She was born around nineteen hundred, and she came from a family over in Kentucky. And she married a man named Jack Thurman. And in nineteen twenty-four, the two of them moved to Logan, West Virginia, where Jack got a job as a patrolman on the city police.

Now, Logan in those years was a coal boom town. The mines were going full blast up every hollow in that county, and the money flowed down into the town of Logan, the county seat, where the bankers and the lawyers and the coal company men and the politicians lived. It had paved streets and fine houses and a big hotel and stores with plate glass windows. And it had a darker side too, the way boom towns do. There was money, and there was liquor, even though it was Prohibition, and there was gambling, and there were men who were used to getting whatever they wanted.

And by nineteen thirty-two, friend, the Depression had come down on Logan too. The coal market had collapsed. Mines were shutting down or working one or two days a week. Miners' families up the hollows were going hungry. But down in the town, the men at the top still had their money, and their power, and their politics. Logan County had a political machine in those years that folks all over the state talked about. They used to joke that Logan County could deliver however many votes a candidate needed, and that the dead voted in Logan as regular as the living. I cannot prove that. But I heard the joke a hundred times, and I never heard anybody from Logan deny it.

So that was the town where Mamie Thurman lived. Hungry hollows, and a courthouse that answered to a few men, and those few men drinking whiskey in a dry country and doing as they pleased.

Jack and Mamie Thurman did not have much money. A patrolman's pay was not much. So they rented a little apartment, two rooms, over a garage. And the garage was in the backyard of one of the finer houses in Logan. The house belonged to a man named Harry Robertson, and his wife, Louise.

Hold on to that garage, friend. A policeman and his wife, living over the garage of a rich man's house.

And Mamie Thurman, by every account, was something. Pretty. Dark-haired. Well dressed, better than a patrolman's wife might have been expected to be. She went to church. She was in the women's clubs. She was in the social life of that town, more than you would think a patrolman's wife would be. And there were whispers about her. There always are, about a woman like that, in a town like that.

Now the morning.

On the twenty-second of June, nineteen thirty-two, a young man named Garland Davis was out picking blackberries up on a mountain road above the little coal town of Holden, a few miles out of Logan. The road went up the side of a mountain to a mine, and the mountain was called Trace Mountain then, and folks have called that road the Twenty-Two Mine Road ever since.

And Garland Davis was deaf and could not speak. And he found Mamie Thurman's body in the brush below the road.

The accounts say her throat had been cut, ear to ear. And that she had been shot twice in the left side of the head. And that she had been dumped there, down the bank, as if somebody had driven up that road in the night, and opened a car door, and rolled her out.

And here is a thing I have always noticed, friend, and I want you to notice it too. The young man who found her could not tell anybody what he had seen. Not in words. He had to make them understand some other way. I have always thought there was something about that which fit this whole story. In Logan County, in nineteen thirty-two, the only one who saw anything was the one who could not say it.

And I want you to understand what a murder investigation was in a place like that, in those days. There was no state crime laboratory to speak of. There were no experts flying in from anywhere. The sheriff and his deputies and the city police did it, with whatever they had. Doctors could tell blood from paint, and could tell some kinds of blood apart, but not much more. Fingerprints were taken, sometimes. Mostly, friend, a case was built on who said what, and who was seen where, and whose car was where it should not have been. Which meant that a case was built on people. And in Logan County, people knew which way the wind blew.

Now the suspects.

The first suspect is the husband. Jack Thurman.

In a murder like that, friend, the law always looks first at the husband. That is just good sense. And Jack Thurman was a policeman, which meant he had a gun, and he knew how the law worked, and he would have known how to cover his tracks.

But the accounts say that on the night his wife was killed, Jack Thurman was on duty. Working his shift. With other policemen who could say where he was. And so the law, by most accounts, did not look at him long. Whether it should have looked longer, I will leave to you. But I will tell you that in all my years of hearing this story, I never once heard anybody from Logan say they believed Jack Thurman did it.

What I did hear, friend, was this. That Jack Thurman knew. Knew what his wife was doing, and who with. And kept his mouth shut, because the men she was doing it with were the men who signed his paychecks. That is not murder. But it is a hard thing to think about.

The second suspect is the man who owned the garage. Harry Robertson.

Harry Robertson was somebody in Logan, friend. The accounts say he worked at a bank, the National Bank of Logan. And he was a politician, the president of the city commission, which made him something like the head man of the town government. A banker and the head of the city. In a town like Logan in those years, there were not many men more powerful.

And when the police went over the Robertson property, the accounts say, they found blood. In the house, by some accounts. And in Harry Robertson's car.

And Harry Robertson, when they questioned him, admitted something. He admitted that he and Mamie Thurman had been carrying on. An affair. The man who owned the garage, and the policeman's wife who lived over it.

Hold on to that, friend. The banker admitted the affair. But he said he did not kill her.

And then the third suspect came into it. Because the police arrested two men. Harry Robertson. And a man who worked for him.

His name was Clarence Stephenson. And Clarence Stephenson was a Black man who worked for the Robertsons as a handyman. He did their chores, ran their errands, and by some accounts drove their car.

Now, I want you to understand something about Logan County and Black folks in those years, friend. A great many Black men had come up from the Deep South to the West Virginia coalfields in the years around the First World War, to work in the mines. The coal companies brought them, by the trainload. And in the mines, a Black man and a white man often did the same work for the same pay, which was more than could be said for most places in the South. Some of them came to the union side by side, in the mine wars. But up top, out of the mines, West Virginia was still a place with separate schools, and separate everything else, and a Black man who worked as a handyman in a white banker's house knew exactly how much his word was worth against that banker's.

Hold on to that, friend. It is the whole case.

And here is where the story goes the way stories like this went in nineteen thirty-two, friend.

The case the law built went something like this. That Harry Robertson's car had been used to carry Mamie Thurman's body up that mountain road. And that Clarence Stephenson had been the one to do it, or to help do it, or to do the killing itself. There was talk of his having been seen with the car. There was talk of things found. The accounts differ on the details, and I will not give you more than I can stand behind.

But here is how it came out. The charges against Harry Robertson, the banker, the head of the city commission, went away. He was let go.

And Clarence Stephenson, the Black handyman, went on trial for murder.

His trial was in November of nineteen thirty-two, in Logan. And the jury was white, all of it, the accounts say, though there were more than a thousand Black people living in Logan County at the time. And his lawyers argued that the case against him was nothing but circumstance. That there were holes in the timeline, and holes in the story about the car, and that there was nothing that put the knife or the gun in his hand. And the jury found him guilty. And he was sentenced to life in prison.

And here is a thing, friend, that I only put together years later, and it gave me a chill. That very same month, November of nineteen thirty-two, the Supreme Court of the United States handed down a famous decision. It was about nine young Black men down in Scottsboro, Alabama, who had been accused of a crime against two white women, and tried in a rush, by all-white juries, without any real lawyer to defend them, and sentenced to die. And the Supreme Court said, in that November, that a man on trial for his life in this country had a right to a real defense. That a trial where the outcome was decided before it started was not a trial at all.

The whole country read about the Scottsboro boys that month. And in the same days, in a courthouse in Logan County, West Virginia, a Black handyman went on trial before an all-white jury for the murder of a white woman, with the white banker he worked for already turned loose. I do not know if anybody in that Logan courtroom was reading the newspapers from Washington that week. I expect they were not.

They could have sent him to the gallows, friend. West Virginia was still hanging men in those years, at the penitentiary in Moundsville. The jury gave him life instead. I have heard some say that was because even the jury was not sure. I cannot tell you what was in their heads.

He was sent to the state penitentiary at Moundsville, the accounts say, and later moved to a prison farm. And in nineteen forty-two, ten years after Mamie Thurman died, Clarence Stephenson died too. Of a cancer, in his stomach. Still a prisoner.

Now I want to stop here, friend, and tell you how this sounded to the folks at my counter, years later, when they told it. Because it always sounded the same way. They would get to the part where the banker went free and the Black handyman went to prison, and they would stop, and look at me. And they would not have to say another word. Every one of us knew what that meant, in nineteen thirty-two, in a coal county run by a few powerful men.

There was one man in particular I remember, friend. A Black man, a retired coal miner, who had worked in Logan County as a young fellow and later came over our way, and traded at my store for years. A quiet, careful man. And one day, when this story came up and a couple of white fellows at the stove were going on about it, he finished buying his things, and on his way out, he stopped at the door, and he said, without turning around, we all knew. Every one of us. We knew the day they arrested him. And then he went on out.

I never heard him say another word about it. He did not need to.

Now the fourth suspect. And this one, friend, is not one man. It is a room.

The story that has been told in Logan County for ninety years, and I give it to you as a story, because I cannot prove it and neither can anybody else, is that there was a club in Logan. A secret club, among the most powerful men in town. Bankers, lawyers, politicians, coal men. And they kept a room, the story goes, somewhere downtown. A room where they could drink, in Prohibition, and gamble, and bring women who were not their wives. And each member, the story goes, had a key to that room.

Folks call it the key club.

And the story is that Mamie Thurman was part of that world. That she knew those men. That she went to that room. And that she kept a book. A little book, a diary, with the names of the men she had been with. And the story is that there were something like sixteen names in it, and that every one of them was somebody in Logan County.

Now, friend, think about what that means, if any of it is true. A policeman's wife, with a little book, with sixteen of the most powerful men in a county like Logan written down in it. Bankers. Politicians. Maybe judges. Maybe men from the courthouse. Maybe men who would sit on a jury, or pick one.

That is not a woman with a secret, friend. That is a woman with a loaded gun.

And I want to say one thing for Mamie Thurman here, because in all the telling of this story, she has become a kind of legend, the woman with the little book, and I think that is not fair to her. She was a young woman in a hard town, married to a man without much money, living over somebody else's garage. And the men with the money and the power took notice of her, the way men like that do. Whatever she did with them, and whatever she wrote down, I do not believe she was the one with the power in that room. I believe she found out too late that she had been playing a game with men who did not lose.

Whatever she did, friend, she did not deserve the side of that mountain road.

And the story is that the book was never made public. That if it was found, it disappeared. That those sixteen names were never said out loud in any court.

I cannot tell you if there was a book. I cannot tell you if there were sixteen names. I can only tell you that in all my years of hearing this story, the book was always in it. Every telling. And I have learned, friend, that when a detail stays the same in every telling for fifty years, there is usually something underneath it.

And there was a woman who traded with me, who had been a girl in Logan in the nineteen thirties. And she told me once that when she was a girl, her mother would not let her walk down a certain street downtown by herself, past a certain building. She never would say why. And the girl never asked. It was only years later, when she heard the stories about the key club, that she wondered. She said, Otis, I never knew which building it was, or if it was even the right one. But I always wondered what Mama knew.

And here, friend, is where this story turns. Because up to now, every suspect I have given you is a man. And a good many people in Logan County never believed it was a man at all.

Now the fifth suspect. And she is the one I have heard named most, by folks who did not want to be heard saying it.

Louise Robertson. The banker's wife.

Think about it from where she stood, friend. Her husband, a big man in town, carrying on with the policeman's wife. Not in some other town. In her own backyard. Over her own garage. Where she could see the light in the window every night.

The story that has been told, and I give it to you as a story, is that Louise Robertson found out. And that it was not Harry who killed Mamie Thurman, and not the handyman. It was the wife. And that Harry Robertson, the banker, the head of the city, used his power to protect her. And that the handyman, who worked for them both, who maybe drove the car, or cleaned up after, or simply knew too much, was the one who was handed to the law.

I want to be very careful here, friend. Louise Robertson was never charged with anything. She is long dead and cannot answer. And I will not stand here and tell you she did it. I will only tell you that I have heard her name said, low, at my counter, by more than one person with kin in Logan. And that every time it was said, the person saying it looked over their shoulder first.

Now I want to give you the other side, as fair as I can, because I always try to.

There are folks who will tell you that Clarence Stephenson was guilty. That a jury heard the evidence, and found him so, and that we have no business, ninety years later, second-guessing twelve men who sat in that courtroom and heard it all. That the story about the key club and the little book is the kind of thing that grows up around any murder of a pretty woman, and that it makes a better story than the plain truth, which may be that a handyman with some grudge or some reason killed a woman and was caught.

And I will give them this. I was not in that courtroom. I did not hear the evidence. I cannot tell you for certain that Clarence Stephenson did not do it.

And I will give them one more thing. The year Mamie Thurman died, nineteen thirty-two, was the year somebody kidnapped Charles Lindbergh's baby, up in New Jersey. Every newspaper in the country was full of it. And it was a time, friend, when folks all over this country were frightened of crime, and wanted somebody caught, and wanted it fast. A jury in nineteen thirty-two did not have much patience for a defense lawyer saying there was not enough evidence. Folks wanted the thing settled. That is not an excuse for what may have happened to Clarence Stephenson. But it is part of why it could.

But I will tell you what I can tell you. The banker who admitted the affair, in whose car the blood was found, walked free. The Black handyman who worked for him went to prison for life, convicted by a white jury, in a county where the courthouse had been bought and paid for by the coal companies for twenty years. If you want me to believe that was all fair and square, friend, you are going to have to do a good deal more convincing than anybody ever did at my counter.

And here is another thing that has always stuck with me. Mamie Thurman's own family, the accounts say, did not believe it either. A half brother of hers, a man named George Morrison, who became a lawyer himself, a prosecutor out west, came back to Logan in his later years and looked into it. And he came away believing, the accounts say, that the wrong man had gone to prison. A prosecutor, friend. Her own brother. Not some story-teller at a stove.

Now I promised you the old stories too, and with Mamie Thurman, the old stories are mostly about the road.

Folks say Mamie Thurman still walks the Twenty-Two Mine Road. That on certain nights, people driving up that road have seen a woman in a long old-fashioned dress, standing by the side of the road, with long dark hair. And that when they slow down, she is gone.

Folks say that if you drive to the bottom of the hill where she was found, and put your car in neutral, and take your foot off the brake, your car will roll back up the hill, by itself, like somebody is pushing it. And that it is Mamie, pushing you away, telling you to leave, the way she was never able to leave.

I have never tried it, friend. I have heard there are a good many hills in this country where a car seems to roll uphill, and that it is a trick of the eye, the way the land lies. But I will not tell you that to the young folks from Logan who go up there on Friday nights. They do not want to hear it.

And folks say, and this one I believe, that nobody in Logan County ever talked about the key club while any of those men were alive. And I have noticed something about ghost stories like the one on the Twenty-Two Mine Road, friend, over all my years of hearing them. When a town cannot get justice for somebody, it very often gets a ghost instead. A woman who was never avenged in the courthouse walks the road at night, and every young fellow in the county knows her name. The men who did it, if they were ever named at all, are forgotten. But she is not. I have always thought that is the way a place like Logan keeps a secret and tells it at the same time. That everybody knew, or thought they knew, and nobody said. And that by the time it was safe to say anything, there was nobody left alive who could prove it.

So I promised you I would tell you who I believe killed Mamie Thurman.

Here is where I come down, friend. And I will tell you plainly, it is an old storekeeper's guess, and you can throw it out.

I do not believe Clarence Stephenson killed her. I believe he was the one who was easiest to send. A Black man, working for a white man, in Logan County, in nineteen thirty-two. If somebody powerful needed this to be over, and needed somebody to pay for it, there was nobody in that county easier to hand to a jury than Clarence Stephenson. And I believe that is exactly what happened.

As for who did it, I do not know. I cannot tell you if it was the banker, or the banker's wife, or one of the sixteen names in a little book that nobody ever saw, or somebody none of us has ever heard of.

But I will tell you what I believe Mamie Thurman's murder really was. I believe it was a woman who knew too much about too many powerful men, in a county where powerful men did not have to answer to anybody. I believe somebody decided she had to stop knowing it. And I believe the whole machinery of that county, the police and the courts and the newspaper and the men with the keys, decided all together, without ever saying a word to each other, that it would be better for everybody if the case went away quick. With the right man walking free. And the wrong man in a cell.

And it worked, friend. For ninety years, it has worked. Nobody ever stood trial but the handyman. The little book was never found. The key club, if there ever was one, kept its keys.

There were two people in this story, friend, who could not tell what they knew. A young man picking blackberries, who could not speak. And a handyman in a prison cell, who could speak all he wanted, and nobody in Logan County would listen.

I have always thought those two were the most honest men in the whole story.

That's as far as the story goes, friend, and about as far as I'll take you tonight.
