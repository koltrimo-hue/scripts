On a hillside in France, in October of nineteen eighteen, a big redheaded farm boy from the mountains of Tennessee was lying flat on his belly in the mud, with a rifle, and German machine guns firing over his head.

Six of the men he had come up that hill with were dead. Three more were wounded. The sergeant in charge was down. And somehow, in the noise and the smoke, that farm boy from Tennessee was the only one left who could do anything about it.

So he did what he had done since he was a boy, back home, at the turkey shoots. Every time a German soldier raised his head up over that ridge to look for him, the farm boy fired. And he did not miss.

And when it was over, he walked back to the American lines with a hundred and thirty-two German prisoners marching in front of him.

Up on the Cumberland Plateau in Tennessee, near the Kentucky line, in Fentress County, there is a little valley where three forks of a river come together, the Wolf River. And there is a little settlement there called Pall Mall. And in eighteen eighty-seven, in the middle of December, Alvin Cullum York was born there, in a two-room log cabin.

He was one of eleven children. His daddy was a farmer and a blacksmith. They were poor, friend, poor the way folks up there were poor, which is to say they had a roof and a garden and a rifle and not much else. Alvin went to school, the accounts say, for about nine months in his whole life. That was all the schooling there was to be had, and a boy was needed in the field.

What he had instead was a rifle.

In that country, a boy learned to shoot as soon as he could hold a gun up. Not for sport. For supper. Squirrels, rabbits, turkeys. And Alvin York was a natural. And the men of that valley had a contest they held, called a turkey shoot. They would tie a turkey behind a log, so only its head showed, and men would shoot at it from a long way off, and the head would be bobbing and moving the whole time. If you hit it, you won the turkey. Alvin York won a great many turkeys.

Hold on to that turkey's head, friend. A head bobbing up from behind a log. It is going to save his life.

Now, his daddy died when Alvin was in his early twenties. And Alvin, as one of the oldest boys still at home, had to help carry the family. And he worked hard. But he also went bad, the way young men in those hills sometimes did.

He and his friends would go over the state line into Kentucky, to the rough places along the line where you could buy liquor, and they would drink and gamble and fight and shoot off their pistols. The accounts say Alvin York was one of the wildest young men in that whole county. His mother prayed over him. The church folks shook their heads.

And then something happened that turned him around.

The accounts say his best friend, a young man named Everett Delk, was killed in a fight in one of those rough places across the line. Killed. And Alvin York, the hell-raiser, took it hard.

And at the end of nineteen fourteen, there was a revival meeting in that valley. A preacher came, the way they did, and held meetings every night. And Alvin York went. And on New Year's Day, nineteen fifteen, the accounts say, he gave his heart to the Lord, right there.

And he quit. All of it. The drinking, the gambling, the cussing, the fighting. He quit cold, and the accounts say he never went back to any of it for the rest of his life.

And there was something else in it too, friend, and I am glad to tell it. There was a girl. Her name was Gracie Williams, and she lived in that valley, and Alvin York had been sweet on her. Now here is the part that matters. The church Alvin York joined was a small one, called the Church of Christ in Christian Union. And it was very strict. And among the things it taught, the way Alvin understood it, was that a Christian must not kill. Not in a fight. Not in a war. Thou shalt not kill. It said it right there in the Bible, and Alvin York, who had come to the Lord the hard way, meant to do exactly what it said.

Hold on to that, friend. Thou shalt not kill. Because in April of nineteen seventeen, the United States went to war.

Alvin York registered. He was twenty-nine. And on his draft card, there was a place where you could claim an exemption, a reason why you should not be made to go. And the accounts say Alvin York wrote, in his own hand, yes. Don't want to fight.

And he and his preacher asked the government to excuse him as a conscientious objector. That is a man who refuses to fight because of his religion. And the government said no. His little church was not one of the ones the government recognized as forbidding war. And so in November of nineteen seventeen, Alvin York was drafted into the United States Army, and sent off to a camp in Georgia.

Now, friend, I want you to imagine being that man. You have finally turned your life around. You have given yourself to God. You believe, with everything in you, that killing is a sin. And your government puts a rifle in your hands and says, you are going to France to kill Germans.

And here is where Alvin York surprised everybody. He did not run. He did not refuse to put on the uniform. He went. He trained. He did what he was told. And on the rifle range, the officers found out very quickly that this quiet redheaded mountain man could shoot better than anybody they had ever seen.

But he told them the truth. He told his officers he did not believe he could kill a man.

And here is the thing I love about this part of the story, friend. His officers did not throw him in the stockade. They did not call him a coward. His company commander, a captain, and his battalion commander, a major named Edward Buxton, sat down with him. And they talked with him about it. And they did it with the Bible. Major Buxton, the accounts say, was a man who knew his scripture as well as York did, and the two of them sat and went back and forth, verse by verse. Thou shalt not kill, said York. And the major answered him with other verses. About a time for war and a time for peace. About the watchman on the wall, who is bound to warn the city and defend it. About rendering unto Caesar.

And then the army did something that I have always thought was remarkable. They gave Alvin York leave. They sent him home to the Wolf River for ten days, and they told him to think about it, and pray about it, and come back and tell them what he had decided. And if he still could not fight, they would find something else for him to do.

And Alvin York went home, and the accounts say he went up on the mountainside above his home, alone, and prayed. For a long time. A day and a night, by some tellings.

And he came down from that mountain, and went back to the army, and told them he would go.

He said, in the way it has come down, that he had come to believe that if some men had to fight so that other men would not have to, so that the killing would stop, then that could be God's will too. That he could fight to bring peace. He had made his peace with it. He was going to France.

That is the first half of Alvin York, friend. A man who did not want to fight, and who did not run from it either. He did it the hard way. He wrestled it out with God, on a mountain, and came down and did what he had decided.

Now the morning.

On the eighth of October, nineteen eighteen, Alvin York was a corporal in the Eighty-Second Division of the United States Army, in France, in a great forest called the Argonne. The war was in its last weeks, though nobody knew it yet. And the Americans were pushing into the Argonne against the Germans, and it was some of the worst fighting of the whole war.

I want you to understand how big it was, friend. The fight the Americans called the Meuse-Argonne was the largest battle the United States Army had ever fought, before or since. More than a million American soldiers were in it, the accounts say. And over the six weeks it lasted, something like twenty-six thousand Americans were killed. In six weeks. Farm boys and city boys who had never been farther from home than the county seat, sent into a forest in France where the Germans had spent four years digging in, with barbed wire and concrete and machine guns on every hill.

And York's division had a nickname. The Eighty-Second was called the All American division, because the army had filled it with men from every state in the Union. Farm boys from Tennessee alongside immigrants' sons from New York and Boston, some of whom could barely speak English. And that matters for what happened that morning, friend, because the handful of men who went around behind those guns with York were not all mountain boys. They had names from all over. That morning, York's battalion was sent to take a ridge. And as they moved out across an open valley, German machine guns up on the hills on either side opened up on them, and the Americans were cut down in the open.

So a sergeant named Bernard Early was sent with a little group of men, seventeen of them, the accounts say, York among them, to work their way around behind those German machine guns and knock them out.

They went into the woods. They worked their way around. And they came up behind the German lines, and they stumbled onto a group of German soldiers, a headquarters, the accounts say, men eating breakfast, and an officer. And then the German machine guns on the ridge above them turned around.

The Germans up on that hill saw what was happening below, and they swung their guns around and opened fire, right down into the clearing, at the Americans and the German prisoners together. And in a few seconds, six of the Americans were dead. Three more were wounded, and one of them was Sergeant Early.

And Alvin York was the one left in charge.

He was out in front, the accounts say, a little separated from the rest, lying on the ground. And the machine guns were firing over him, cutting the brush to pieces. And the rest of the Americans who were still alive were down behind the prisoners, guarding them.

So it was York, mostly by himself, against those machine guns on the hill.

And here is where the turkey's head comes back, friend.

The German machine gunners had to lift their heads up over their guns to aim. And every time a head came up, Alvin York fired. And he did not miss. Because he had spent his whole boyhood shooting at turkey heads bobbing up from behind a log, and a German helmet coming up over a machine gun was the same thing to his eye. He said so himself, later. He said it was like the turkey shoots back home.

And he talked to them while he did it. That is in the accounts too. He kept hollering up at the Germans to come down and surrender, that he did not want to kill any more of them than he had to.

Then a German officer sent a squad of men charging down the hill at him, with bayonets. Six of them, the accounts say. And York was out of rifle shots. So he pulled his pistol. And here is the thing that every hunter in the hills understood the minute they heard it. When you are shooting at a flock of turkeys coming at you, you shoot the last one first. Because if you shoot the front one, the ones behind see it fall and scatter. But if you shoot the last one, the ones in front do not know it happened, and they keep coming. And Alvin York did exactly that. He shot the last man first, and then the next, working forward, and the men in front never knew until it was too late.

And then the German officer who had surrendered at the start, a major, the accounts say, called out to York, in English. He had lived in Chicago, by some tellings. And he said, if you will stop shooting, I will make them give up.

And the officer blew a whistle, and the Germans on the hill came down with their hands up. Dozens of them. By the time Alvin York got back to the American lines, the accounts say, he had a hundred and thirty-two German prisoners. And the accounts say he had killed about twenty-five German soldiers that morning, and that thirty-five German machine guns had been put out of action.
