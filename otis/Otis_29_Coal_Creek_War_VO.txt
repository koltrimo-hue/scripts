On a summer night in eighteen ninety-one, in the coal mountains of East Tennessee, several hundred coal miners with rifles and shotguns walked up to a prison stockade and told the guards to open the gate.

Inside the stockade were convicts. More than a hundred of them, by the accounts. Men in stripes, a great many of them Black, a good many of them sent there for crimes no bigger than stealing a chicken or being out of work. The state of Tennessee had rented them out, like mules, to a coal company. And the coal company had brought them up into those mountains to dig coal, in the place of the free miners it had fired.

The free miners had come to get them out.

And they did. They opened that stockade, and they marched every one of those convicts down to the railroad, and they put them on a train, and sent them back to Knoxville. And they did not hurt a single guard doing it.

It was the beginning of a war. Not a war against the government of Tennessee exactly. A war against a system. And before it was over, there would be soldiers on a hill, and stockades burned to the ground, and men killed. And the miners of Coal Creek would do a thing that the courts and the legislature and the governor of Tennessee had all refused to do.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I have told you about a good many coal wars on these nights, friend. Matewan and Blair Mountain in West Virginia. Bloody Harlan in Kentucky. But this one came first. And it is the one that most folks have never heard of. And it is, I believe, the strangest of them all. Because it is the only one I know of where the miners went to war, not for higher pay, not for a union, but to set somebody else free.

I heard about Coal Creek from an old man who traded with me, whose granddaddy had been a miner there. And he told it with a kind of pride I did not hear often, friend. He said, my granddaddy did not take one dollar out of it. He went up that hill with a rifle, to let some poor fellows out of a pen, who he never met, and who was mostly colored men he would not have sat at supper with. And he did it anyway. And he said that was the best thing anybody in his family ever did.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to tell you about the Coal Creek War through three places. A miner's cabin. A prison stockade. And a hill with a fort on it. And then I am going to tell you about a fourth place, a mine, ten years later, and some notes that dying men wrote in the dark. Because they belong to this story too.

And at the very end, I am going to tell you what I believe those miners were really fighting for. Because it was not what the newspapers said, and it was not exactly what the miners said either.

Let me start with the cabin.

Up in Anderson County, Tennessee, north of Knoxville, there is a valley with a creek running through it called Coal Creek. And in the years after the Civil War, coal companies came into those mountains and opened mines. And men came to work them. Mountain men off the farms. Men from Kentucky and Virginia. Some Welshmen and Englishmen who had dug coal in the old country. And a good many Black men, too, some of them born in slavery, come up from the South to dig coal for a wage.

They lived in little houses near the mines, in the towns of Coal Creek and Briceville and the camps around them. And by the eighteen nineties, the miners there had a few things that they were proud of. They had a union. And they had won some things that most miners in the South did not have.

They had the right, by the law of Tennessee, to have a man of their own at the scales. A checkweighman. Do you remember that, from the story of Paint Creek in West Virginia? A miner was paid by the ton. And the company weighed the coal. And the miners believed, with good reason, that the company's scales were crooked. So they wanted one of their own men standing at the scales to watch.

And they wanted to be paid in money. Real money. Not in scrip that was only good at the company store.

Hold on to those two things, friend. A man at the scales, and real money. They are what started it.

In the spring of eighteen ninety-one, the miners at one of the companies at Briceville said they wanted their own checkweighman, the way the law said they could have. And the company said no. And it said something else. It said the miners would have to sign a paper, a contract, promising they would not join a union, and they would not ask for a checkweighman, and they would take their pay in scrip.

The miners would not sign. So the company locked them out. Fired the lot of them.

And then the company did something the miners had been afraid of for years. It sent for convicts.

Now the stockade.

You have to understand something about the state of Tennessee in those years, friend, and it is an ugly thing, and I want to tell it plain.

After the Civil War, Tennessee, like a good many Southern states, did not want to pay to keep its prisoners. Prisons cost money. So the state came up with a scheme. It leased its prisoners out. It rented them, by the hundreds, to private companies. Railroad companies. Coal companies. The company paid the state a fee for every convict, and the company fed them and guarded them, and worked them. And the state did not have to build prisons or pay guards. It made money on its convicts instead of spending it.

They called it the convict lease.

And here is who the convicts were, friend. A great many of them were Black men. In those years after slavery, the accounts say, Black men in the South were arrested for every small thing you could think of. Vagrancy, which meant being out of work. Loitering. Stealing something small. Petty things, that a white man might get a fine for, or a warning. And they were sentenced to hard labor, and leased out to the coal companies. Some folks have said, and I believe they are right, that it was slavery by another name. The war had freed the slaves. And within a few years, the state had found a way to put a great many of them right back in chains, working for somebody else, for nothing.

And the conditions, friend. The accounts say the convicts were worked in the mines from before daylight to after dark, in water, in bad air, chained up at night in stockades, fed poorly, beaten, and that they died. They died at a terrible rate. When a convict died, the company did not lose anything. It just asked the state for another one.

And here is why the free miners hated the convict lease, friend, and I want to be honest about it. It was not only because it was cruel to the convicts, though a good many of them came to see that it was. It was because the convicts took their jobs. A coal company that could rent a convict for almost nothing, who could not strike, could not join a union, could not ask for a checkweighman, could not complain about scrip, did not ever need to deal with a free miner again. Any time the free miners asked for anything, the company could send for convicts. The convict lease was a knife at the throat of every free working man in Tennessee.

So when the company at Briceville fired its miners and sent for convicts, the miners knew exactly what was happening. And so did every other miner in the valley.

In the summer of eighteen ninety-one, the convicts came to Briceville. More than a hundred of them, the accounts say, under armed guards. And they built a stockade, a prison pen of logs, to be locked up in at night. And the accounts say that in building it, they tore down some of the houses the free miners had lived in.

The convicts were made to tear down the homes of the men whose jobs they had been brought to take.

Hold on to that, friend.

Now the first night.

On the night of the fourteenth of July, eighteen ninety-one, the accounts say, the miners of Coal Creek and Briceville gathered. Several hundred of them, the accounts say, armed. And they marched to the stockade at Briceville. And they did not attack it. They surrounded it, and they told the guards to open it up and bring out the convicts. And the guards looked at several hundred armed miners, and did.

And the miners marched the convicts and the guards down to the railroad, and put them on a train, and sent them off to Knoxville. Peaceably. The accounts say they did not shoot anybody. They did not hurt anybody. They just sent the convicts away.

And then they did the same thing at another stockade, at Coal Creek, a few days later.

And the governor of Tennessee came.

His name was John Buchanan. He was a farmer's governor, elected with the help of the farmers' movement of those years, and the accounts say he was not without sympathy for working people. But he was the governor. And the law said the convicts were the state's, and the state had leased them to the company, and a crowd of armed miners had just taken them away. So he brought the state militia, soldiers, and he brought the convicts back to the stockades.

And the miners came again. Thousands of them this time, the accounts say, from all over that coal country and from Kentucky too. And they surrounded the soldiers. And there was a standoff. And the governor, to his credit, did not order the soldiers to fire. He talked. He came up and spoke to the miners himself. And he made them a deal.

He said that if the miners would let the convicts go back to work, he would call the legislature of Tennessee into a special session, and ask it to do something about the convict lease. A truce. Sixty days, the accounts say.

And the miners agreed. They trusted the law, friend. One more time.

Now here is what the legislature did.

It met. And it listened. And it did some small things. It said, again, that miners had the right to their checkweighman, and to be paid in real money. But it would not end the convict lease. Not at all. It would not touch it. The state was making money on it, and the companies wanted it, and the men in the legislature were not about to give it up for a bunch of coal miners. And then, the accounts say, it went further. It passed a law making it a serious crime to interfere with the state's convicts.

So the miners had trusted the law. And the law had told them, in so many words, no. And if you do it again, it is a felony.

Now the second night. The burning.

At the end of October, and into November of eighteen ninety-one, the miners came again. And this time, friend, they did not just march the convicts off to the train. They opened the stockades, at Briceville, and at Coal Creek, and at another mine over at a place called Oliver Springs. And they let the convicts out. Hundreds of them. And this time, the accounts say, they did not send them to Knoxville under guard. A good many of the convicts they simply turned loose. Gave them clothes, some say, to change out of their stripes, and pointed them toward the hills. And then they set fire to the stockades and burned them to the ground.

Hundreds of convicts, friend, walking out of those burning pens, free, into the mountains of East Tennessee. The accounts say something like four or five hundred in all. Not all of them stayed free, I will tell you. The state hunted a good many of them down again in the weeks after, and brought them back. But some got away. Over the mountains into Kentucky, and north, and gone. And I have always hoped a few of them made it all the way to somewhere they could start over, friend, with a name nobody knew.

Now I want to stop and tell you what that means. Those were the state's prisoners. And a crowd of white mountain miners had just set them free. Black and white together, turned loose to go wherever they could get to. There is no other story quite like it in all the coal wars I know.

Now the fort.

The state of Tennessee was not going to stand for that. And in eighteen ninety-two, the state built a fort. On a hill overlooking Coal Creek, it put up earthworks and a stockade and a garrison of soldiers. The miners called it Militia Hill. The state called it Fort Anderson. And it brought the convicts back to the mines, under the guns of that fort.

And in the summer of eighteen ninety-two, it came to real fighting.

The miners rose up again, all through the coal country of Tennessee. At Tracy City, down on the Cumberland Plateau. Tracy City was the biggest of them, a coal town where the biggest coal and iron company in the state worked hundreds of convicts. And the accounts say the miners there freed the convicts and burned that stockade too, and put the convicts on a train to Nashville, and told the train to keep going. At Oliver Springs. And at Coal Creek, they laid siege to Fort Anderson itself. Miners with rifles, in the hills all around it, shooting down at the soldiers. And the soldiers shooting back.

And men were killed, friend. On both sides. Soldiers. Miners. The accounts do not agree on how many, and I will not give you a number I cannot stand behind. But it was a war now. Not a march. A war.

And the state sent more soldiers. A great many more. A general came with them. Hundreds of soldiers, the accounts say, the biggest call-out of the state militia Tennessee had seen since the Civil War. Coming into a coal valley to protect a coal company's right to work prisoners. And they broke the siege of Fort Anderson. And they went through the valley arresting miners, hundreds of them, the accounts say. And the miners who had led it were put on trial. Some went to prison themselves. And the convicts went back into the mines under the guns.

So the miners lost, friend. They lost the war.

And then they won anyway.

Here is what happened. The fighting at Coal Creek had made the convict lease the biggest question in the whole state of Tennessee. Newspapers all over the country wrote about it. Folks who had never thought about the convict lease in their lives were suddenly reading about men chained in stockades, and coal companies renting prisoners from the state, and free miners burning the pens. And a good many of them did not like what they read. And the governor, John Buchanan, who had tried to talk it out, was not reelected. The whole thing had broken him.

And when the state's contract with the coal company ran out, a few years later, Tennessee did not renew it. The convict lease in Tennessee came to an end, in eighteen ninety-six.

Tennessee was one of the first states in the South to end it. And it ended it, friend, because a bunch of coal miners in Anderson County kept burning the stockades until the state could not stand the cost of building them back.

Now I have to tell you what the state did instead. Because it is the twist at the end, and it is a hard one.

When Tennessee stopped leasing its convicts to the coal companies, it did not stop making convicts dig coal. It built its own prison. Up in the mountains, not far from Coal Creek, in a deep hollow, at a place called Brushy Mountain. And the mine went right into the mountain from inside the prison walls. And the state's own prisoners dug coal there, for the state. For years and years. The accounts say the opening of the mine was inside the prison yard.

And one more thing about Coal Creek, friend, that you may find a little strange. The town is not called Coal Creek anymore. In the nineteen thirties, when the government built Norris Dam, the one I told you about in the story of the towns under the lakes, the lake came up not far from Coal Creek. And the town decided it would rather be known for the lake than for the coal. So they changed its name. They called it Lake City. A town that had fought a war over coal, and buried hundreds of its men in coal mine explosions, decided it would rather put all of that behind it and be known for the fishing.

I do not blame them one bit, friend. But I have always thought the old name told the truth better.

So the state got out of the business of renting convicts to the coal companies, friend. And went into the coal business itself.

And that prison at Brushy Mountain stood for a very long time. You may have heard of it for another reason. In nineteen seventy-seven, the man who killed Martin Luther King Junior, James Earl Ray, was a prisoner there, and he escaped over the wall and was loose in those mountains for a few days before they caught him. I remember it on the news. The whole country watching a manhunt in the woods of Morgan County, Tennessee, not far from where the miners of Coal Creek had burned the stockades eighty-some years before.

Now the fourth place. The mine at Fraterville.

I promised you notes written in the dark, friend, and here they are. They do not belong to the war exactly. But they belong to Coal Creek, and I believe they belong in this story.

On the nineteenth of May, nineteen hundred and two, about ten years after the war, there was an explosion in a mine near Coal Creek, at a place called Fraterville. And something over two hundred men and boys died in it. The accounts say it was the worst mine disaster in the history of Tennessee. And in the little town of Coal Creek, the accounts say, there were hardly any grown men left in some families. Whole families of men and boys, gone in one morning. And it was not the last, friend. In nineteen eleven, nine years after Fraterville, another mine in that same valley, at a place called Cross Mountain, exploded, and something like eighty more men died. And there were notes found there too, the accounts say, written by trapped men to their families. That valley gave more than its share to the coal.

And here is the part that I have never been able to read without stopping, friend.

When the rescue men finally got into the mine, they found that a good many of the miners had not died in the explosion. They had lived through it, and been trapped, back in the mine, behind the bad air. And they had gone on living for some hours in the dark, slowly suffocating. And some of them, knowing they were going to die, had written notes to their families. On scraps of paper. On the backs of things. By the light of their lamps, as long as the lamps lasted.

And those notes were found, and carried out, and given to the families. And some of them have come down to us.

I will give you a piece of one, friend, the way it has been printed, and then I will stop. A man named Jacob Vowell wrote to his wife. And the words that have come down are something like this. Ellen, darling, goodbye for us both. Elbert said the Lord has saved him. We are all praying for air to support us, but it is getting so bad without any air.

Elbert was his son, friend. Thirteen or fourteen years old, the accounts say, down in the mine with his father. And the father wrote to his wife that their boy said the Lord had saved him. Meaning his soul. Not his life.

And another man wrote, the accounts say, telling his wife what debts were owed and what was owed to him, so that she would not be cheated after he was gone. Settling his accounts, by lamplight, while the air ran out.

I have thought about that man more than any other in this whole story, friend. A miner at the end of his life, in the dark, thinking about the company store ledger. Making sure his wife would know what he was owed.

That is the miners of Coal Creek. Right there. That is who those men were.

Now I want to give you the other side, as I always try to.

There are folks who will tell you that the miners of Coal Creek were lawbreakers, plain and simple. That they took up arms against the lawful government of their state, and freed convicted criminals, some of whom had done real harm to people, and burned state property, and shot at soldiers. And that whatever you think of the convict lease, you cannot have a country where any crowd with rifles can decide which laws to obey.

And there are folks who will tell you something harder still. That the miners did not set those convicts free out of the goodness of their hearts. They did it because the convicts were taking their jobs. That if the company had been leasing convicts to dig some other man's coal, the miners of Coal Creek would not have marched one step. And that a good many of those miners had no love at all for the Black men they let out of those pens.

And I will give them both of those, friend. I think there is truth in them both.

But I will tell you what the old man at my counter said, about his granddaddy, when I put that to him. He said, Otis, I don't know what was in Granddaddy's heart. Maybe it was just his job. But he knew those men in the pen were getting worked to death for nothing, and he knew it was wrong, and he knew if it could happen to them, it could happen to him. And he went up the hill.

Now I promised you the old stories, and there are a few.

Folks said that some of the convicts who were turned loose in the hills were hidden by mountain families, and fed, and sent on their way north. I cannot prove it. But I have heard it said by more than one family from that country, and it is the kind of thing mountain people did, friend, for somebody being hunted by the law, whatever color he was.

Folks said that the women of Coal Creek carried food and ammunition to the miners in the hills during the siege of the fort, in their aprons and their baskets, walking right past the soldiers. I believe that. The women of these mountains have always been in the wars, whatever the history books say.

And folks said that there was a song about it. There always is. I never heard all of it. But I heard an old man sing a piece of it once, at my stove, about the miners of Coal Creek and the convict pen. He did not know where he had learned it. His daddy, he said.

So I promised you I would tell you what those miners were really fighting for.

Here is where I come down, friend.

The newspapers said they were fighting against the law. The miners said they were fighting for their jobs. And both of those were true.

But I believe that underneath it all, they were fighting about one question. The same question every story I have told you about these coalfields comes back to, sooner or later.

What is a man worth?

The coal company said a man was worth what it cost to rent him. And if it could rent a convict cheaper than it could hire a free miner, then the free miner was worth nothing, and the convict was worth a few cents a day, and if the convict died, he was worth the price of the next one.

The miners of Coal Creek said no. They said a man was worth more than that. A free man and a convict both. And they could not have told you that in a fancy speech, friend. Most of them could barely sign their names. But they said it with their rifles, on a summer night at the gate of a stockade.

And ten years later, when men like them were trapped in the dark at Fraterville, with the air running out, one of them spent his last hour making sure his wife would know what he was owed.

That is the same kind of man, friend. Asking the same question. What is a man worth. And answering it, to the very last breath, the only way he knew how.

That's as far as the story goes, friend, and about as far as I'll take you tonight.
