On a summer night in nineteen forty-six, in a little county seat in East Tennessee, a crowd of young men with rifles surrounded the county jail.

They were not outlaws. They were not a mob from out of town. They were veterans. Farm boys and mill hands and store clerks who had come home a year before from Normandy and Okinawa and North Africa and the Pacific. Men who had fought the Germans and the Japanese to defend democracy in places they could not have found on a map before the war.

And now they had come home. And they had found that in their own county, in their own courthouse, there was no democracy left to defend.

So they took up their rifles one more time. And they laid siege to their own county jail, where the sheriff's men were holed up with the ballot boxes from that day's election. And they fired on it, half the night. And a little before dawn, they threw dynamite.

Now hold on a second before I get into this one. Folks ask me every so often why I bother telling these at all, so let me answer it plain, just the once, and then I won't bring it up again tonight. Name's Otis Greer. Kept a store counter and a post office window in these hills the better part of forty years, and all I'm doing here is passing along what came across it. Want more of these, subscribe, and hit the bell so the next one finds you without you going looking. That's the last I'll say about it. Now, where was I.

I had a good many veterans in my store over the years, friend. Men from the First World War, when I was young. And then, after nineteen forty-five, men my own age, from the Second. They came home and went back to their farms and their mines and their jobs, and came into my store for their coffee and their shotgun shells, and mostly did not talk about the war at all.

But there was one story those men would talk about. Every one of them. They would light up when it came up. And it was not a story about the war. It was a story about what some boys like them did when they came home.

The Battle of Athens.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to take you through that one day in August of nineteen forty-six, from the morning the polls opened to the morning after, when the ballots were finally counted. Hour by hour. And before that, I am going to tell you how a county in Tennessee got to the point where its own war heroes had to shoot their way into their own jail to count their own votes.

And at the very end, I am going to answer the question that every man who ever told me this story was asking under his breath. Were they right? And I will tell you plainly, I have gone back and forth on it. But I will give you where I come down.

Let me start with the county.

McMinn County sits in East Tennessee, in the valley between Knoxville and Chattanooga, with the mountains rising up to the east. Good farm country, some mills and factories. And the county seat is a town called Athens. A pretty little town with a courthouse square, the way so many Southern county seats are.

And in the nineteen thirties and forties, McMinn County was run by a machine.

Now if you did not grow up in these parts, friend, let me tell you what a political machine was. It was a little bunch of men who ran a county like a business. They controlled the sheriff's office and the courthouse and the election officers. They decided who got the county jobs and the county contracts. And every election, one way or another, they won. Whether the people voted for them or not.

In McMinn County, the machine was run by a man named Paul Cantrell. He had been the sheriff, and then he got himself elected to the state senate, and he put his own man in as sheriff behind him, a fellow named Pat Mansfield. And Cantrell was hooked in with a bigger machine, the one that ran a good deal of the whole state of Tennessee out of Memphis, under a famous political boss named Ed Crump.

Ed Crump was something, friend. He had been mayor of Memphis years before, and then he simply kept running it, from behind the curtain, for decades. He wore fine suits and a straw hat and had a shock of white hair, and for most of the nineteen thirties and forties, it was said that nobody got to be governor of Tennessee or senator from Tennessee unless Ed Crump said so. His people in Memphis could deliver tens of thousands of votes, and the little machines in the counties, like Cantrell's, delivered the rest. Folks in the mountains of East Tennessee had never much cared for Memphis, and they cared even less for being run from it.

And here is how the McMinn County machine made its money, friend, and I want you to listen to this part, because it explains everything.

In those days, in a good many counties in Tennessee, the sheriff and his deputies were paid by what folks called the fee system. They did not get a salary like a schoolteacher. They got paid a fee for every person they arrested. For every one they booked into the jail. For every one they let out. The more people they arrested, the more money they made.

Think about what that does to a sheriff's office, friend.

The accounts say the McMinn County deputies made a regular business of it. They would stop buses coming through the county on the highway, and go through the passengers, and arrest folks for being drunk, whether they were drunk or not. Travelers passing through, who would pay a fine and get back on the bus rather than come back to Tennessee for a trial. Every one of those arrests was money in the sheriff's pocket. The accounts say that over about ten years, those fees came to something close to three hundred thousand dollars. In the Depression and the war. In one county.

And if you did not like it, and tried to vote them out, the machine controlled the ballot boxes. And the men who counted the votes. And there were stories, every election, of ballot boxes being stuffed, and dead men voting, and boxes disappearing and coming back with different numbers in them.

Hold on to that, friend. The machine counted the votes.

I will tell you something, friend, that makes this story come close to home for me. For a good many years, my store was a polling place. On election day, they would set up the booths along the back wall, past the feed sacks, and bring in the ballot box, and two or three folks from each party would sit at a table by the stove all day, watching. And at the end of the day, with the doors locked, they counted the ballots right there on my counter, under the light, one at a time, out loud, with everybody watching everybody. Sometimes it took till midnight. And when they were done, they signed the papers and carried the box to the courthouse, two of them together, one from each side, so neither one could be alone with it.

That is all an honest election is, friend. Folks from both sides watching each other count, on a store counter, under a light bulb. It is not fancy. But when it is gone, it is all gone.

Now the boys came home.

During the war, McMinn County sent its young men off to fight, the same as every county in America. Something like three thousand of them, the accounts say. That was about one in ten of the whole county.

And they fought. In the deserts of North Africa, and in Italy, and across France and Germany, and on the islands in the Pacific. They saw their friends killed. They were wounded. They watched whole countries that had been taken over by men who did not believe in the vote. And they were told, every day, that they were fighting for freedom. For democracy. For the right of free people to choose their own government.

And then, in nineteen forty-five and forty-six, they came home to McMinn County.

And they found Paul Cantrell and Pat Mansfield and the fee system and the bus arrests and the stuffed ballot boxes, right where they had left them. Worse, the accounts say, because while the young men were gone, there had been nobody to stand up to it. And the veterans had heard, the accounts say, that in the election of nineteen forty-four, while they were overseas, things had been as crooked as ever. Folks had complained to the federal government about it, and the federal government had not done anything. So when those boys came home, they already knew one thing for certain. Nobody was coming to help them.

And they had their own troubles, besides, the way every soldier coming home did. There were not enough houses. There were not enough jobs. A man who had been a sergeant in charge of forty men in the war came home and could not get hired to sweep a floor. The government had passed a law, the GI Bill, to help veterans go to school and buy houses, and it was a fine thing, friend, it changed this country. But in nineteen forty-six, a great many of those boys were still sitting at their mothers' kitchen tables, restless and angry, wondering what they had come home to.

And a good many of those boys, friend, came home not inclined to put up with anybody pushing them around anymore. They had just finished with Hitler. They were not going to be scared of a county sheriff.

So they did something about it. The legal way, first.

In the spring of nineteen forty-six, the veterans of McMinn County got together and formed their own political party. They called it the GI Non-Partisan League. Non-partisan, friend, because it was not Democrats against Republicans. It was the veterans against the machine. And they put up their own candidates for county offices, men from both parties. And for sheriff, they put up a man named Knox Henry. A veteran himself, who had been wounded in North Africa.

And they made one promise, above all. An honest election. A fair count. That was the whole platform. Count the votes the way the people cast them.

And the Cantrell machine laughed at them. And got ready.

Now the day.

The election was on the first of August, nineteen forty-six. A Thursday.

And the accounts say that in the days before, the machine brought in deputies. A great many of them. Some say a couple of hundred men, sworn in as special deputies for the election, some of them from other counties, some, by some accounts, from across the state line in Georgia. Armed. To stand at the polling places. To keep order, the machine said. To keep the voters in line, the veterans said.

And the morning the polls opened, there was trouble right away.

At one polling place, the accounts say, an elderly Black farmer named Tom Gillespie came in to vote. And a deputy, a man folks called Windy Wise, told him he could not. And Tom Gillespie said, the way the story has come down, I've always voted here before. And for saying that, the deputy hit him, and when Tom Gillespie went out the door, the deputy shot him.

Shot him, friend. For wanting to vote. Tom Gillespie lived, the accounts say. And that deputy, later on, went to prison for it. But it tells you what kind of a day it was going to be.

And at other polling places, the veterans who had volunteered to watch the count were kept out, or thrown out, or roughed up. At one of them, the accounts say, a couple of GI poll watchers were beaten. And at another, some of the veterans, when the deputies tried to carry off the ballot box, refused to let them, and held the deputies right there.

And as the day went on, the deputies, at the end of the voting, gathered up ballot boxes from some of the key polling places and carried them off. Not to the courthouse, where they were supposed to be counted in public. To the county jail. Where the sheriff and his men could count them with the door locked.

And the veterans knew exactly what that meant. Everybody in McMinn County knew what that meant. They had seen it before. The machine was going to count those boxes behind a locked door, and whatever the people had voted, the machine was going to win.

Now the evening.

So the veterans did what soldiers do, friend. They got organized.

The accounts say they gathered, a good many of them, and somebody said, we are not going to let them do it this time. And they went and got guns. Some had their own hunting rifles and shotguns. And some of them, the accounts say, went to the National Guard armory in town and took the rifles out of it. Army rifles. The same kind of rifles a good many of them had carried in the war.

And as it got dark, a crowd of those veterans, armed, a few dozen of them at first and then more, came to the county jail. And they took up positions, the way they had been trained, behind cars and on the rooftops and in the buildings across the street. And they called out to the sheriff's men inside to hand over the ballot boxes.

And the deputies inside the jail, the accounts say, with the ballot boxes, would not come out.

And somebody fired a shot.

Nobody can tell you for certain who fired first, friend. I have told you that about a good many gunfights in these mountains, and it was true at Athens too. But once it started, it did not stop. The veterans fired into the jail, and the deputies fired out. For hours. The windows of the jail were shot out. Folks all over Athens heard it, and a great many of them came downtown to watch, standing back at the edges of the square, the way folks always do, as if it were the Fourth of July. It was August, friend, and hot, the kind of thick East Tennessee summer night where the air does not move and the bugs sing in the trees. And there in the middle of it, around the courthouse square of a pretty little Southern town, the crack of Army rifles going back and forth, and the sound of breaking glass, and boys shouting to each other in the language they had learned in the war.

And while the shooting went on, the crowd in the streets grew. Not just veterans now. Townspeople. Farmers who had come in to vote. Folks who had been afraid of that sheriff for ten years and were not afraid of him tonight.

Now here, friend, I want to tell you about the veterans, because this is the part that the old soldiers at my counter always told with the most pride. These were not drunks shooting wild. They were trained men. They had been under fire in the war. They knew how to take cover, and how to keep their heads, and how to wait. One of them said later, the accounts tell, that it was like being back in the Army, except that this time they knew exactly what they were fighting for.

And the hours went by. Ten o'clock. Midnight. One o'clock. And the deputies in the jail would not come out. And the veterans could not get in.

Now the dynamite.

Sometime in the small hours of the morning, a little before dawn on the second of August, the veterans got hold of dynamite. And they threw it. First, the accounts say, at the cars parked out in front of the jail, the sheriff's cars, and they blew some of them up, and turned them over. And then they threw dynamite onto the porch of the jail itself. And the front of that jail blew in.

And the deputies inside had had enough.

They called out that they would surrender. And they came out with their hands up. And the veterans took the ballot boxes.

And here is a thing, friend, that I have always thought was remarkable. There was a crowd in that street by then, angry, a great many people who had good reason to hate those deputies. And some of the deputies, the accounts say, were beaten by the crowd when they came out. Some were hurt bad. But nobody was killed. Not that night. All that shooting, for all those hours, and the dynamite, and the accounts say not one person died. Some were wounded. A good many were scared nearly out of their minds. But the veterans, by most accounts, kept the crowd from lynching anybody. They had come for the ballot boxes, not for blood. And I will say one thing for the men inside that jail, too. Some of them, the accounts say, were young fellows themselves, hired on as special deputies for the day for a few dollars, who had no idea what they were getting into, and who spent that whole night on the floor of the jail with bullets coming through the windows, scared to death. Not all of the men on the wrong side of a thing are bad men, friend. Some of them just took the wrong job on the wrong day.

And the sheriff, Pat Mansfield, and Paul Cantrell, the boss of the whole machine, were gone. They had gotten out of town during the night, the accounts say. And they did not come back.

Now the morning.

When the sun came up over Athens on the second of August, nineteen forty-six, the veterans did the thing they had said they would do from the beginning.

They counted the votes.

Out in the open. In public. With people watching. Every ballot. The way it was supposed to be done.

And when the votes were counted, the veterans' candidates had won. Knox Henry, the wounded soldier from North Africa, was the new sheriff of McMinn County. In the boxes that the deputies had carried off, the accounts say, the veterans' side had won by big margins, the kind of margins that tell you why the machine had wanted those boxes behind a locked door.

And the machine was finished. Not just in McMinn County, friend. The boys of Athens had shown the whole state that the machine could be beaten. And two years later, in nineteen forty-eight, a young congressman named Estes Kefauver ran for the United States Senate against Ed Crump's man. And Crump put out ads calling Kefauver a pet coon, meaning a sneaky creature doing somebody else's bidding. And Kefauver went out on the campaign trail wearing a coonskin cap, like Davy Crockett, and told the crowds, I may be a pet coon, but I'm not Mr. Crump's pet coon. And he won. And Ed Crump's hold on Tennessee was broken. I have always believed that started in Athens.

Now here is where the story turns, friend. Because a lot of folks who tell the Battle of Athens stop right there. The good guys won. The end. And it was not the end.

The Battle of Athens was in newspapers all over the country. Some of them cheered. Some of them were horrified. Armed men shooting up a county jail and dynamiting it, in the United States, over an election. What would be next? And what was to stop any group of men with guns, who did not like how an election was going, from doing the same?

And one of the people who wrote about it was Eleanor Roosevelt. She wrote a column for the newspapers in those years, and she wrote about Athens. And what she said, as near as it has come down to us, was a warning. But not to the veterans. To the politicians. She said, in effect, that when a government stops letting the people choose, when the people cannot get a fair election, then you should not be surprised when the people find another way. She said that any government, anywhere, local or state or national, that does not truly express the will of the people, had better watch out, because its days are numbered.

And here is the other part of what came after, and I want to be fair. The veterans' government, once it got in, the accounts say, did clean things up. They did away with the worst of the fee system. They paid officials salaries. But it did not stay perfect, friend. Nothing does. Within a few years, some of the same kinds of complaints people had made about the old machine were being made about the new folks too. Power is power, and a county courthouse is a county courthouse, and new men get comfortable in old chairs. I do not say that to take away from what they did. I say it because it is true, and because I promised you I would tell it all.

Now I want to give you the other side, as I always try to.

There are folks who will tell you that what happened in Athens was a riot. An armed uprising against lawful officers, however crooked. That the veterans could have gone to the courts, or to the governor, or to the federal government, and that taking guns from a National Guard armory and dynamiting a jail is not how a free country settles an election. That once you decide the law can be pushed aside when it is wrong, you have no way of stopping the next fellow who decides the law is wrong about something else.

And I take that seriously, friend. I do. I have seen what happens in this world when men decide their rifles are smarter than the vote. That is what those boys had just been fighting against, all over Europe.

But I will tell you what the old soldiers at my counter always said back, and I never heard anybody answer it. They said, they did try the courts. They did try the governor. For years, folks in McMinn County complained, and nothing happened, because the machine was hooked in with the men who ran the whole state. And on that day in August, they did everything the legal way. They registered. They ran candidates. They sent poll watchers. They went and voted. And the machine shot an old man for trying to vote, and beat up their poll watchers, and carried off the ballot boxes to count them in secret.

And then they went and got the boxes back. And the very first thing they did with them was count the votes, out in the open, honestly. They did not take over the county and appoint themselves. They did not throw out the election. They held it. They just made sure it was counted.

One old soldier said to me, Otis, we did not overthrow the government. We were the government. They were the ones who overthrew it, every two years, and we finally came home and put it back.

That old soldier had been in the Pacific, friend, in the Marines. He had been on one of those islands where the fighting was so bad that he would not tell me its name, only that he had gone ashore with a great many friends and come back with a few. And he said to me once, standing at my counter, I watched boys die on that beach for the right to vote. Then I came home and some fat sheriff in a county courthouse thought he could take mine. He shook his head. He said, those boys in Athens did right. I'd have been there with them. I'd have brought the dynamite.

And then he paid for his things and said, but I'd have counted the votes first thing, same as they did.

Now I promised you the old stories, and there are a good many.

Folks said that some of the veterans, during the siege, ran it just like a military operation, with squads and orders and men posted on the roofs. That one I believe. They had just had four years of practice.

Folks said that when the dynamite went off on the jail porch, you could hear it all the way out in the county, and folks woke up thinking the war had come to Tennessee. That one I believe too.

Folks said that years later, they made a television picture about it, in the early nineteen nineties, and that some of the old veterans who had been there watched it and laughed, and said it was a good deal neater than the real thing. I expect that is always so.

And folks said, and I heard it from more than one veteran, that the boys of Athens did what a great many soldiers coming home all over this country wished they could do. Not with dynamite. But the feeling. That they had gone off and fought for something, and they came home and expected to have it. And when they did not, they went and got it.

So I promised you I would tell you whether they were right.

Here is where I come down, friend.

I do not like guns being taken to elections. I do not like dynamite thrown at jails. I have seen enough of what men do when they decide they are the law to be afraid of it. If somebody tried the same thing today, over an election they did not like, I would want them stopped.

But here is the difference, the way I see it, and it is the only thing that matters.

The veterans of Athens did not go to that jail to change the count. They went to make sure there was a count. They did not want to win by force. They wanted to win by votes, and the other side had taken the votes away. And the minute they got those ballot boxes back, the very first thing they did, before they did anything else, before they went home, before they slept, was sit down in public and count every vote, and accept whatever it said.

That is the whole difference, friend, between a mob and a free people. A mob takes power. A free people takes back the ballot box and counts it.

And I will tell you one more thing. There was an old Black farmer in that story named Tom Gillespie, who went to vote that morning and was shot for it. He has always been the man I think about most, when I think about Athens. The veterans got the headlines. But he was the first one in McMinn County that day who stood up and said, I have always voted here before. And he took a bullet for it. And by the next morning, because of a lot of young men with rifles, his vote got counted too.

That's as far as the story goes, friend, and about as far as I'll take you tonight.
